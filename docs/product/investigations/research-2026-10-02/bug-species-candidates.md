# Real bug species for each family: candidates and suggested lineups

Research for Bug Farmer (2026-10-02). The owner marked the 116-bug list (D79) and asked for fresh suggestions,
starting from the bugs kept: recognizable bugs or variants of them (one to three kinds per family), now and then a
unique bug, every bug part of an ecosystem. This page lists real species for each family, with what makes each one
good to play against, raise or look at, and suggests a lineup per family.

Research only: nothing here is decided. The lineups are suggestions for the owner's marks. A one-line-per-family
summary of the suggested lineups is the last section; the owner's three questions are answered just before the
source table.

Status: COMPLETE (2026-10-02). Seventeen families, the owner's three questions, a summary of the suggested
lineups and the source table.

## How to read this page

- **Each family** has a table of real candidates, then a suggested lineup with reasons. Where a family needs one, the
  lineup is a ladder from easy (home ring) to dangerous (far and edge rings), matching the game's rule that bugs get
  more dangerous farther out, not bigger.
- **Columns:** *Known?* is how likely an ordinary player is to recognize the bug (high, medium, low). *Look* is size
  and silhouette for the pixel art. *Play* is the real behaviour that makes good gameplay. *Danger* is the real danger
  to people (the game is free to raise it). *Zone* is where it fits among the game's zones. *Food web* is what it eats
  and what eats it, tied to bugs already kept where possible. *Examine* gives real facts for the examine text.
- **Every fact carries a source number** like [S3], listed in the source table near the end and under each family.
  A fact without a number is my own reasoning and is marked as such.
- **Real facts are flavour, not rules.** Where the real bug is gentler than the game needs, the page says so plainly
  and suggests what the game can add without lying in its examine text.
- **Zones used here (from the brief, 2026-10-02):**
  - Home ring: Village, Bee Meadow, Ant Tunnels, Mining Camp, Ant Colony.
  - Middle ring: Wasp Thicket (half woods, a ranger station), Butterfly Fields, Hilltop Meadow, Centipede Cavern,
    Underground River.
  - Far ring: Scorpion Rocks (dry and rocky rather than sandy), Shallow Swamp, Millipede Forest, Locust Farmland (a
    western-style town), Deadly Ants outpost, the deep river.
  - Edge ring: Deep Swamp, Spider Vale West, Spider Vale East, Deadly Ants core.
- **Already settled (D79, 2026-10-02), which this page builds on and does not reopen:** kept bugs include the common
  fly, a meadow butterfly on milkweed, a village wasp, the giant hornet, the honeybee, black garden ants and fire ants
  (workers, warriors and a queen each), garden, tiger and giant centipedes, a millipede, the carrion beetle, a blue
  dragonfly, the firefly, the locust, the mosquito, the glowworm, a cave beetle, a small cave spider, a cave fly, the
  killer bee, the black widow, a scorpion, the rhinoceros beetle, the horse fly and woodland butterflies such as the
  purple emperor. Cut: aphids, ladybirds, cockroaches, leeches, snails, extra ant species and invented creatures; the
  old zone-plan picks the owner turned down (among them the desert hairy scorpion, the giant water bug, the damselfly,
  the camel and mole crickets, the diving beetle, the whirligig, the orb weaver, the trapdoor spider, the mud dauber,
  the atlas moth, the cicada and the pill bugs); and picture-only extras such as the emperor scorpion and the
  funnel-web spider, cut unless a zone's design asks for them. The dragonfly nymph and the grasshopper are not separate
  bugs: they are the dragonfly's young and the locust before it swarms. A cut bug appears below only where the brief
  asks about it, and is marked as cut.

## How the research was done

- **138 pages were fetched whole and read for the facts needed** (not search snippets): Wikipedia species, genus and
  family articles; a university extension fact sheet (Penn State, for the one claim Wikipedia lacked: that European
  hornets fly at night); and a research paper (PLoS ONE, 2014, for mosquitoes feeding on caterpillars). 130 of them
  are cited; the other eight were read and found thin or off-topic (they are listed under the source table). Every
  fact with a source number was checked against its page; numbers were taken as written there.
- **No web searches were possible.** The session's web-search allowance was already used up when this task began
  (the search tool refused the first query). In place of search-engine angles, five other routes were used:
  (1) a species page for every candidate; (2) family and genus overview pages (centipede, millipede, scorpion,
  dragonfly, mosquito, mantis, locust, social insects) to check claims across species; (3) an extension fact sheet
  where Wikipedia was silent; (4) a primary research paper for the one surprising claim the whole marsh answer rests
  on; (5) following leads named inside pages already read (the ant-decapitating fly from the scuttle-fly page, the
  "vampire spider" from the blood-feeding page, the millipede-eating beetle from the yellow-spotted millipede page).
- **Limits to keep in mind.** Most sources are Wikipedia. Before a fact goes into the game's examine text, the
  numbers (deaths, records, sizes) deserve a check against the original references those pages cite. One claim is
  marked unverified (a mosquito that feeds on mudskippers). A few pages were thin (southern cave crayfish, giant
  petaltail), and the page says so where it relies on them.

The brief asks for the house fly (the common fly of the fly farm), the horse fly, and a real cave fly that is faster
and stronger than surface flies.

**Suggested lineup (three flies, no ladder needed: flies are prey and pests, not a danger family):**

1. **Common fly = the house fly** (kept). The fly farm is true to life: house fly maggots really are raised as feed
   for fish and livestock [S1].
2. **Horse fly** (kept), the biting fly of sunny wet places: Shallow Swamp edges, the farmland. Its real habits give
   play for free: it hunts only by day [S2], dives at dark, shiny, moving targets [S2], and its young live in the
   swamp mud [S2]. An optional touch: pale outfits draw fewer horse flies (true to life as flavour; not a rule that
   blocks anything).
3. **Cave fly = a scuttle fly.** The real underground fly that best fits the faster fly the owner asked for: it
   runs rather than flies [S3], so a net swing aimed at the air misses it, and it lives in the nests of social
   insects [S3], which suits the Ant Colony granaries where the plans put it. "Stronger" is the game's own licence.
   For the species name,
   *Conicera tibialis* (the coffin fly) is the real one that lives underground for generations [S5]; if "coffin fly"
   is too grim for the tone, "scuttle fly" is the plain family name and is accurate.
4. **Optional unique bug: the ant-decapitating fly** in the Deadly Ants zones, as the fire ants' real enemy [S4]. It
   gives the fire-ant zones a living counter the player could raise and release, exactly as the United States has
   done [S4]. This is a sandbox system, not a random event.

The black soldier fly is a possible later farm fly (it bites nobody, isn't a pest, and its larvae turn waste into
feed [S6]), but the house fly already carries the farm truthfully, so it is not needed.

**Do wasps or hornets have soldiers? No.** No wasp or hornet has a soldier caste. Their colonies hold a queen, workers
and males [S14, S15]. In paper wasps the founding queen and her helpers barely differ in body [S16, S17]; in
yellowjackets and hornets the queen is simply bigger (about 19 vs 12 mm in yellowjackets [S14]; 35 vs 25 mm in the
European hornet [S11]). Every female worker can sting and defend the nest. Soldier castes with special bodies are
known in ants and termites [S20, S21], in some gall aphids and thrips, and in snapping shrimp [S21]; among bees only
one stingless bee, *Tetragonisca angustula*, was found (in 2012) to have soldiers: bigger guards, 1–6% of the
colony, standing or hovering at the entrance [S20]. Among honeybees and many social insects, older workers do the
risky outside work and younger ones work inside [S22, S21].

What this means for the game's "wasp soldier" (my suggestion): keep the behaviour, drop the separate body. Workers
near the nest act as guards (stay close, attack anyone who comes too near), while foragers range out. The examine
text can say plainly that every wasp worker is a fighter and there is no soldier kind. If a tougher nest defender is
wanted, the queen is the true big one.

**Suggested lineup (a ladder: paper wasp → common wasp → European hornet → giant hornet):**

1. **Village wasp = the paper wasp** (easy). Its open comb under the eaves is readable at a glance [S16], and it
   hunts caterpillars [S16], which ties it to the butterflies and the player's caterpillars.
2. **Second wasp = the common wasp, or yellowjacket** (medium), in the Wasp Thicket. Hidden ground and hollow-tree
   nests [S15], thousands of workers [S14], repeated stings [S14], and it chases attackers [S14]: the step up the
   owner wants from the village wasp.
3. **Hornet = the European hornet** (medium to hard), in the Wasp Thicket woods. It is the real night-flier [S12],
   so the night hunting the game gave the giant hornet belongs here and is true to life: lamps at the ranger station
   would draw it [S12]. It hunts the other wasps, dragonflies and mantises [S11], a predator over predators.
4. **Giant hornet = the northern giant hornet** (dangerous). Real giant hornets fly by day (as the bug list already
   notes), so it should be the daytime hive raider: group raids on honeybee hives [S13] give the player's bee farm a
   real enemy, and its underground nests among old pine roots [S13] give the zone a findable nest. If the owner wants
   to keep a giant hornet that hunts at night, the real one is Bingham's hornet [S18], but players won't know it.

**Optional unique bug: the tarantula hawk** in Spider Vale East. Not a nest wasp, so it doesn't count toward the two
wasps, but it ties the vale's tarantulas to a real hunter [S23] and drinks from milkweed, which the game already grows
[S23]. Its sting is famous for pain, not harm [S23]: a short stun in the game would be true to life.

The bald-faced hornet is left out: it is really a yellowjacket [S14], so naming it "hornet" would teach the wrong
thing, and it would crowd the European hornet.

## 3. Bees

The owner wants three bees, the killer bee among them. The honeybee and the killer bee are kept; the third is open
(bumblebee and carpenter bee are the listed candidates).

**Suggested lineup (a ladder: bumblebee → honeybee → killer bee):**

1. **Bumblebee** (gentle, home ring). The most recognizable bee after the honeybee, slow to anger [S26], and it adds a
   real farm role the honeybee doesn't: buzz pollination of tomatoes, for which bumblebee colonies are really bred and
   sold [S26]. A boxed bumblebee colony in a greenhouse is a true-to-life second bee to raise. Real nests are often
   in old rodent burrows [S26]; with no mammals left, the game can put them in grass tussocks and tree holes, which
   are also real nest sites [S26].
2. **Honeybee** (kept; calm unless provoked), the main farmed bee: honey, wax, swarming [S24].
3. **Killer bee** (kept; dangerous), in the hot, dry far ring. Its real danger is numbers and persistence, not
   stronger venom [S25]: a hive that chases 400 m and stings ten times as often [S25] fits the owner's picture of a
   bee that may be deadlier than some wasps. One honest problem: real killer bees look identical to honeybees [S25]. The
   game will need a small visual tell (for example darker banding) for readability; the examine text can say the
   real ones can't be told apart by eye, which is itself a good fact.

**Alternative third bee: the carpenter bee**, if the owner would rather have a village pest than a second meadow bee.
It bores round holes in dead wood [S27], so it could pester wooden buildings and fences, and its males buzz at you
but can't sting [S27]. It would make the village livelier but adds no farm role.

The giant honey bee is noted only as a possible later far-ring honey site (its rippling defence [S28] is a great
sight); it is not needed for the three.

Apis dorsata (all Wikipedia).

**Do black garden ants have soldiers? No.** The black garden ant is one size throughout, with "no major caste" [S29];

ants' warriors are real, the black ants' warriors are a game role.

**Suggested lineup (a ladder: black ants at home → fire ants far out):**

1. **Black ants: worker, scout, "warrior", queen** (home ring). Make the black ants' warriors a **role, not a new
   body**: the workers that stay by the queen and the nest mouths and fight, using the real weapons every worker has
   (bites and a spray of formic acid [S31]). If they must look bigger for readability, the fact that old colonies
   raise larger workers [S29] is a fair, truthful hook for the examine text, for example: "Black garden ants have no
   soldiers. Every worker bites and sprays acid, and old colonies raise bigger workers."

3. **The fire ants' living enemy**, if wanted: the ant-decapitating fly (section 1) [S4], their real natural enemy

black ants another sweet source (nectar and fruit are real foods of theirs [S29]) without saying anything false.

## 6. Millipedes

The owner wants three millipedes: one more dangerous, perhaps poisonous, with fast, dangerous ones among them. The
kept millipede is the slow one that eats rotting plants (village and Millipede Forest). One honest point first:
**real millipedes are slow** ("slow-moving", built for burrowing and crevices [S45]) and **none hunts**: they eat
rotting plants and similar matter [S45]. Their danger is chemical. Many give off foul defensive fluids from pores
along the body, containing quinones, cyanide and other compounds; tropical kinds can blister skin, and in the eyes
the fluid causes "millipede burn" [S45].

## 12. Swamp and marsh bugs (mosquitoes and their neighbours)

The mosquito is kept as a later danger that lunges and swarms, held back by sand laid on the marsh and other means,
and the marsh needs something for mosquitoes to feed on (D79). The horse fly (section 1) also belongs here. The water
strider is a candidate; the giant water bug was cut. The owner's food question is answered in full in the questions
section; the short version is in the lineup below.

Facts for all mosquitoes: **both sexes drink nectar and plant juices; only females take blood, for the protein to
make eggs** [S97]. Different kinds favour amphibians, reptiles, birds or mammals [S97], and malaria mosquitoes also
seek out **caterpillars and drink their body fluid** [S97, S98]. Three groups never take blood at all [S97]. They find
hosts by smell (carbon dioxide and other scents) and by sight [S97]; egg to adult can take as little as five days in
heat [S97]; larvae breathe at the surface through a tube [S97]. Their enemies: dragonfly nymphs, whirligig beetles,
water striders and fish such as the mosquitofish eat the larvae; dragonflies eat the adults [S97].

## 17. Locusts (and their two phases)

Kept: the locust of the Locust Farmland, which feeds on the wheat and other plants; there is no locust boss and a
swarm is not a boss either (D42, D62, D63). The grasshopper is not a separate bug: the bug list already notes that
the lone grasshoppers can be the locust before it swarms (D79), which is exactly the real phase change below. The
brief asks about phases.

**What a locust is.** There is no separate "locust" kind of animal: a locust is a grasshopper species that can switch
into a swarming form when crowded, and the switch is called a phase change [S129]. In the **solitary phase** the
insects live scattered and do little harm [S129]; desert locusts are then green or pale beige [S130]. In the
**gregarious (swarming) phase** they turn darker with strong yellow-and-black markings and change shape, metabolism
and breeding speed [S129]; desert locusts turn from pink when young to bright yellow when mature [S130]. **What flips
the switch is touch:** repeated bumping of the hind legs in a crowd raises serotonin, and several touches a minute
over about four hours are enough [S129, S130]. Crowded young (wingless "hoppers") march together in bands; winged
adults form swarms [S129].

## The owner's three questions

### 1. Do wasps or hornets have soldiers?

**No.** No wasp or hornet has a soldier caste. Their colonies are a queen, workers and males [S14, S15]; in paper wasps
the founding queen and her helpers barely differ in body [S16, S17], and in yellowjackets and hornets the queen is
just bigger than the workers [S11, S14]. Every female worker can sting and defend the nest. Real soldier castes,
with bodies built for fighting, are known in ants and termites [S20, S21], in some gall-making aphids and thrips and
in snapping shrimp [S21], and in one bee: the stingless *Tetragonisca angustula*, whose larger guards (1–6% of the
colony) were discovered in 2012 [S20]. Among honeybees, and in many social insects, older workers take the risky
outside work, foraging and defence [S22, S21].

The fire ants, by contrast, do have soldiers: big-headed major workers [S30]. Black garden ants do not: all their
workers are one size, with "no major caste" [S29].

**What this means for the game (my suggestion):** keep "guard" as a behaviour, not a separate wasp. Workers near the
nest stay close and attack intruders while foragers range out. The game's existing "wasp soldier" can become wasp
guards that look like the workers (with a guarding pose for readability), or be dropped, with the queen as the real
big defender. The examine text can teach the fact directly: "No wasp has soldiers. Every worker is a fighter."

### 2. What lives in a marsh that mosquitoes could feed on, with no mammals, birds, amphibians or reptiles?

Real mosquitoes are less dependent on warm-blooded hosts than the question assumes. Here is what the real record
supports, in order of strength:

1. **Nectar and plant juice, for every mosquito.** Both males and females drink nectar, honeydew and plant juices;
   only females take blood, and only for the protein to make eggs [S97]. So marsh flowers keep adults alive.
2. **Some mosquitoes need no blood at all.** Three groups never take blood [S97], among them the elephant mosquito,
   whose young eat other mosquitoes' young [S102]. The house mosquito's underground form lays its first eggs without
   any blood meal [S101].
3. **The players.** People are the last mammals. Mosquitoes find hosts by the carbon dioxide we breathe out, by
   scent and by sight [S97], so a player in the marsh is exactly what they hunt for.
4. **The body fluid of big insects.** This is the real answer for a giant-bug world. Malaria mosquitoes seek out
   caterpillars and drink their body fluid [S97]; in a 2014 study, 82–87% of malaria mosquitoes were drawn to
   caterpillars of two moth species, and some fed until full [S98]. Old field records add mosquitoes attacking small
   flies, cicadas and caterpillars [S98]. Their cousins, the biting midges, include kinds that live as parasites on
   larger insects and drink their fluid, even from mantises [S106, S107]. The study showed the mosquitoes lived
   longer on caterpillar fluid but did not show them laying eggs from it [S98], so the game making giant bugs a full
   "blood" source is a small, honest stretch of a real behaviour.
5. **Fish (unverified).** Fish survive in the game. I recall a 2004 report of a crab-hole mosquito feeding on
   mudskippers in Japan's Ryukyu Islands, but no page reached in this session confirmed it, so treat it as unchecked.
   The mosquito overview lists amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals as hosts, not fish [S97].

**A marsh food web this gives the game:**

- **Mosquito larvae** need standing water: they breathe at the surface [S97]; house mosquitoes lay egg rafts on
  stagnant water [S101]; tiger mosquitoes breed in as little as 30 ml [S99]. So **sand laid on the marsh, removing
  standing water, is a true-to-life way to hold mosquitoes back**, as the owner wanted.
- **Adults** feed on marsh flowers, on caterpillars and other soft bugs (including the player's), and on players.
- **Their enemies** (all real): dragonfly nymphs, whirligig beetles, water striders and fish eat the larvae [S97];
  dragonflies eat the adults [S97, S63]; the diving bell spider eats mosquito larvae [S96]; the "vampire spider", a
  jumping spider, picks out blood-fed mosquitoes by smell and sight [S108]; elephant mosquito larvae eat other
  mosquito larvae [S102]; some biting midge larvae do too [S106].
- **Other marsh links:** non-biting midge larvae (bloodworms) feed the fish [S105]; horse fly larvae hunt in the mud
  [S2]; the red swamp crayfish burrows in the banks [S109]; the fishing spider eats small fish [S95].

So the marsh's mosquito numbers can rise with standing water and flowering plants, and fall with sand, dragonflies,
water striders and the player's own released predators. Nothing in it needs a random event.

### 3. Is a "raid centipede" a real species?

**No.** No real centipede goes by that name: it appears in none of the thirteen centipede pages read for this report,
and the general centipede page, checked for it specifically, mentions no raiding centipede [S41]. The name began as a
role in the bug list (a centipede that dens by the ants and raids their colony), and D79 already records that it
isn't a species. The question is which real centipede should live by the Ant Colony.

- **My pick: the tiger centipede** (*Scolopendra polymorpha*), already in the game: a night hunter that shelters
  under rocks and in burrows [S36], a step up from the garden centipede, and centipedes are general hunters that will
  take ants (my reasoning; the house centipede's recorded diet includes ants [S37]).
- **If the bug list's picture of a green centipede matters:** real centipedes with green tones include a form of the
  giant redheaded centipede, red-headed with a greenish-black body [S35], and the waterfall centipede, greenish black
  [S39]. Either would be a real, green-tinged choice, though the first is a dry-country centipede and the second a
  swimmer.
- **If a smaller, faster home-ring bug is wanted:** the house centipede, a real ant-eater, very fast and nearly
  harmless [S37].

## Summary of the suggested lineups (one line per family)

1. **Flies:** common fly = the house fly (the farm fly, true to life); the horse fly in sunny wet places; cave fly =
   a scuttle fly, which runs instead of flying and lives in ant nests; optional: the ant-decapitating fly against the
   fire ants.
2. **Wasps and hornets:** paper wasp (village) → common wasp / yellowjacket (Wasp Thicket) → European hornet (the real
   night-flier, Thicket woods) → northern giant hornet (the daytime hive raider). No wasp has soldiers: make guards a
   behaviour. Optional: the tarantula hawk in Spider Vale East.
3. **Bees:** bumblebee (gentle, a real greenhouse pollinator) → honeybee (the farm bee) → killer bee (hot, dry far
   ring; dangerous by numbers and persistence). Alternative third: the carpenter bee as a village pest.
4. **Ants:** black garden ants (worker, scout, queen, and a "warrior" that is a guard role: they have no soldiers) →
   fire ants (worker, the real big-headed majors as warriors, queen; living rafts when it floods).
5. **Centipedes:** garden = the stone centipede (harmless, fast) → tiger = the banded desert centipede (venom runs
   out; the centipede that lives by the ants) → the Vietnamese centipede (the most aggressive, a recorded death; the
   forest centipede). Optional: the waterfall centipede, which swims. Alternative third: the Amazonian giant as a
   cave-ceiling hunter.
6. **Millipedes:** American giant millipede (skin-burning ooze) → giant African millipede (eye-harming spray,
   armour) → shocking pink dragon millipede (cyanide, more of them after rain). No real millipede is fast. Optional:
   the glowing *Motyxia* at night.
7. **Scorpions:** Asian forest scorpion in the Wasp Thicket (big claws, mild venom) → fat-tailed scorpion at
   Scorpion Rocks (thin claws, deadly venom): together they teach "big claws, weak sting". Optional: the Arizona bark
   scorpion; a blacklight lantern to find them.
8. **Dragonflies:** blue dasher (beginner, the pond west of the village) → emperor dragonfly (Butterfly Fields) →
   dragonhunter (hunts other dragonflies; Deep Swamp). None is dangerous to people in real life.
9. **Butterflies and moths:** meadow butterfly (its real model is the monarch; fold the monarch candidate into it)
   and the purple emperor; the luna moth and the death's-head hawkmoth (hive raider, potato pest); the domestic silk
   moth as farm livestock, with the Chinese oak tussar as an optional outdoor silk moth.
10. **Beetles:** burying beetle (the carrion beetle), *Leptodirus* (the cave beetle), Hercules beetle (the rhinoceros
    beetle mini-boss), stag beetle (Thicket woods), Colorado potato beetle (the crop beetle). Optional: the
    bombardier beetle.
11. **Spiders:** European cave spider (the small cave spider); the harvestman as the daddy longlegs; West vale: wolf
    spider and Brazilian wandering spider; East vale: black widow, Goliath birdeater, giant huntsman. Optional: the
    fishing and diving bell spiders in the swamp.
12. **Swamp and marsh:** house mosquito (marsh) and Asian tiger mosquito (swamp), malaria mosquito optional; the
    elephant mosquito as a natural check the player can release; the water strider; the horse fly. Mosquitoes feed
    on nectar, players and the body fluid of big insects such as caterpillars; sand on the marsh removes their
    breeding water.
13. **Crayfish and crabs:** red swamp crayfish (the farmed one) → river crab (Underground River) → Tasmanian giant
    freshwater crayfish (the deep river).
14. **Mantises:** a praying mantis (the Chinese mantis, whose egg cases are sold as pest control, set against the
    locusts); optional: the orchid mantis in the Butterfly Fields.
15. **Crickets:** house cricket (the farm cricket) and field cricket (the meadows); optional: the snowy tree cricket,
    whose chirps tell the temperature.
16. **Fireflies and glowworms:** one lightning-bug firefly (with the "femme fatale" firefly as a fact or later
    kind); the cave glowworm = the New Zealand glowworm, a fly larva that fishes with sticky threads; optional: the
    railroad worm, a glowing millipede hunter.
17. **Locust:** the desert locust in two phases, green and solitary or yellow-and-black and swarming; swarms follow
    crowding after rain, a cause rather than an event; the extinct Rocky Mountain locust as the western town's
    history.
