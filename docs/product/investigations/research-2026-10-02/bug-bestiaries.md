# How the best bug games pick and tier their creatures (bestiary research)

Research for Bug Farmer (2026-10-02). After reviewing the bug roster on 2026-10-02, the owner set its direction: bugs that
players recognize; small families of one to three kinds of a recognizable bug; an occasional one-of-a-kind bug with no
variants; every bug placed in an ecosystem rather than dropped in by random events; nothing obscure or confusing. This page
studies how bug games and famous creature-roster games choose and structure their creatures, and what makes players love or
dislike a roster.

Research only: nothing here is decided.

Status: COMPLETE (2026-10-02). A 10-line summary closes the page.

Method: four angles. (1) *By game*: each game's creature list on its wiki (read from the page or from the wiki's own data
feed), store pages and Wikipedia. (2) *By technique*: how variants are built — Monster Hunter's subspecies and variant history,
Pikmin's creature families, Bug Fables' regional adaptations, Pokémon's regional forms. (3) *By player praise and complaint*:
about 15,000 of the most-helpful English Steam reviews across 11 games (Grounded, Grounded 2, Smalland, Empire of the Ants,
Empires of the Undergrowth, Bug Fables, Webbed, Hollow Knight, Monster Hunter World, Rise and Wilds), counted with whole-word
matches and read in context, plus Steam forum threads. (4) *By developer interview*: Obsidian (Adam Brennecke, Kaz Aruga,
Chris Parker), Nintendo (Shigeru Miyamoto and the Pikmin 4 team), Game Freak (Shigeru Ohmori), and Capcom's design books as
cited on the Monster Hunter wiki. Every row in Part 1 was fetched and read in full, not taken from a search snippet (row 40's
fact checks read the opening section of each article).

Two limits: this session's web-search allowance was already used up, so sources were reached by direct addresses, each
site's own search and the reference lists of pages already read, not by a search engine; and Reddit, TV Tropes, Bulbapedia
and Fandom's web pages refused automated reading (Fandom wikis were read through their data feed instead). Review counts
cover the most-helpful reviews only, so they show what players stress, not exact shares. Quotes from players are reproduced
with their spelling.

## Part 2 — The games, one by one

### Grounded (Obsidian, 2020 early access, 1.0 in 2022) — the closest reference

- **Roster size.** The long-running Grounded Wiki's creature table (source 37) lists 8 harmless, 8 neutral and 40 angry
  creatures, 7 bosses and 2 New Game+ ants (the wiki.gg copy, source 5, also lists babies and queens separately, about 90
  entries in all). Under those entries sit about 30 real families. Almost all are real backyard bugs named by plain common
  names (aphid, weevil, ladybug, stinkbug, bombardier beetle, wolf spider, black widow, antlion, termite, tick). The invented
  ones are few and fenced off as story: three lab robots, six "infected" forms from a weed-killer leak, and a mutant "Mant"
  boss. Non-bugs are rare set dressing (a crow, koi, tadpoles).
- **Each bug drops parts named for its famous trait** (source 37): the bombardier beetle a *Boiling Gland*, the firefly *Glow
  Goo*, the stinkbug a *Gas Sack*, the mosquito a *Beak* and *Blood Sack*, the black ox beetle a *Horn*, the wolf spider
  *Fang* and *Venom*. The material tells you what the bug is famous for.
- **How kinds are made.** Four patterns, all from real biology:
  1. *Species within a family:* three ants (red, black, fire), two-plus spiders by time of day (orb weaver by day, wolf spider at
     night — art director Kaz Aruga, Game Informer 2020-04-28), black widow later.
  2. *Castes:* every ant species has baby, worker, soldier and queen; termites have worker, soldier and king; wasps have wasp,
     drone and a queen boss.
  3. *Life stages:* orb weaver Jr., spiderlings, black widowlings, ladybird larva.
  4. *Look-alike pairs, the second kind living in the harder upper yard* (source 37): mosquito (lower yard) → tiger mosquito
     (Moldorc Highlands, Upper Grasslands); water flea (pond) → spiny water flea (upper yard); stinkbug (grasslands) → green
     shield bug (upper yard); ladybug (neutral, grasslands) → "ladybird" (angry, upper yard); wolf spider → black widow
     (upper yard). Most second kinds are real, distinct species — a sound choice — yet late in the game players called them
     "reskins" (source 17) because they fought much like the first. One was not a distinct species at all: *ladybird* is
     simply the British word for ladybug (source 40), which a knowledgeable reviewer caught (source 14), and it dropped ladybug
     parts.
- **Danger by area.** The start (grasslands) holds aphids, weevils, gnats, curious worker ants and ladybugs; spiders own the
  hedge; the upper yard brings black ox beetles, wasps and tiger mosquitoes; the pond, sandbox, haze and termite mound each add
  their own hazards. Story steps (giving the robot a chip) make some areas harsher (source 15). Temper, not size, carries the
  difficulty: the wiki sorts every bug as harmless, neutral (fights back) or angry (attacks on sight).
- **What players love.** Spiders, overwhelmingly, as the thing they remember (475 of 2,500 helpful reviews mention them; "legit
  scary even to a non-arachnophobe"); the variety of tempers ("Some are annoying, some are just cute, some are a serious
  challenge… and some are the stuff of nightmares" — a 163-upvote review); the mantis boss ("beautiful and has a nice fighting
  pattern"); cute harmless bugs and pets ("quantum weevils are cute", "having an aphid pet, killing whoever threatens the
  aphids"); the arachnophobia setting.
- **What players complain about.** "More spiders" as the content for new areas; bugs respawning daily long after they stop
  being dangerous; mosquitoes that reach you everywhere; larvae spawning inside bases; a generic "Larva" species; two names for
  one insect; a variant dropping its parent's parts (source 14, 15). One reviewer asked for the arachnophobia treatment to be
  extended to bees and wasps.
- **Ecology shown.** Through wants you can watch: ants trail to big food and send soldiers if you hoard it (Brennecke, Game
  Informer); aphids seek clovers and leave honeydew; weevils gather at mushrooms; gnats keep to water by day and light by night
  (Xbox Wire 2020-09-24). Predation between wild bugs exists but is incidental (a review recalls ladybird larvae and a roly poly
  fighting to the death). Attacks on the player's base are tied to the player's own deeds, species by species: "Bugs will take
  the fight back to your base when they get tired of you intruding on their territory or killing their kind. The more you annoy
  a particular species, the more likely they will come knocking at your door" (Xbox Wire 2022-05-10, source 36). Neutral bugs
  "swarm" — hit one and its nearby kin join in — and some turn angry only in their home ground ("Bees at the Picnic Table";
  source 37).
- **Naming.** Plain common names, mostly regional US usage ("roly poly" not pill bug), which drew a few corrections from
  knowledgeable players. The creature bosses carry titles (Hedge Broodmother, Wasp Queen, Orchid Mantis) but are real species
  underneath; the story bosses are robots and one mutant.
- **How Obsidian chose.** Research per bug from Wikipedia and enthusiasts' films; "we try to keep, at a base level, how insects
  behave" without going "in the weeds" (Brennecke, GamesRadar 2019); in the interviewer's words every insect has "distinctive
  functions for your survival"; some creatures and features were added because the community asked (flying bugs, mounts, a
  lizard — Xbox Wire 2021, 2025, 2026).

### Grounded 2 (Obsidian and Eidos Montréal, early access 2025)

- **Roster size.** 9 harmless, 11 neutral and about 43 angry entries (the angry list includes three robots and four named
  koi), plus bosses (Grounded Wiki, *Creatures (Grounded 2)*). Returning bugs were "refreshed and reworked" (Xbox Wire 2025-06-08).
- **How kinds are made.** Life stages lead: *scorpling → scorpion Jr. → scorpion* for two scorpion species (northern and
  striped bark), *toe biter nymph → toe biter → Leviathan*, *cockroach nymph → cockroach → cockroach queen*, *praying mantis
  nymph*. New kinds are introduced by a new *way of fighting* — the cockroach "having the ability to block your attacks" — and
  two earwigs (pincher and whipper) with different moves that hunt in mixed groups.
- **Bosses.** A tarantula (AXL) whose defeat unlocks a full set of tarantula gear and furniture (Xbox Wire 2025-09-30), and a
  horned lizard (King Dozer) designed by asking "what would a lizard do?" — so it seeks heat and squirts from its eyes, a real
  horned-lizard defence, softened (game director Chris Parker, Xbox Wire 2026-04-14).
- **Bugs as livestock.** The headline feature, "Buggies": steal an egg, hatch it in a Hatchery, raise and ride a red or black
  soldier ant, an orb weaver, a ladybug or a toe biter. Each family has its own job (ants haul, chop and recruit workers; the
  spider is fast and hits hardest; the ladybug heals and shoots water). Riding a soldier-ant buggy makes wild soldiers of that
  species neutral. Buggies level up so early ones don't go stale: "nobody would… want to use a Red Ant Soldier beyond early
  areas", and "all Buggies in a given family [should] have similar tier abilities" (Parker). It was "one of the most requested
  community features" (Xbox Wire 2025-06-08).
- **Ecology and danger by area.** The wiki lists every bug's named areas: common harmless bugs everywhere; the dangerous kinds
  (scorpions, toe biters, ticks, shield bugs) in a few places.
- **What players say** (1,499 most-helpful English Steam reviews, source 17). Buggies are, with spiders, the most-discussed
  creature topic (the word "buggies" appears in 87 reviews, "spider" in 96): riding "makes travelling actually *feel* fast… you actually feel like you're riding an ant", but
  getting them felt thin — "The 'taming' process lacks a sense of progression… Ant bait and Spider bait… a saddle, friendship
  levels" would have given depth (155 upvotes). The same reviewer misses Grounded 1's *territories*: enemies "seem to be
  scattered randomly, lacking the defined territories that made encounters in the first game so memorable". The top review
  (499 upvotes) calls Grounded 1's late additions "a thousand reskinned enemies, with 4 of them literally just being ants
  with a new attack… The Black Widow, Tiger Mosquito, Termites, Ladybirds… stopped adding in new creatures and started adding
  in reskins". Bug-on-bug fights are a memory players share ("an active warzone with ants, spiders, scorpians and ladybugs
  fighting constantly"; "30 whole irl minutes of Grand master mantis whooping the scorpions butt"). Killing a friendly bug for
  gear can feel wrong: "the caterpillar is an awesome addition, but having to kill the caterpillar to get certain gear is so
  cruel, its so cute and friendly". New phobias surface: "a slider for arachnophobes but… [no] warning for large roaches in
  the dark".

### Smalland: Survive the Wilds (Max Ent Games, full release 15 February 2024 per its Steam page) — the cautionary twin of Grounded

- **Roster size.** About 50 entries on the wiki's list: 6 passive (carpenter ant worker, firefly, ladybug, butterfly, moth,
  spiderling), 3 neutral (damselfly, grasshopper, jumping spider), 35 aggressive and 6 bosses (source 18). Mostly real
  bugs by common name (bull ant, black widow, cockroach, lubber grasshopper, sawyer beetle, stag beetle, tarantula), with
  invented or vague ones mixed in ("Dawn Ant", "Colony Spider", "Nocturnal Spider", "Green Beetle", "Grey Fly") and many
  non-bugs (blue tit, golden eagle, gecko, lizard, toad, viper).
- **How kinds are made.** By colour and size: players count "4-5 different hornets that are just rendered different colors"
  and beetles that "all have the same attacks"; bosses are "often-unique variants of seen mobs" with extra moves (source 19).
  Some bugs simply turn aggressive at night (the wiki lists night versions of the dragonfly, grasshopper and ladybug).
- **Danger by area.** Each biome has its own list (Oak Forest, Swamp, Beach, Ruins, Greylands, Amber Valleys…), but the
  density is high everywhere: "The world is VERY overpopulated with every insect that wants to kill you" (source 19).
- **Bugs as livestock.** Trap-and-ride tames (grasshopper, damselfly, ladybug, wolf spider, hornet, gecko, blue tit,
  scorpion). Reviews call them weak, easy to lose and quickly replaced; "the time it takes to tame anything is nowhere near
  worth it".
- **Ecology shown.** The wiki says predators attack lower creatures and competitors, and players use it ("you can make them
  fight each other if you need to make a run for it"), but spawns are fixed: "the same number of the same creature in the
  same spot 100% of the time" (source 19).
- **Lesson.** Smalland has roughly Grounded's ingredients and gets roughly the opposite reception on creatures. The
  differences players name are exactly the owner's worries: colour-swapped kinds, bosses that are big versions of mobs,
  fixed spawns instead of a living population, and too many hostiles.

### Empire of the Ants (Tower Five / Microids, 2024) — photoreal ants

- **Roster.** You are a red wood ant worker of the Bel-O-Kan colony; ant legions follow a worker / warrior / spitter triangle,
  and allied local wildlife joins as "support" and "super-predator" legions unlocked by nest tier: snails, rhinoceros
  beetles, hornets and the ant-nest beetle *Paussus*; one mission lets you ride a praying mantis (Steam store page and
  reviews; sources 20–22). Enemies are termites and rival insect colonies, with spiders as stealth threats while exploring.
- **What players love.** The look ("beautiful instead of fearsome, even if you aren't fans of insects"); real details such
  as beetles living in the ant nests; riding the mantis ("the highlight of the game"); the ant–termite politics.
- **What players complain about.** Ant-savvy players object to borrowed kinds when ants have their own (beetles in place of
  major workers, hornets in place of winged ants); the species diary reads as a checklist; the counter-triangle makes fights
  predictable; only 3–4 of 9 unit types get used.
- **Naming.** Plain common names in play; a collectible encyclopedia for facts.

### Empires of the Undergrowth (Slug Disco, 2024) — the "ant simulator" that teaches

- **Roster.** 12 playable real ant and termite species and about 40 real non-player creatures (centipede, mantis, bombardier
  beetle, devil's coach horse, ladybird, earwig, woodlouse, tiger beetle, trap-jaw and army ants…), listed by scientific name
  on Wikipedia; one invented story species (source 23).
- **How kinds are made.** By *species* and *caste*, each with its real trick: fire ants bridge water, army ants sweep areas,
  slave-maker ants steal larvae, exploding ants burst.
- **Ecology shown.** Each food source brings its own threat — ladybugs eat the aphids whose honeydew the colony harvests, and
  night hunters take careless workers (source 39); a documentary narrator explains each level's animals. Players say they
  learned real biology and some took up ant keeping (source 23).
- **Lesson.** Real behaviour is the content players remember. Its obscure picks work because a narrator introduces each one
  to an audience that came for ants; a broad game should lean on recognizable bugs and tell their true story.

### Bug Fables: The Everlasting Sapling (Moonsprout Games, 2019) — a charming RPG bestiary

- **Roster size.** 100 bestiary entries (source 24): wild families, social-insect soldiers, robots, named characters and
  bosses.
- **Real vs invented.** Two worlds side by side: the social insects (ants, bees, termites, wasps) are talking kingdoms; "other
  insects… remained feral" (source 25). Feral wildlife mixes real bugs (weevil, jumping spider, diving spider, water strider,
  giant water bug as "Belostoss", mantidfly, peacock spider, dune scorpion) with invented plant creatures (Seedlings,
  Chompers).
- **How kinds are made.** Region by region, with a reason. The Seedling family changes with the land (Acornling shell
  against Golden Hills predators; Cactiling spikes and water storage in the desert; Plumpling bulk in the Forsaken Lands).
  The Wasp Kingdom's castes become military roles (scout, trooper, bomber, driller), and the Leafbug faction splits by
  fighting role (ninja, archer, clubber).
- **Ecology shown.** In the log text: predators named, food chains named ("this primal creature stands at the top of the
  Forsaken Land's food chain").
- **What players love.** The spy log, with each of the three heroes giving their own comment on every enemy; charm; the
  spider boss's music. Complaints about the roster are rare.
- **Naming.** Plain or punny common names ("Water Strider", "Jumping Spider", "Mothfly"); some Latin-flavoured
  ("Belostoss" from *Belostomatidae*).

### Hollow Knight (Team Cherry, 2017) — stylized insects, invented names

- **Roster size.** 164 journal entries (source 26), almost all invented bug-like creatures; no real species names.
- **How kinds are made.** Families that change by area (about 20 "Husk" kinds: farming husks, guards, miners, crystal- and
  fungus-grown husks); a base bug and its area variant (Vengefly → Furious Vengefly); "mother" and "king" mini-bosses that
  breed or lead the common kind (Gruz Mother spills Gruzzers; Aspid Mother spawns hatchlings); castes in the hive and mantis
  societies.
- **Danger by area.** Each area has its own palette and its own enemies; reviewers single out how enemy colours match the
  area. The spider-filled Deepnest is remembered as the fear zone ("predatory spiders & pools of parasites").
- **What players love.** Variety with distinct attack patterns ("unique enemies with different attack patterns throughout the
  entire game"); the Hunter's Journal, whose notes unlock after many kills and are written in a character's voice.
- **Lesson.** The family-and-area structure is excellent; the invented names are not a model for Bug Farmer, which promises
  real bugs. Note that even Hollow Knight's "mothers" are earned by a behaviour (they spawn the common kind).

### The Pikmin series (Nintendo, 2001–2023) — the best-structured families

- **Roster.** Invented animals on an unnamed planet, but built like real ones: each sits in a family with a mock-Latin name
  (sources 27, 28). The grub-dog family alone holds about 18 kinds across the games.
- **How kinds are made.** Every kind has a biological reason: a life stage (larva, first moult, second moult), a habitat form
  (hairy for the cold, frosty, fiery, aquatic), a temper (the easily woken Orange Bulborb), a freak individual (the polyploid
  Jumbo Bulborb that ambushes), an age (the Sovereign Bulblax, an Emperor of "advanced age"), a queen that births young (the
  Empress Bulblax). The reason changes the *behaviour*: juveniles hide at night from adults, young Bulbears follow a parent's
  patrol, the jumbo ambushes rather than sleeps. Plain colour swaps appear only in a theme-park spin-off.
- **Danger by area.** Sleepy common kinds above ground (Bulborbs "prefer to spend the daytime sleeping"); the Empress queen
  and repeated Emperor Bulblaxes in the caves of Pikmin 2 and 4; habitat forms where the habitat is (source 27).
- **Ecology shown.** Adult Bulborbs prey on their own young, so the albino young come out only at night or in caves (source
  27); Pikmin carry the dead home "like ants carrying a cicada" (source 29); the
  Piklopedia counts how many Pikmin each creature has eaten and lets you throw a carrot to watch it react (source 28).
- **Naming.** A plain, catchy common name for play (Bulborb, Bulbear, Water Dumple) and a mock-Latin name in the book.
- **Food and creatures.** Pikmin 2's book carries a second voice, Louie's, giving a recipe for every creature — a light,
  funny way to say "this animal is also food".
- **Riding.** Nintendo considered letting players "ride and control various creatures" in Pikmin 4, found it hard to make
  coherent, and built one trainable partner creature instead (source 29).

### Webbed (Sbug Games, 2021) — one famous spider and real bug societies

- **Roster.** Small and real: the player is a peacock spider; the friends are an ant colony (at war with fire ants), a bee
  hive, dung beetles and a moth; a redback spider cameos; the villain is a bowerbird (source 30).
- **How kinds are made.** By society, not by tier: each colony is a quest built on a true habit (ants returning larvae to a
  nursery; bees feeding pollen to raise a princess; dung beetles rolling dung). Two ant species appear as rivals (the
  colony and the fire ants).
- **Danger.** Low — a cosy puzzle-platformer. The one real predator of spiders, a bird, is the villain.
- **What players love.** Cuteness (half of the helpful reviews), dancing with other bugs, and the spider itself; people with
  arachnophobia praise it; one top review describes falling for real jumping spiders afterwards (source 31). Arachnophobia
  mode exists and is noticed.
- **Lesson.** Recognizable species with their famous habits (the peacock spider's dance, the dung beetle's ball, the
  bowerbird's hoard) are the hook; a small, warm roster beats a long, obscure one.

### Monster Hunter (Capcom, 2004–2025) — the variant system, for the variant idea only

- **What the series does.** Three separate ideas, often confused: a *subspecies* (a population changed by its habitat and
  usually more aggressive), a *variant* (one individual changed within its life — scarred, one-horned, stuck between young
  and adult) and *tempered / apex / arch-tempered* (the same monster made stronger for the endgame) (sources 32, 33).
- **Where subspecies came from.** As cheap colour swaps for more armour colours. The developers themselves found plain
  recolours "pretty boring" and changed where and how you hit them; a natural-science-minded designer then made them about
  habitat and ecology (source 32).
- **What players say.** They love creatures fighting each other ("turf wars"). They reject stronger versions that bring no
  new behaviour or reward ("Apex monsters have no unique gear and therefore no reason to hunt them"; "the same monsters…
  with 4 times the hp"), and a thin endgame of tempered repeats drew one of the most-upvoted complaints about *Wilds* (3,660 upvotes — source 34).
  Small wildlife is wanted when it reacts and matters (Rise's emote-reacting endemic life), and called "useless" when it
  doesn't.
- **Lesson for Bug Farmer.** If a family gets a second or third kind, give it a habitat reason *and* a different way to
  deal with it, plus its own materials. "Same bug, more health" is the one variant players reliably dislike.

### Pokémon (Game Freak, 1996–) — regional forms, for the variant idea only

- **What the series does.** Since *Sun and Moon* (2016), well-known creatures get regional forms: same species, new look and
  abilities "due to regional adaptation" (source 35).
- **Why those creatures.** Director Shigeru Ohmori chose the original 151 because they are "the most recognizable among
  long-time fans": the familiar version makes the change surprising for veterans, while newcomers see a new creature. An
  in-game researcher explains each form as adapting to the islands' environment.
- **Riding.** The same game replaced vehicles with riding Pokémon, in service of "the relationship between humans and
  Pokémon… how they might… work together or live together" (source 35).
- **Lesson for Bug Farmer.** Recognizable first, variant second: a second kind of a famous bug (a desert-adapted scorpion; a
  fire ant next to the black garden ant) works *because* players already know the first. A variant of an obscure bug is
  just another obscure bug.

## Part 3 — Rules for picking and tiering a bug roster (from the evidence)

Each rule names the sources (row numbers in Part 1) that back it.

1. **Start with bugs players can name, and make variants only of those.** The familiar original makes a second kind readable;
   a variant of an obscure bug is just another obscure bug. Pokémon chose the original 151 for regional forms because they are
   "the most recognizable" (35); the bugs Grounded's reviewers write about most are famous ones — spiders above all (475 of
   2,500), then ladybugs, mosquitoes, bees and aphids — and reviewers single out the praying mantis boss as a favourite (14);
   community requests name famous bugs (flying insects, a centipede, cicadas, pill bugs — 9, 15, 38); Webbed's one famous
   spider sent players to look at real jumping spiders (31). Obscure species only landed where a narrator introduced each one
   to ant fans (23).
2. **Every kind must play differently — a new behaviour, not a new colour or a bigger number.** Monster Hunter's own developers
   found plain recolours "pretty boring" and changed how each is fought (32). Players call colour-and-stat kinds lazy: "4-5
   different hornets that are just rendered different colors" (19); Grounded's late kinds read as "reskins" (17); "the same
   monsters… with 4 times the hp" (34). The kinds players accept change *how you deal with them*: the orb weaver hunts by day,
   the wolf spider at night (2); Grounded 2's cockroach blocks (11).
3. **Build a family's 2nd and 3rd kind from that family's own real structure, in this order: a second real species of the same
   family; a caste (worker, soldier, queen); a life stage (nymph, larva, adult); a population adapted to another habitat.**
   Grounded's ants are species × caste (5, 37); Grounded 2's scorpions and toe biters are life stages (6); Pikmin gets a dozen
   believable kinds from stage, habitat, temper and age (27); Bug Fables and Monster Hunter explain each regional kind as an
   adaptation (24, 32). Ant-savvy players object when a game borrows another animal where the family has its own kind ("beetles
   instead of majors, hornets instead of flying ants" — 22). A temper difference can also be real: the "killer bee" is the
   same species as the honeybee with a far more defensive colony (it reacts faster and chases people up to 400 m — 40) — the
   real-world twin of Pikmin's easily-woken Orange Bulborb (27). Likewise the dragonfly is a predator both as a water-dwelling
   nymph and as a flying adult (40): two kinds, two habitats, two behaviours, one family.
4. **Name bugs the way players say them; never ship two names for one insect, or a life stage passed off as a species.** The
   most-upvoted knowledgeable review of Grounded lists exactly these slips: a generic "Larva" ("not a stand alone species"),
   ladybug and ladybird as two kinds though "the same insect", roly poly vs pill bug (14). Keep plain common names in play and
   put the scientific name, the true fact and the regional names in the journal, as Pikmin's book does (27, 28).
5. **Order danger by real reputation first, then by distance from the village.** The bug that is famously dangerous in life
   (fire ants, black widows, hornets) belongs farther out; tiering against reputation reads wrong to players who know bugs ("if
   the red ants are supposed to fire ants, would it not make more sense to switch the two ants' tiers?" — 14). Grounded puts the
   second, harsher kind of each family in the upper yard (37); Monster Hunter's habitat subspecies are "generally more
   aggressive" (32). Bug Farmer's black garden ant near home and fire ant farther out already follows this.
6. **Mix tempers in every zone; raise the share of hostile bugs outward, never to "everything attacks".** Players praise
   Grounded's spread ("Some are annoying, some are just cute, some are a serious challenge… some are the stuff of nightmares" —
   14) and the joke of a cute bug turning dangerous ("aww look a ladybug!" — 17). They punish saturation: "VERY overpopulated
   with every insect that wants to kill you" (19); "too many spiders in EVERY direction… add more ants early on" (38). Grounded's
   wiki sorts the whole roster by temper — harmless, neutral, angry — because that is the first thing a player needs to know
   (5, 37).
7. **Give every bug a home ground, a want and a timetable, so encounters can be learned.** Grounded 2 reviewers miss the first
   game's "defined territories that made encounters… so memorable" (17); bees are calm except at their hive (15, 37); aphids
   seek clover, gnats keep to water by day and light by night (8); spiders split day and night (2). But a home is a *range*, not
   a fixed spot: Smalland's "same number of the same creature in the same spot 100% of the time" made its world "unbelievably
   boring" (19).
8. **Let the ecosystem and the player's history make the events — never dice.** Grounded's bug raids come from "intruding on
   their territory or killing their kind… the more you annoy a particular species" (36); wasp nests answer when disturbed (10).
   One of Grounded's most-upvoted negative reviews (899 upvotes) asks for a yard that "fluctuate[s] with how players handle it…
   spiders dying, other things moving in" (14), and the best-loved moments in creature games are creatures fighting each other (Monster Hunter's turf
   wars, Grounded 2's "active warzone" — 34, 17). Bug Farmer's living food web is the advantage here; make its swings visible.
9. **Each kind gives its own materials, named after its famous trait.** Grounded's bombardier beetle drops a *Boiling Gland*,
   the firefly *Glow Goo*, the mosquito a *Beak* (37); new bugs arrive with gear from their trait (Firefly Head Lamp, Mosquito
   Needle — 9; tarantula gear — 12). Players object when a kind shares its parent's parts ("Ladybirds drop ladybug parts" — 14)
   and ignore stronger kinds with no reward of their own ("Apex monsters have no unique gear and therefore no reason to hunt
   them" — 34).
10. **A family's "boss" is its real top form in its real home, woken by disturbing that home.** The Wasp Queen waits in the
    hidden nest (10); Grounded's Broodmother is a real, striking spider (the spiny orb-weaver) fought at her egg sacs, calling in
    young (16); Pikmin's Empress births larvae (27); Hollow Knight's mothers spill or birth their young (26). Smalland's bosses,
    "often-unique variants of seen mobs" with extra moves, disappointed (19). A titled boss is fine when the title is earned by a
    real behaviour and the animal is a real species; an invented creature is not.
11. **Use one-of-a-kind bugs as rare landmarks.** The single famous creature tied to one place is what players retell: the
    tarantula boss and its gear (12), the orchid mantis (14), riding a praying mantis in one mission, "the highlight of the game"
    (22). Keep them few, famous and placed — Grounded's are bosses at landmarks (4, 16), not a second roster.
12. **Tell each bug's true story in a journal the player fills by meeting it.** Hollow Knight unlocks its hunter's notes only
    after many kills of a kind (26); Bug Fables gives every enemy a line from each hero (24, 25); Pikmin pairs a naturalist's
    note with a cook's recipe (28); Empires of the Undergrowth's narrator made players into ant keepers (23). A bare list is
    called "a checklist" (22). One true fact, its food, its enemies, what it gives, and how many you have met.

## Part 4 — What to avoid (with the evidence)

- **Colour swaps sold as new kinds.** "4-5 different hornets that are just rendered different colors and the beetles all
  have the same attacks" (Smalland, 502 upvotes — 19); Grounded's late additions called "reskins" (17). Monster Hunter's
  developers reached the same verdict from the inside: "pretty boring" (32).
- **"Same bug, more health" as a tier or an endgame.** "The same monsters… with 4 times the hp and double the damage"; apex
  forms with "no unique gear and therefore no reason to hunt them" (Monster Hunter Rise — 34); bosses that are "variants of
  seen mobs" with extra moves (Smalland — 19).
- **Two names for one insect, or a life stage passed off as a species.** Ladybug and "ladybird" as two kinds; a generic
  "Larva" enemy (Grounded — 14, 40).
- **Borrowing another animal where the family has its own kind.** "Beetles instead of majors, hornets instead of flying ants"
  (Empire of the Ants — 22).
- **Invented creatures mixed in with real ones.** Smalland's vague "Dawn Ant", "Colony Spider", "Nocturnal Spider" sit next to
  real black widows and stag beetles (18). Grounded keeps its few invented things fenced off as story (robots, an infection, one
  mutant) and every creature boss is a real animal (5, 16, 37).
- **Fixed spawn spots and identical daily respawns.** "The same number of the same creature in the same spot 100% of the
  time" (19); wolf spiders that "respawn daily… no longer a threat… just annoying" (14).
- **Saturating a zone with hostiles, especially early.** "VERY overpopulated with every insect that wants to kill you" (19);
  "too many spiders in EVERY direction" leaving the start (38); "giant scorpions and praying mantis… always between you and
  where you want to go" (Grounded 2 — 17).
- **Harassers with reach you cannot escape.** Grounded's mosquitoes that "hit you when you zip line" and stay angry while you
  swim away drew a long run of complaints (the Grounded forum search for "mosquito" returns 660 entries — 15).
- **Bugs appearing inside the player's home.** Larvae "who keep spawning in my base" (14); "Spiders spawning in my 4x4 House"
  (a Grounded 2 forum thread, https://steamcommunity.com/app/2661300/discussions/0/418424007826625458/).
- **Making the cute, friendly bug something you must kill.** "having to kill the caterpillar to get certain gear is so cruel,
  its so cute and friendly" (17).
- **Tames that are slow to get, fragile and soon obsolete.** Smalland's taming is "Absolutely pointless… nowhere near worth
  it"; tames "die fast, and are mostly useless" (19); Obsidian added buggy levels because otherwise "nobody would… want to use a
  Red Ant Soldier beyond early areas" (13). And the reverse warning: if raising bugs becomes required, "it becomes not a choice
  but a necessity" (15).
- **Ignoring the fears bugs raise.** Both Grounded and Webbed ship an arachnophobia setting (1, 30) and players praise it (14,
  31); a reviewer asked for the same care for "bees and wasps" (14), another warned of "large roaches in the dark" (17).
- **A pile of special-tier labels.** Monster Hunter has gathered about 30 variant classes over the years (33). Pick one or two
  ideas (habitat form, old individual) and keep them.
- **A species book that is only a checklist** (22).

## Part 5 — How giant-bug games make a bug read as livestock or as a threat

- **Temper is the first signal, and it is consistent.** Grounded sorts every creature as harmless (flees), neutral (fights back
  if hit; its kin swarm in) or angry (attacks on sight), and the players' own wiki is organised by those three classes (5, 37). A bug can be calm in
  the field and angry at its home (bees at the picnic-table hive — 15, 37). The joke and the lesson of "aww look a ladybug!"
  then "body-checked into next week" (17) is that temper must be readable *before* contact.
- **The tame ones are the harmless and the young.** Grounded's pets are aphids, gnats, weevils and baby ants, each won with the
  food it really eats; baby ants come from feeding an ant queen (16). Grounded 2's mounts come from eggs stolen from the wild
  and hatched (7). The same species can be both: wild soldier ants attack, a raised soldier ant carries you, and riding one makes
  wild soldiers of that species leave you alone (7).
- **Raising should be a process with steps, and the animal should grow.** Egg → hatchery (a day) → raise → level up (7, 13).
  A much-upvoted review asked for more steps, not fewer: bait, "the saddle, friendship levels" (17). Livestock that cannot grow is dropped as
  soon as a better one appears (13, 19). Nintendo found one deep partner creature (Oatchi, who rides, carries and is trained)
  more coherent than letting players "ride and control various creatures" (29).
- **Players want live yields, not only kill drops.** "A pen filled with aphids I can collect honeydew from every day" (14);
  ranching where ants bring food, young orb weavers give silk, bees give pollen and honey, and young scorpions give venom (38);
  regret at killing a friendly caterpillar for gear (17). Real biology backs the premise: "dairying ants" tend aphids "for their
  honeydew and protecting them from predators", and ladybugs are those predators (40); Empires of the Undergrowth turns exactly
  that into its loop (39).
- **The threat to livestock is a real predator, at a real time.** Ladybugs raid the aphid herd; "dangers that lurk after
  sundown" take careless workers (39); Grounded's raids come from species the player has angered (36). This fits Bug Farmer's
  rule of no random spawn events (my reading: the danger to a fly farm would be what really eats flies, drawn in by the farm).
- **Look and setting do the rest.** Stylize the scary bugs until they are almost cute (4); beautiful, not fearsome, wins over
  non-fans (20); cute with a touch of eeriness reads as wildlife (29). Bug Fables splits talking social insects from "feral"
  wild bugs (25) — a reminder that a reader needs a clear sign of which bugs belong to people. (My inference, not from a
  source: in Bug Farmer that sign could be the farm itself — pens, harness, a name tag — rather than a different species.)
- **Riding and working with creatures is a relationship feature.** Pokémon replaced vehicles with riding Pokémon to put "the
  relationship between humans and Pokémon" first (35); Grounded's players made mounts one of their most-requested features (11).

## Summary (10 lines)

1. Loved rosters are made of bugs players already know; variants work best on the most recognizable creatures (Pokémon's regional forms, Grounded's backyard bugs).
2. A second or third kind must play differently (a habitat, a time of day, a caste, a life stage, a temper), never just a new colour or more health (Monster Hunter's own developers; Smalland's "4-5 hornets… different colors").
3. Build kinds from the family's real structure: another real species, a caste, a life stage, or a population adapted to a place (Grounded's ants, Grounded 2's scorpions, Pikmin's Bulborbs, the killer bee as a honeybee with a different temper).
4. Never two names for one insect, never a life stage passed off as a species, never a borrowed animal where the family has its own kind (Grounded's "ladybird" and "Larva"; Empire of the Ants' beetles in place of major workers).
5. Put famously dangerous bugs farther out; mix harmless, fight-back and hostile bugs in every zone, with hostility rising outward but never "everything attacks" (the trait Smalland's reviewers attack).
6. Give every bug a home range, a want and a timetable: players miss territories when they are gone and tire of bugs fixed to one spot.
7. Let events come from the food web and the player's own deeds (Grounded's species-by-species raids), never from dice; bugs fighting bugs is what players retell.
8. Each kind drops its own materials, named for its famous trait; a family's boss is its real top form in its home (a queen in her nest); famous one-of-a-kind bugs work best as rare landmarks.
9. For livestock: tame the harmless and the young, raise from eggs in steps, let the animal grow, and give live yields (honeydew, silk, honey); Grounded players have asked for this, and real ants already herd aphids.
10. Tell each bug's true story in a journal filled by meeting it, and offer fear settings; a kind, true portrait (Webbed's peacock spider) turns fear into affection.
