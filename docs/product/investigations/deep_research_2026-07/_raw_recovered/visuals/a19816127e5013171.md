## What actually makes it look good (concrete techniques)

### 1. Ambient/global COLOR as a multiplier — the single biggest lever
Every dev source starts here, not with point lights. The whole scene is first tinted by one global color and then lights are *added back* on top:

- **Godot** uses `CanvasModulate` — a single node that multiplies the entire scene by a color. Merxon sets it **dark blue for a night vibe**; GDQuest stresses using **dark gray, not pure black**, so shadows still show sprite detail and don't over-saturate into flat black.
- **URP's** equivalent is a **Global Light 2D** ("lights all 2D GameObjects equally with no attenuation"). The glow tutorial sets Global Light intensity **~0.65** so the base scene reads as dim-but-legible, then local lights lift specific areas.
- **Why it reads better than piling on point lights:** a single multiplier establishes one coherent mood and one shadow color across the whole frame. Point lights only *add* brightness — they can't create the darkness that makes light feel like light. Without a dark ambient base, adding more point lights just washes everything toward flat mid-brightness. Devs "tweak per object" by letting emissive/lit materials or a rim ignore part of the ambient, so hero objects pop against the tint.

### 2. Soft falloff vs hard point falloff
This is the number-one "tell" of amateur lighting. A raw point light with linear/hard falloff gives a hard-edged bright disc. The good-looking approaches all soften it:

- **Orangepixel's method is the purest version:** they don't use a real-time light at all for the glow — they **pre-render a soft light image** (a white dot painted with a soft, anti-aliased brush) and **alpha/additively blend it over the screen** at various sizes, colors and translucency. The softness is baked into the texture. This is effectively a **light cookie**, and it's cheap and pixel-art friendly.
- **Godot** reproduces this with a `GradientTexture2D` set to **Radial** fill (From (0.5,0.5) → To (0.5,0)), then dragging the gradient knobs to tune **hard vs soft edges** — a hand-tuned falloff curve rather than an inverse-square formula.
- **URP** exposes **Falloff / Falloff Strength** ("how quickly light intensity decreases from inner to outer") on Freeform and Spot lights, plus **Inner Radius / Outer Radius** so the core is solid and the edge feathers. You can also **override the falloff function** (e.g. to quadratic/inverse-square) for a more physical rolloff. The **Sprite** light type literally takes a sprite as the light shape — i.e. a custom gradient **cookie**.
- **Rim light** (from the first-angle search) is called out as especially effective in pixel art: a light grazing the silhouette edge adds contour/depth cheaply.

Takeaway: soft, feathered, gradient-driven falloff (baked cookie or high Falloff Strength) instead of a hard-edged disc.

### 3. Layering warm + cool, and per-area ambient
- Local lights are tinted **warm** (SpriteIlluminator uses "saturated yellow-orange" for lamps/windows; Merxon uses yellowish point lights) while the **ambient** multiplier is pushed **cool** (dark blue night). The warm/cool contrast is what makes a torch feel hot against a cold room.
- **Blend styles matter for layering:** URP lets each light be **Additive** (stacks toward white — good for glows/torches) or **Multiply** (tints/darkens — good for colored ambient zones), with an **Overlap Operation** (Additive vs Alpha Blend) controlling how overlapping lights combine. Additive stacking is how warm pools read on top of the cool base without muddying.
- **Per-area ambient for mood:** rather than one global tint everywhere, devs change the ambient per room/zone (cave = cold blue-black, lava room = warm amber) so movement between areas has emotional beats.

### 4. Normal maps — surface relief, and the honest cost
How: a normal map encodes, per pixel, the direction the surface faces in RGB (XYZ), so a flat sprite "catches" light as if it had 3D relief.
- **Authoring (SpriteIlluminator):** select each surface region, pick its facing with the angle selector, paint the corresponding RGB with a brush (size/opacity/hardness), verify in normal view, export as `texture_n.png` (the `_n` suffix tells Unity).
- **Generating:** Photoshop/Krita "Height → Normal Map" filter, or Sobel-based auto-generators (Laigter, ModLab, Aseprite Normal Toolkit). RefresherTowel's real pipeline: hand-draw a **height map** (white = nearest, black = farthest), then generate the normal from it in ModLab.
- **Engine hookup:** assign the normal as the sprite's secondary/normal texture; in URP enable **Normal Map Quality (Accurate/Fast)** and tune **Normal Map Distance**. In Godot the **light Height** property (Merxon uses **30 px**) lifts the light off the plane so the normals actually shade — height 0 = no relief.
- **The cost (stated plainly by devs):**
  - RefresherTowel: the height map alone is **~1–2 hours per sprite**, and you often want a whole map stack (height, normal, roughness, AO, emissive).
  - GDQuest: **animated sprites and skeletons need a custom shader** (no built-in normal slot), and **tilemaps require assigning the normal per tile — "tedious."** Every animation frame needs a matching normal frame.
  - URP: normal-mapped lights need enough **pixel light count** or you get artifacts.
- **Worth it for pixel art?** Mixed verdict. It shines on large, slow, hero/props and metallic/organic surfaces (RefresherTowel's Dice Trek), and animated flicker/torchlight crawling over relief looks great. But for a whole world of small animated sprites the per-frame authoring cost is brutal, and at low resolution the payoff per pixel shrinks. Most pixel-art devs get 80% of the look from ambient tint + soft cookies + baked shadows first, and reserve normal maps for select surfaces.

### 5. Fake/contact shadows + ambient occlusion — the grounding that stops "pasted-on"
This is the single biggest fix for sprites that look like stickers:
- **Contact shadow baked under the sprite:** Pixel Joint's pixel artists recommend a **top-down light assumption so the shadow sits directly beneath the object** — it can be baked into the sprite and never overlaps neighbors. A simple soft ellipse of shadow under feet/base instantly grounds a sprite.
- **AO baked into the art:** RefresherTowel authors an **AO map by hand** (there's "no easy way to automate it") — take a brightened height map and manually darken crevices, contact edges and undersides, keeping the **darkest value above the midpoint** so it doesn't go muddy. AO darkens where surfaces meet, which is exactly what sells contact.
- **Dynamic cast shadows:** URP Freeform/Sprite/Spot lights have **Shadow Intensity** with shadow-caster components; Godot uses `LightOccluder2D` polygons / tileset occlusion layers. Pixel Joint warns of the classic bug: **layering multiple semi-transparent shadows double-darkens overlaps** — the fix is a single **black-and-white shadow map applied at low opacity**, and remembering "shadow = absence of light," so you never stack a shadow on already-shadowed pixels.
- **Shadow color, not shadow black:** repeatedly emphasized — "shadows look best when they're not just darker versions of the original colours." Tint them toward the ambient/complementary hue (cool blue in a warm scene). Pure-black drop shadows are a naive tell.

### 6. Bloom, color grading/LUT, vignette (post FX for mood)
- **Bloom on emissive/glow (the gamedeveloper.com pipeline, end-to-end):**
  1. URP 2D renderer, **enable HDR** in the pipeline quality asset (so brightness >1 can exist and Bloom can pick it up).
  2. Enable **post-processing on the camera**.
  3. Give glowing sprites an **emission map** (`_Emission` secondary texture) and a **Sprite-Unlit/Lit shader** that multiplies the emission by an **HDR color** (e.g. cyan `#00D1FF`) — intensity >1 is what actually blooms.
  4. Global Volume → **Bloom override: Threshold 1, Intensity 0.5** (only pixels above 1 glow). Optionally add a **Point Light 2D** on the emitter (intensity ~0.51, falloff ~0.765) so it also lights its surroundings, not just itself.
- **Color grading / LUT (Alisavakis, with shader):** a **neutral LUT** is a 1024×32 strip (32 tiles of 32×32; R/G across X/Y, B quantized into 32 steps). To grade: drop the neutral LUT as a layer **over a game screenshot**, apply curves/levels/saturation to both together, export the modified LUT. Sample it in a fullscreen shader (full CG frag provided) and **`lerp(original, graded, _Contribution)`** for dialable strength. Import as **no compression, no mipmaps, sRGB off, clamp, aniso 0** or you get banding/edge bleed. ProPixelizer even ships **NES/PAL/GameBoy LUTs** for instant retro moods. This is how you set a consistent palette/mood across the whole game in one pass.
- **Vignette:** darkens frame edges to focus the eye and deepen mood; per ProPixelizer it's cheap enough to run in the geometry pass alongside grade/film-grain.

### The specific "tells" — naive vs polished

| Naive point-light look | Polished look |
|---|---|
| No dark ambient base; scene is uniformly bright | Dark colored ambient multiplier first, light added back on top |
| Hard-edged bright discs (linear falloff) | Soft feathered cookies / high Falloff Strength / inverse-square |
| All lights white | Warm lights against a cool ambient (warm/cool contrast) |
| Sprites float / look pasted-on | Contact shadow + baked AO grounding every object |
| Pure-black shadows | Tinted, colored shadows toward ambient hue |
| Glow entities are just bright pixels | HDR emission + Bloom halo + a real Point Light casting onto neighbors |
| Inconsistent per-scene color | One LUT/color-grade unifying mood across the game |
| Flat frame, eye wanders | Vignette + per-area ambient guiding attention |

## Prioritized changes for your project (URP 2D, post-processing OFF, glow entities emit no light, pixel art)

Ordered by look-improvement per unit of effort:

1. **Turn on a dark, colored Global ambient and stop relying on default brightness.** Add/adjust a **Global Light 2D** to a cool, dim tint (e.g. dark blue-gray, intensity ~0.6) so there's actual darkness for lights to fight. This is the foundation; nothing else looks lit until this exists. Cheapest, biggest single jump.

2. **Make `mushroom_glow` and `crystals` actually emit light.** Two-part, both cheap:
   - Add a **Point/Sprite Light 2D** on each emitter, colored to the glow, with **Outer Radius** feathered and high **Falloff Strength** (or a soft radial **Sprite cookie**), **Additive** blend. Now they pool warm/colored light onto nearby tiles instead of being inert bright pixels.
   - Give the emissive pixels an **HDR emission color** (>1 intensity) so they're ready for bloom.

3. **Re-enable post-processing — you have it configured to zero, which is pure upside.**
   - **Bloom:** Threshold ~1, Intensity ~0.5, with **HDR enabled** on the pipeline. Combined with step 2 this is what makes mushrooms/crystals actually glow and haze.
   - **Color grade / LUT:** author one graded LUT from a screenshot to lock a consistent mood; dial via a contribution slider.
   - **Vignette:** subtle, to frame the scene.

4. **Soften every light's falloff and layer warm/cool.** Audit existing point lights: increase **Falloff Strength**, set a solid **Inner Radius** with a feathered **Outer Radius**, or swap to **Sprite** lights using a soft radial gradient cookie (the Orangepixel pre-baked approach — cheapest and most pixel-art-authentic). Tint local lights warm against the cool ambient.

5. **Ground your sprites with baked contact shadow + AO.** Add a soft, slightly-tinted (not black) ellipse shadow under each object's base, and darken contact crevices in the art. This kills the "pasted-on" look and needs zero runtime cost. Use a single shadow layer / low-opacity shadow map to avoid double-darkening overlaps.

6. **(Optional, later) Normal maps on select hero surfaces only.** Reserve for large, slow, or metallic/organic props where torch/glow flicker crawling over relief pays off. Skip it for the general animated cast — the per-frame + per-tile authoring cost (1–2 hrs/sprite for the height map, custom shader for animated sprites, per-tile assignment for tilemaps) isn't worth it project-wide. Steps 1–5 deliver most of the "polished" look for a fraction of the effort.
