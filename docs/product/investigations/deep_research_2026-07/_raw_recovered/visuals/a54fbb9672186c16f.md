# Look-and-feel / game-feel techniques for BugFarmer (cozy top-down pixel farming, Unity 6 / URP 17.2 2D Renderer, multiplayer)

## Framing / the one philosophy to keep

Vlambeer's own framing (Art of Screenshake; "Juice it or lose it"): **juice is additive, never load-bearing** — it sits on top of a game that already works. The Nuclear Throne trick list is ~30 layered micro-effects, but that list was tuned for a *violent* game. For a cozy farming game the discipline is the opposite of "add screenshake": most of these techniques should be dialed to 10–20% of their action-game intensity, made *optional*, and applied to **reward/feedback moments** (harvest, pickup, plant growth, tool use) rather than to combat/impact. The cross-cutting hard constraints for your project are: (1) **pixel-perfect rendering fights fractional scaling and sub-pixel camera motion**, and (2) **all of this is presentation-layer and must stay client-local** — never in the networked sim.

I've split every technique into **cheap cozy wins** vs **action juice that would feel wrong here**.

## CATEGORY A — Shadows / AO / grounding (no baked-in shadows allowed)

Key architecture fact I verified and want to lead with, because it changes your options: **URP Decal Projectors and the Decal Renderer Feature belong to the 3D Universal Renderer, not the 2D Renderer.** The most-cited "blob shadow" tutorial (prasetion, below) literally has you add the Decal Renderer Feature and rotate a projector to face down — that path is **not available on a 2D-Renderer project** without switching renderers. So for BugFarmer, blob shadows should be **sprites or shader quads**, not decals. (2D `ShadowCaster2D` + `Light2D` shadows do exist but are a *lighting* feature — out of scope per your note.)

### A1. Blob / contact shadow sprite (a second SpriteRenderer under the object) — THE cozy default
- **Approach:** Give each grounded object a child `SpriteRenderer` using a soft dark oval/radial-gradient sprite, semi-transparent (multiply or straight alpha ~40–60%), sorted just under the object, anchored at the sprite's *base* (feet/trunk), not its center. Scale the oval to footprint width. Optionally shrink/fade it slightly when the object "hops" (bug jump, item toss) for cheap verticality.
- **Cost:** Dev: trivial (one reusable sprite + a prefab slot). Runtime: one extra draw per object; batches well if all use the same shadow texture/atlas — negligible unless you have thousands, in which case pool/atlas them.
- **Reference game:** Stardew Valley grounds characters, trees, and debris with small soft under-shadows; near-universal in top-down (Zelda-like) games.
- **Fit: HIGH.** Cheapest possible fix for "pasted-on" sprites; reads instantly; art-pipeline-safe (the shadow is a separate asset, nothing baked into the object sprite).
- **Pixel-art / MP gotcha:** A soft radial oval is *not* hard pixel art — decide deliberately whether you want a crisp dithered pixel blob (matches your "hard pixel art" mandate) or an intentionally soft blob (breaks the pixel rule but reads as light). If you scale the oval per-object, scale in integer steps or accept softness on the shadow only. Purely cosmetic → render client-local, no network involvement.

### A2. Projected / skewed silhouette shadow via shader (dynamic, shaped like the sprite)
- **Approach:** A canvas/sprite shader that re-uses the object's own alpha silhouette, then **skews it from a base line and vertically squashes it** to lie on the ground. The concrete math (Gobs & Gods writeup): define a `_yRoot` = base of sprite in UV; compute `dy = clamp(_yRoot - UV.y, 0, 1)`; offset the sampled shadow pixel by `dy * shadowDirection`, with `shadowDirection` converted to **pixel space via `TEXTURE_PIXEL_SIZE`** so the lean angle doesn't depend on sprite aspect ratio; expand the vertex quad on the shadow side so it isn't clipped; `smoothstep` the edge alpha to kill border artifacts. Result: a directional, object-shaped ground shadow with zero baked art.
- **Cost:** Dev: moderate (custom shader, per-sprite `_yRoot`, quad expansion). Runtime: extra silhouette sample(s) per fragment + larger quad; cheap per object but real if every tile/bug uses it. Multi-part sprites need a render-to-texture pass first (extra cost).
- **Reference game:** This exact technique is the Gobs & Gods approach; conceptually the "leaning silhouette" grounding many action-RPGs use for characters.
- **Fit: MEDIUM.** Lovely for hero-ish objects (the player, big trees, buildings) but overkill for every pebble — mix with A1 for small props.
- **Pixel-art / MP gotcha:** A skewed/squashed silhouette will land on **fractional pixels** and shimmer under a pixel-perfect camera; snap the shadow offset to whole texels or let the shadow be the one "soft" element. Fully cosmetic → client-local.

### A3. URP Decal blob shadow (documenting it so you *don't* burn a day on it)
- **Approach:** Decal Shader Graph + Decal Projector rotated `(90,0,0)` facing down, projecting a soft blob onto the ground; add the Decal Renderer Feature.
- **Cost:** Dev: low *if you were on the 3D renderer*. Runtime: decals are cheap in small numbers but you'd be adding a whole rendering feature.
- **Reference game:** Standard 3D/2.5D blob-shadow method.
- **Fit: LOW for BugFarmer.** Requires the 3D Universal Renderer; **not supported on your 2D Renderer.** Use A1/A2 instead. Listed only so nobody proposes it later.
- **Gotcha:** Renderer incompatibility is the whole story here.

### A4. AO-style contact darkening / "grounding band" (no shadow shape at all)
- **Approach:** Two cheap tricks that read as ambient occlusion: (a) a soft dark **gradient patch multiply-blended** right where the object meets the tile (part of the blob sprite or a tiny separate quad), darkest at contact and fading out ~a few px; (b) a subtly darker **1–2px band at the base** of tall objects (fence posts, trunks) so the bottom "sits" in shade. This is the "objects meet ground" darkening you asked about, independent of any cast direction.
- **Cost:** Dev: trivial. Runtime: negligible (baked into the blob or one small alpha quad).
- **Reference game:** Ubiquitous in cozy pixel art; Stardew's tile-edge and object-base darkening reads this way.
- **Fit: HIGH.** Cheapest AO you can get; pairs with A1.
- **Pixel-art / MP gotcha:** Do the darkening with a dithered/indexed dark tone to keep it "hard pixel," not a Gaussian blur. Cosmetic → client-local.

### A5. Non-shadow grounding tricks (do these *first* — they're free)
- **Approach:** (1) **Overlap** the object's base a few pixels *into* the ground/foliage (sink feet/trunk 2–3px below the tile seam) so the silhouette interrupts the ground plane; (2) add a small **contact tuft** — grass blades, dirt scuff, pebbles — hand-drawn at the base to break the hard bottom edge; (3) correct **Y-sort/overlap** so nearby objects visibly occlude each other. None of these is a shadow, and all directly kill the "pasted-on" look your prompt is worried about.
- **Cost:** Dev: art-time only (tufts), placement/sorting logic you likely already have. Runtime: zero.
- **Reference game:** Stardew, Spiritfarer, and most quality pixel top-downs lean heavily on base-overlap + tufts, *plus* a light blob shadow.
- **Fit: HIGH.** Combined with A1+A4 this is the whole "professional grounding" package without touching the forbidden baked-shadow pipeline.
- **Pixel-art / MP gotcha:** These are static authoring choices — perfectly pixel-crisp and zero network cost.

## CATEGORY B — Camera juice / game-feel (be discerning: cozy ≠ action)

### B1. Screenshake — mostly a "when NOT to" for you
- **Approach (action canon):** Trauma-based shake — keep a `trauma` float 0–1, add on events, decay each frame; offset = `maxOffset * trauma²` and **rotation = maxAngle * trauma²** (squaring makes small hits subtle, big hits punchy). Nijman's key point that most implementations miss: **add a fraction of a degree of rotation**, and use magnitude tiers (tiny for routine, big for real events).
- **Cost:** Dev: low (a dozen lines + an Impulse source if you use Cinemachine). Runtime: negligible.
- **Reference game:** Nuclear Throne (heavy, per Nijman). Cozy counter-example: Disney Dreamlight Valley and Death Must Die ship explicit **screen-shake toggles** because the audience skews motion-sensitive.
- **Fit: LOW–MEDIUM, and gate it.** Motion sickness affects up to ~1/3 of people; camera shake is a top trigger, and cozy audiences expect calm. Allowed uses: a *tiny* thud on a tree finally falling, a chest opening, a big harvest — offset of a couple pixels, no rotation, ~100ms. **Ship an on/off toggle (binary, not just a slider), default low, available before the game starts.** Do NOT shake on every hoe/water/step.
- **Pixel-art / MP gotcha:** Shake writes sub-pixel camera positions → jitter under Pixel Perfect Camera. The verified fix: **nest the pixel-perfect camera under a "shaker" parent** — the shaker moves/rotates in floats, the camera keeps a pixel-rounded local offset of 0. Screenshake is **strictly client-local**; never derive it from or feed it into the networked sim.

### B2. Hitstop / freeze-frame — repurpose gently for tool "weight"
- **Approach:** On impact, freeze the acting sprite's animation for a few dozen ms, then resume — it gives the eye a couple frames to register the hit and makes the strike feel like it *cost energy*. Pair with a hit-spark/particle at the contact point.
- **Cost:** Dev: low. Runtime: negligible.
- **Reference game:** Fighting games / Zelda hit emphasis (freeze both parties); Dark Souls 2 is the cautionary tale — its *lack* of hitstop makes hits read as weak.
- **Fit: LOW for combat, MEDIUM as a tool micro-hold.** No fighting-game hitstop in a farm sim. But a **very short (~40–60ms) hold on the swing frame** when an axe bites a tree or the hoe strikes soil adds satisfying weight. Keep it under a couple frames or it feels laggy.
- **Pixel-art / MP gotcha — important:** In a deterministic multiplayer sim you **cannot pause the simulation clock** for hitstop (it would desync or freeze other players). Implement it as a **visual-only animation freeze** on that client's sprite/particles while the sim keeps ticking. Client-local only.

### B3. Squash-and-stretch on sprites — the classic, with a pixel-grid catch
- **Approach:** Volume-preserving deform: on impact/landing/collect, scale Y down + X up, then ease back (ease-out-back overshoot) — a bouncing "pop." Great targets in cozy: item pickup pop, crop/flower **growth pop-in**, tool wind-up (anticipation) and follow-through, a bug's little hop. Celeste squashes Madeline *aggressively* on dash/jump/wall-grab; even a 16×16 sprite benefits from ~1px compress before a jump and 1px stretch at peak.
- **Cost:** Dev: low if done by tweening transform scale; **high if hand-authored** as deform frames per sprite.
- **Reference game:** Celeste (aggressive, character); cozy dialing = a gentle overshoot on pickups/plants.
- **Fit: HIGH (this is a top cozy win).** Cheap, charming, non-violent, and it makes rewards feel alive.
- **Pixel-art / MP gotcha — the big one:** **Runtime non-integer scaling breaks pixel-perfect** — a 1px feature can only render as 1 or 2 screen-px, so continuous scaling shimmers/decimates. Three verified options: (1) **hand-drawn squash frames** (crisp, but expensive art); (2) **secondary-motion / offset timing** — delay one body part behind another to *imply* stretch without scaling (the "Mind Over Magnet" trick), pixel-safe; (3) allow true scaling but **exempt that sprite from pixel-snapping** (render it slightly soft during the ~150ms pop, snap back after) — usually acceptable on a brief reward pop, jarring on constant motion. Pure cosmetic → client-local; do NOT let the squash gate the authoritative "item collected" event.

### B4. Camera follow: lerp + dead zone + subtle look-ahead
- **Approach:** Smooth follow via `Vector3.Lerp`/`SmoothDamp` toward the player, a **dead zone** (soft box) so tiny movements don't nudge the camera, and a **small look-ahead/lead** in the movement direction. In Cinemachine (v3 Position/Rotation Composer): **Look Ahead Time** + **Look Ahead Smoothing**, per-axis **Damping**, and a **Dead Zone**. Weight multiple points-of-interest by proximity × importance if you want the camera to hint at nearby things (russmatney).
- **Cost:** Dev: low (Cinemachine handles it) to moderate (hand-rolled with pixel snapping). Runtime: negligible.
- **Reference game:** Celeste / Hollow Knight dead-zone + look-ahead cameras; Cinemachine's own 2D samples.
- **Fit: HIGH but keep look-ahead SMALL.** Smooth follow reads as calm and premium — perfect cozy. But heavy look-ahead/whip feels action-y and can induce sickness; use gentle damping and a modest lead. Consider a "reduce camera smoothing" accessibility option.
- **Pixel-art / MP gotcha — cluster:** Smoothed follow writes sub-pixel positions → jitter with Pixel Perfect Camera. Verified fixes: run follow in `LateUpdate` with **negative execution order** so it precedes the pixel camera, and **snap the smoothed float to `1/pixelsPerUnit`** before assigning:
  ```csharp
  float unit = 1f/pixelsPerUnit;
  desired = Vector3.Lerp(transform.position, target.position, smooth*Time.deltaTime);
  desired.x = Mathf.Round(desired.x/unit)*unit;
  desired.y = Mathf.Round(desired.y/unit)*unit;
  ```
  Note Pixel Perfect also **restricts smooth damping, dynamic zoom, and bouncy scaling** — budget for the tension up front. In multiplayer, **each client owns its own camera** following its own player; camera state is never networked.

### B5. Tween / easing on pickups & UI — your single biggest, cheapest cozy win
- **Approach:** Ease everything that rewards the player. Concretely: **pickup magnet** (items within a radius accelerate toward the player and vanish — Vampire Survivors' gem magnet is the canonical feel, base radius ~30u there); **arc toss** on drop (item pops out on a parabola with ease-out); **ease-out-back overshoot** on collect and on menu/panel open; **number roll** on coin/XP counters; wobble/scale-punch on new-item toasts. Use named easings — ease-out-back for "pop," ease-in-out for panels — or Squirrel Eiserloh's `smoothStart2–5` / `smoothStop` curves for tunable intensity.
- **Cost:** Dev: low with a tween lib (**DOTween** is the standard Unity choice: `transform.DOScale`, `DOMove`, `DOPunchScale`, `value.DOTween` for counters). Runtime: cheap; DOTween pools tweens — just don't spawn thousands of simultaneous UI tweens unpooled.
- **Reference game:** Vampire Survivors (pickup magnet), Stardew (item toss arcs + UI), Spiritfarer (soft eased UI). Cozy games live and die on this layer.
- **Fit: HIGH — do this first.** It's the "expensive-feeling" polish that is actually cheap, and it's entirely non-violent: pure reward feedback, exactly the cozy register.
- **Pixel-art / MP gotcha:** Same fractional-position/scale shimmer risk — snap final resting positions to the grid; the in-flight arc can be soft. **Multiplayer:** the magnet/arc is *cosmetic*. The authoritative "who collected what" is a networked event; play the visual pull toward where the item will resolve, and don't let a client's tween decide ownership (avoid two players' visuals both sucking in the same gem). Client-local visuals, server-authoritative pickup.

### B6. Screen flash & subtle zoom-punch — mostly action juice; use homeopathically
- **Approach:** Brief white/color full-screen overlay on impact (Nuclear Throne), or a quick zoom-in/out "punch" on a big event.
- **Cost:** Dev: low. Runtime: negligible (a fullscreen quad / small ortho-size tween).
- **Reference game:** Nuclear Throne (flash), action platformers (zoom punch on hits).
- **Fit: LOW for cozy, with narrow exceptions.** A hard white flash is aggressive and a **photosensitivity risk** (Disney games toggle flashing off) — avoid as combat feedback. Acceptable cozy reskins: a **gentle vignette/glow pulse** on level-up or a rare treasure, or a **tiny** zoom-in (a few % ortho size, eased over ~300ms) on a special cutscene/first-harvest — never on routine actions.
- **Pixel-art / MP gotcha:** Zoom that changes ortho size mid-play **fights Pixel Perfect** (breaks integer scaling) and can nauseate — prefer flash/vignette over zoom, and gate both behind the accessibility toggle. Client-local.

## Cheap cozy-appropriate WINS (do these)
1. **A1 blob shadow sprite + A4 contact darkening + A5 base-overlap/tufts** — the complete grounding package, no baked shadows, near-zero runtime.
2. **B5 tween/easing on pickups & UI** (magnet, arc toss, ease-out-back pop, number roll) — highest feel-per-hour, pure reward register.
3. **B3 gentle squash-and-stretch** on pickups, tool wind-up/follow-through, and crop growth pop-in (via secondary-motion or brief snap-exempt scaling).
4. **B4 smooth follow + dead zone + small look-ahead** — reads calm and premium.
5. **B2 a ~40–60ms visual-only tool hold** on axe/hoe impact for weight.

## Action-game juice that would feel WRONG here (avoid or homeopathic + optional)
- **B1 heavy screenshake with rotation** — Nuclear Throne register; at most a tiny, rotation-free, toggle-able thud on rare big events.
- **B2 fighting-game hitstop** on combat — no combat to justify it; and it must never pause the shared sim.
- **B6 hard white screen flash / big zoom punches / chromatic aberration** — photosensitivity + motion-sickness risk, and tonally jarring for cozy.
- Constant camera whip / aggressive look-ahead — save the lead for subtle.
- **Always ship: screen-shake OFF toggle + reduced-motion option, binary, default-calm, available pre-game.**

## Cross-cutting engineering constraints (apply to nearly every technique)
- **Pixel-perfect vs juice tension is real:** smooth follow, sub-pixel shake, fractional squash, and dynamic zoom all shimmer under Pixel Perfect Camera. Fixes: negative-execution-order `LateUpdate`, snap smoothed positions to `1/PPU`, **nest the camera under a float-moving shaker**, and exempt brief pop-scales from snapping.
- **Everything here is presentation/client-local.** Cameras, shake, flash, shadows, tweens are per-client and never networked. **Hitstop is the trap:** freeze the *animation*, never the deterministic simulation. Pickup magnet/arc are cosmetic; the authoritative collect is a server event.
