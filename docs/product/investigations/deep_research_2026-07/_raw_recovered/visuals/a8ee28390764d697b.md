## Per-game findings

### APICO (the one you flagged) — palette is the whole strategy
This is the clearest "we picked soothing colors on purpose" case. Lead dev Elliott (TNgineers), from source 1:
- **Palette:** *"In terms of the palette, I wanted to pick some interesting colors like mint green and purple brown."* The move here is choosing *unconventional, low-saturation, slightly-desaturated pastels* instead of default-bright RGB. Mint-green + purple-brown is a **complementary-ish, muted pairing** — that's what reads as "soothing" rather than "generic asset-store bright."
- **Art-style origin:** deliberately anti-realist. *"I just made a square, called it a tree and it kind of stuck."* Simplicity is a mood tool — flat, shape-based forms with no visual noise.
- **Cozy intent, explicit:** inspired by Minecraft/Terraria/Starbound — *"there's just such a nice mood, especially paired with the serene music… a great way to relax."* Built in **GameMaker** (per Wikipedia). No disclosed day/night ramp or bloom specifics — APICO's coziness is **palette + shape simplicity + music**, not lighting tech. Takeaway: you can buy a lot of "cozy" purely by hue selection before touching a shader.

### Stardew Valley — color-shifted shadows + tint-overlay day/night
- **Palette/shading:** limited palettes, dithering, flat perspective, bright-but-controlled colors. ConcernedApe's signature trick: **shadows use a different HUE, not just a darker value** — shadow color is shifted toward a new color, which is exactly why it feels warm/organic instead of muddy.
- **Day/night (source 8):** vanilla implements it as a **screen-wide ambient color overlay lerped over time** — "like tinted sunglasses" — transitioning *toward a night-time ambient color scheme* rather than hard-cutting to black. Sunset timing and **sunset hue vary by season** (pink spring, golden summer, pale winter). This is conceptually identical to what you already have with a Global Light2D color ramp — validation that your architecture is right; the gap is elsewhere (see below).

### Spiritfarer — the "atmospheric" gold standard, and it's mostly lighting + pastels
- **Palette intent (source 3):** *"a simple style, yet not too much. Somewhat soothing for the eye, with a flare of melancholic nostalgia."* Soft, pastel landscapes; a deliberate *emotional* color target (nostalgia), not just "pretty."
- **Lighting is explicitly the mood lever (sources 2,3):** *"The way lighting is handled, and the balance between all the different elements, is crucial to get the right kind of mood."* They hired their **first dedicated technical artist (David Bergamin)** specifically to *"set beautiful lighting ambiances with dynamic effects directly in the game engine."* That's the headline: a hand-drawn game still leaned on **in-engine dynamic lighting/post effects** for its atmosphere.
- **Weather/nature as mood:** water, fog, rain, wind, sunrise/sunset are deployed to evoke *"a dreamy, beautiful and nostalgic place."* Atmosphere = layered environmental FX + lighting, not just backgrounds.

### Dave the Diver — depth via 2.5D + "never the same" ambience
- **Technique (source 5):** flat 2D pixels underwater *"was not that aesthetically pleasing… so we started to mix 3D objects in the background. It felt much deeper and looked unique."* Pixel characters over low-poly 3D environments = perceived depth/height/width on a 2D axis.
- **Mood:** deliberately variable — *"if you dive underwater in real life, it's always different based on weather, current, and fish locations… places that always felt fresh"* — achieved with a *"closed but changing environment."* Draws on ABZÛ/Subnautica for beauty-plus-tension. Source disclosed **no shader/lighting specifics** — so treat their bioluminescence as an art-direction goal, not a documented recipe.

### Moonlighter — the important counter-example: NO dynamic lighting
- **Rendering (source 6):** built in Unity with *"custom shaders, mesh deformations that look like pixel art, camera post-processing."*
- **Lighting:** *"There is no dynamic lighting… we use the superposition of sprites to give the impression of light (for example in Rynoka during the night)."* i.e. night ambiance was **faked with stacked/layered semi-transparent sprites** (glow blobs, shadow overlays) rather than real lights. Inspiration: 16-bit + Hyper Light Drifter + Studio Ghibli. Lesson: **camera post-processing (grading/bloom) + additive sprite layers can substitute for a real light rig** and still look premium.

### Core Keeper — darkness-first so glow reads
- Design premise is **start in the dark**; torches, glowing plants and **glowing crystals** are the light. Community/patch notes describe a reworked **indirect (bounce) lighting** system where light "spreads further and takes surface color into account → softer, more saturated dynamic lighting." The cozy trick: **contrast** — a mostly-dark screen makes every emissive object precious and makes glow POP for free.

## The in-depth technical study: how glow is actually made to POP in URP 2D

Two sources give the concrete recipe. This is the part most relevant to you.

**A. Bloom + emission pipeline (source 4):**
1. URP 2D Renderer; **enable HDR** in Quality settings (bloom keys off pixels that exceed a brightness threshold — without HDR, colors clamp at 1.0 and nothing "over-brights").
2. Global Volume → **Bloom** override: `Threshold ≈ 1.0`, `Intensity ≈ 0.5` (tune to taste). Bloom is a camera-lens effect that only triggers where light concentrates.
3. **Emission maps** (grayscale: black=no glow, white=max) tell bloom *where* to glow, decoupled from ambient light.
4. Assign the emission as a **Secondary Texture (`_Emission`)** on the sprite, and author a **Sprite Unlit Graph**: sample main tex + sample emission, **Add** them; expose an **HDR Color** property, multiply into emission before the add → glow color/strength you can push past 1.0 so bloom always catches it.
5. Then add real lights: **Global Light2D ≈ 0.65** for ambient, plus a **Point Light2D on each glowing object** (intensity ≈ 0.51, falloff ≈ 0.765) so the object also carves a localized halo into darkness. **Bloom (screen bright-pass) + emission (over-bright core) + Point Light2D (halo in the dark) = the three layers that together make lanterns/fireflies/crystals feel like they glow.** You currently have none of these three on your glow entities.

**B. Stylized ramp lighting alternative (source 7, LutLight2D):** instead of light *multiplying* the sprite (which muddies pixel-art palettes), it **replaces each palette color via a per-color gradient ramp/LUT** driven by the URP 2D lighting texture's brightness. It preserves palette identity, gives you hand-authored dawn→night shade ramps per color, and supports tricks like "outline visible only in darkness." Optional, but it's the cleanest way to get *stylized, palette-true* day/night shading rather than a flat darkening tint.

## What makes these feel "cozy/atmospheric" vs generic (the pattern)
1. **Deliberately muted/hue-shifted palettes**, not default-bright (APICO mint/purple-brown; Spiritfarer pastel-melancholic; Stardew color-shifted shadows).
2. **Time-of-day tint lerped toward a *colored* night ambient**, with hue shifts (warm dusk / cool night / pink dawn) and seasonal variation — not a plain brightness fade.
3. **Emissive light sources that POP against darkness** via bloom + real localized lights; contrast is the multiplier (Core Keeper's dark-first design).
4. **Camera post-processing / grading unifies the frame** — even the games that avoided dynamic lights (Moonlighter) leaned on post + layered sprites; Spiritfarer hired a tech artist *specifically* for in-engine lighting ambiances.
5. **Layered environmental FX for mood** — fog, rain, wind, god-rays, dust motes, sunrise/sunset (Spiritfarer), and "always changing" variability (Dave the Diver) so a scene never feels static.

## Top concrete, prioritized things WE should adopt (URP 2D; we already have day/night Global Light2D + weather-tint hooks + layered rain)

Our two named gaps — **no bloom/grading** and **glow entities that emit no light** — are exactly the two biggest levers every atmospheric game above pulls. Fix them in this order:

**P0 — Add URP Bloom + enable HDR (biggest ROI, ~an afternoon).** Global Volume with Bloom (`Threshold ~0.9–1.0`, `Intensity ~0.4–0.6`). This single change is what makes lanterns/fireflies/crystals read as *glowing* instead of flat. Directly closes gap #1. (Source 4)

**P1 — Make glow entities actually emit light.** Attach a **Point/Freeform Light2D** to every lantern/firefly/crystal (intensity ~0.5, falloff ~0.76) so they carve a warm halo into your existing day/night darkness. This is the specific fix for "glow entities that emit no light," and it's what makes them matter at night. (Source 4)

**P2 — Author emission on glow sprites** (Secondary `_Emission` texture + Sprite Unlit/Lit Graph, Add node, **HDR color** property). This over-brights the glowing core so it exceeds the bloom threshold **even in daylight**, and decouples "what glows" from "how dark it is." (Source 4)

**P3 — Add a post-processing color grade tied to time-of-day AND weather.** Even without full grading today, add a Volume with **Color Adjustments** (post-exposure, saturation, contrast) and optionally **Color Lookup (LUT)**. Drive it from your existing tick clock + **weather-tint hooks**: rain = desaturate + cool + slightly lower bloom + duller exposure; dusk = warm/golden; night = cool blue-purple. This is the "cozy secret sauce" Spiritfarer/Moonlighter relied on, and it extends your weather hooks beyond a flat tint-multiply. (Sources 3, 6)

**P4 — Palette/ramp discipline.** Make the day/night ramp **hue-shift**, not just brighten/darken: pink dawn → neutral-warm day → golden dusk → blue-violet night, with per-season sunset hues (Stardew). Adopt **color-shifted shadows** (ConcernedApe: shadow = different hue, not darker value). Pick soothing muted anchors like APICO's mint-green/purple-brown rather than saturated primaries. Cheap, no code. (Sources 1, 8)

**P5 — Atmospheric layers (cheap "superposition of sprites").** Add additive **god-ray/light-shaft, dust-mote, and firefly particle layers** and a soft fog layer — Moonlighter faked its entire night lighting this way, and it feeds your new bloom for free. Extend the rain system so heavy rain also spawns a desaturating fog/overlay layer. (Sources 3, 6)

**P6 (optional) — Stylized ramp lighting (LutLight2D-style).** If plain Light2D multiply muddies your pixel palette, move to **per-color gradient LUT lighting** so day/night shading stays palette-true. Bigger lift; do it only if P0–P3 don't get the look you want. (Source 7)

Bottom line: you already have the *hard* part (tick-driven colored day/night + weather hooks + rain). The three cheap additions that will close ~80% of the gap to these games are **Bloom (P0) + Light2D-on-glow-entities (P1) + emission sprites (P2)** — that trio is precisely the mechanism the Unity breakdown says makes glow POP, and it's the exact thing your current setup is missing.
