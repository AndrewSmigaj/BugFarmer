# Candidate bugs for thin zones, round 2 (2026-10-03)

These are tentative suggestions for the owner, not decisions or game data.

How this was checked: each candidate was read on its Wikipedia pages (English, and German or Polish where they say
more) and on other pages where named (Animal Diversity Web, university extension pages, BWARS, open-access papers),
all fetched on 2026-10-03; only the pages listed under "Sources" were used, and anything they did not state is listed
under "Not confirmed". Facts stated only for a genus or family are labelled as such. The game's rules used for the fit
checks come from `docs/gdd/00_premise.md`: fish and people survive; mammals, birds, reptiles and amphibians are gone;
there are no worms or leeches, and snails go by the same rule. So a life that needs dung, the blood of mammals or
birds, bat guano, frogs, tadpoles, lizards, snails, earthworms or rodent burrows is flagged; occasional prey of such
animals is fine when insects carry the diet. The game has no seasons, so anything that only works with a season is
flagged too. The only two ants are the black garden ant (Lasius niger) and the red imported fire ant (Solenopsis
invicta), so no other ant is proposed, and a guest of another ant genus fits loosely at best. Species the owner cut,
species already on the roster, and species covered in round 1 (`thin-zone-candidates.md`) are not proposed again.

## 1. Ant Colony + Queen (the black garden ant's own colony, deep underground)

Round 1 offered the ant cricket and the ant-nest club beetle. The rove beetles (Lomechusa, Pella) and ant-nest
beetles (Paussinae) named in the brief were checked too, with a "highwayman" beetle found on the way; most of them
belong to other ants, as the "fails" entries show.

### 1a. Ant-nest case beetle — Clytra laeviuscula — fits
- **Which ant:** a 2015 review of case-bearing leaf beetles lists ten host ants for it, in five genera: Tapinoma
  erraticum, a Camponotus, Formica sanguinea, F. pratensis, F. fusca, **Lasius niger**, "Lasius alienonigra",
  L. alienus, L. neglectus and Aphaenogaster subterranea (Agrain et al. 2015, Table 1; the Lasius niger records are
  from Wasmann 1894 and Erber 1988). The German page speaks only of wood ants; the English page names no host. So the
  black garden ant is a recorded host, one of many.
- **Eats:** the larvae "feed on the waste and other detritus left by the ants, on their eggs and on their larvae"
  (English page); the German page lists the ant brood's food, dead ants, moulted skins, leftovers of the ants' prey,
  and the ant brood itself. Adults eat the leaves, flowers and pollen of oak, willow, poplar, birch and ash, and the
  pollen of rose, bistort and oxeye daisy (English page); hawthorn, ash and willow (German page).
- **Breeds:** the female wraps each egg with her hind legs in a ball of her own droppings, about 2 mm across, and
  drops it near an anthill; the ants carry it in. The German page says the ants take the dried balls for building
  material, and that the eggs and larvae give off scents that imitate the pheromone of the ants' own eggs. The larva
  lives in a cone-shaped case made of its own droppings, pupates inside it, and the young adult stays in the nest
  until its shell has hardened. Development in the nest takes about two years (English page), two to four (German).
- **Behaviour worth playing:** (1) the ants themselves carry its eggs into the nest; (2) the larva pulls back into its
  case when an ant touches it, so the ants can't get at it (German page); (3) the adult plays dead when threatened,
  and its warning colours (orange-red wing cases with black shoulder spots and a black band) mean it really is
  unpalatable (German page).
- **Danger to people:** not stated.
- **Habitat and fit:** sunny forest edges, dry slopes, dry grassland, wet forests, floodplains, near-natural gardens
  and parks; nearly all of Europe except the far north, Ireland, Portugal and Greece (German page), east across the
  Palearctic and in the Near East (English page). Fits the Ant Colony: the larvae in the brood and refuse chambers,
  the adults on shrubs and trees above.
- **Needs gone animals?** No. The case is the beetle's own droppings, not dung.
- **Adds:** a lodger the ants carry in themselves, whose cased larvae eat the colony's rubbish and some of its brood
  for years; an ant rancher finds the little cases in the chambers, and the adults on the willows above tie the
  surface to the nest.
- **Size:** 7.5–11.5 mm long, about 4 mm wide (English page); 7–11 mm (German page).
- **Season note:** adults are seen from May to August; the game has no seasons.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clytra_laeviuscula ; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clytra_laeviuscula ;
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4714338/ (Agrain et al. 2015, ZooKeys)
- **Not confirmed:** how often it lives with the black garden ant compared with wood ants; how much brood one larva
  eats; whether the ants ever attack it; an English common name (none was given; "ant-nest case beetle" is a plain
  description, from the German "Ameisensackkäfer").

### 1b. Ant-nest hoverfly — Microdon analis — fits
- **Which ant:** its larvae "are believed to prey on the eggs and larvae of a number of different ant species, notably
  Lasius niger and the Formica rufa group" (English page). Its relative Microdon mutabilis is recorded mainly with
  Formica lemani, and also with Formica fusca, Lasius niger and Myrmica ruginodis.
- **Eats:** larvae: ant eggs and larvae (as "believed" above). For the genus: "Some species actively feed on ant
  larvae in the colony, others are speculated to be scavengers." Adults "are not known to visit flowers to feed but
  settle on foliage."
- **Breeds:** not stated for M. analis. For M. mutabilis: the female lays batches of 3–6 eggs at the nest entrance,
  the stage most at risk of rejection by the ants; the larvae hatch after 10–14 days and eat ant larvae and pupae for
  about 1–2 years; the pupa takes about 14 days; adults live less than a week, and males wait at the nest entrance to
  mate with females that have only just come out. Its larvae copy the colony's recognition scents.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** (1) the larvae are dome-shaped, heavily armoured and "look like stout little slugs";
  they are slow and live "very deep in ant colonies" (genus page): a fly that is not what it seems; (2) the adults
  don't hover at flowers like other hoverflies but stay close to the ant nest (genus page); (3) a brood thief that
  smells like the colony (shown for M. mutabilis), so the ants don't throw it out and the rancher has to find it.
- **Danger to people:** not stated.
- **Habitat and fit:** oak, pine, beech, spruce and birch forest with fallen trees, and heathland; from Scandinavia
  to the Mediterranean and from Ireland to the Pacific coast and Mongolia. Fits the Ant Colony's brood chambers.
- **Needs gone animals?** No.
- **Adds:** a real brood thief that looks like a slug (and snails are not in the game, so the shape itself is a
  surprise worth examining), deep in the chambers where the queen's eggs and larvae are kept.
- **Size:** wing length 6.75–8.25 mm (M. analis); M. mutabilis adults are 9–11 mm long.
- **Season note:** flies from May to July; the game has no seasons.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microdon_analis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microdon ;
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microdon_mutabilis
- **Not confirmed:** M. analis's own egg laying, larval time and scent trick (all stated for M. mutabilis only); how
  much brood one larva eats; the host link itself is "believed" on the page; no second source on M. analis was found
  (the Dutch page is a stub, there is no German page, and BWARS's Lasius niger page names no guests).

### 1c. Pella rove beetles — Pella funesta, P. humeralis, P. laticollis — fits loosely: they live with the jet ant
- **Which ant:** the jet ant, Lasius fuliginosus (Stoeffler et al. 2007 for P. funesta and P. humeralis; Staniec et
  al. 2018 for P. laticollis); a Chinese species lives with Lasius spathepus. No page read puts any Pella with the
  black garden ant. Same genus as the game's ant, different species.
- **Eats / breeds / size:** not stated on the pages read.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** (1) its body secretions hold two of the jet ant's own alarm signals: undecane, which
  makes the ants attack, and sulcatone, which makes them panic; with sulcatone added, the ants avoided the spot and
  attacked less, so the beetle "mimics alarm pheromones of their hosts" to fend off attacks (2007 study); (2) P.
  laticollis has glands that change the ants' behaviour, "which enables the beetles to live unmolested in the near
  neighbourhood of the anthill" (2018 study).
- **Danger to people:** not stated.
- **Needs gone animals?** No.
- **Adds:** a beetle that sets off the ants' own panic signal so they scatter instead of attacking, a striking trick,
  but it belongs to an ant the game doesn't have.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pella_(beetle) ; abstract of Stoeffler et al. 2007, J Chem Ecol,
  read through https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:17558536&resultType=core ;
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6305771/ (Staniec et al. 2018, ZooKeys)
- **Not confirmed:** whether Pella beetles hunt the ants or only scavenge; any record with Lasius niger.

### 1d. Also checked for the colony — fail: they belong to other ants
- **Lomechusa rove beetles — fails:** Lomechusoides strumosus was studied from nests of the blood-red ant, Formica
  sanguinea; its larvae "prey on the ant brood" and are fed by the workers, and infested colonies raise far fewer
  winged queens and males (Hölldobler et al. 2018). Lomechusa pubicollis lives with the red wood ants, Formica rufa
  group (Staniec et al. 2018). Neither is recorded with Lasius in the pages read.
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6059432/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6305771/)
- **"Highwayman" beetle, Amphotis marginata — fails:** it waits in shelters along the jet ant's foraging trails and
  begs food from loaded workers by copying their begging signals, taking about a quarter of what an ant carries; when
  attacked it clamps itself flat to the ground. But the authors "never found it in nature with any other ant
  species", and in tests "no food transfer ... could be observed with ... L. niger".
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5546582/ ; Hölldobler and Kwapich 2017, PLoS One)
- **Ant-nest beetles, Paussinae — fails:** the one host named is Pheidole pallidula, whose queen Paussus favieri
  "antennate[s] and interact[s] by stridulation with"; they eat ant eggs, larvae and adults by piercing them, may
  imitate the sounds of an ant queen, and have a bombardier-like spray that is "never used against ants". No Lasius
  or fire ant host is named. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paussinae)
- **Lycaenid butterflies:** the silver-studded blue (round 1) is still the best Lasius niger partner found; the Lasius
  niger, BWARS and German pages name no other butterfly, and no further one was checked.

## 2. Scorpion Rocks (dry, rocky, hot; mining by day, deadly venom, heat)

Round 1 offered the camel spider and an antlion. Round 1 also noted that no darkling beetle with a playable trick on
rock (rather than sand) had been checked; 2a is one.

### 2a. Desert stink beetle ("circus beetle") — Eleodes, e.g. E. obscura — fits
- **Eats:** the genus is "primarily detritivores feeding on plant and animal debris", and some plains species eat
  living plants; E. obscura eats "dead plant material, animal remains, roots, and seeds". The larvae are false
  wireworms (the family's name for them), "feeding on seeds, roots, and subterranean stems", and several Eleodes
  species are crop pests (E. extricatus, E. hispilabris, E. obsoletus, E. opacus, E. suturalis).
- **Breeds:** not stated. Only seven of the 190-odd species have had their larvae described (2014 study).
- **Behaviour worth playing:** (1) the headstand: it stands on its head and squirts or oozes "a concoction of noxious
  defensive chemicals" (mostly quinones) from two reservoirs near the tip of its abdomen, hence the names "stink or
  circus beetles"; the liquid is foul-smelling and distasteful but "not being poisonous", and the headstand is a
  warning display; (2) mostly out at night, some species by day; it is out by day when it is cool and at night in the
  summer heat, and "pick[s] a new shelter for the day" each night, under wood or rocks; (3) flightless and "fairly
  long-lived for insects".
- **Danger to people:** not stated; the spray is described as not poisonous.
- **Habitat and fit:** "arid desert regions", and also forests and grassland; southern Canada to central Mexico, with
  many species along the US–Mexico border (genus page); E. armata lives "in arid environments" of the western United
  States and Mexico. Fits Scorpion Rocks: it hides under the zone's rocks by day, where a miner turning stones would
  find it, and walks the ground at night.
- **Needs gone animals?** No. It sometimes shelters in rodent burrows, but rocks and wood serve. All the predators
  the page names are gone animals (owls, foxes, coyotes, skunks, bats, turtles), so in 2126 its spray would be aimed
  at bugs, and at the player.
- **Adds:** the zone's common, harmless catch with a warning you can see coming: a black beetle that tips up its rear
  and sprays; its larvae eat roots and seeds, so it is also a crop pest for farmers.
- **Size:** about 10–50 mm (genus); E. obscura 23–31 mm.
- **Heat note:** its day-or-night habit follows the heat; the page ties it to autumn, winter and summer, but in a game
  without seasons it can follow the day's heat instead.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleodes ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleodes_obscura ;
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleodes_armata ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4089825/ (Smith et al.
  2014, ZooKeys)
- **Not confirmed:** how it breeds and how many eggs; how many years an adult lives; how far the spray reaches;
  whether it stings people's eyes or skin; a common name for E. obscura.

### 2b. Six-eyed sand spider — Sicarius thomisoides (Chile), or Hexophthalma hahni (southern Africa) — fits
- **Eats:** S. thomisoides preys "on insects and occasionally vertebrates", including a gecko (2020 venom study).
  What H. hahni eats is not stated.
- **Hunts:** it buries itself: H. hahni "spends most of its time flattened against the ground or lightly covered with
  loose soil, depending on stillness and camouflage rather than detection"; its genus can "stay for long periods
  beneath the soil surface", flicking sand over itself with its legs and holding it in place with tufts of
  sickle-shaped hairs. H. hahni's long, widely spaced legs carry it quickly across sand.
- **Breeds:** the egg cocoon is cup-shaped and buried in the sand (genus page).
- **Behaviour worth playing:** (1) it lies buried, so the player sees nothing until it moves; (2) it is out at night
  and "shelter[s] under large and warm rocks during the day" (S. thomisoides); (3) its family "are able to go long
  periods without food or water", and some live up to fifteen years, "some of the longest living spiders".
- **Danger to people:** the family's venom is "highly hemolytic and dermonecrotic", destroying red blood cells and
  causing wounds up to 25 mm across that are slow to heal. The Hexophthalma page reports one confirmed bite (South
  America, 1992, a 17-year-old with a skin-killing wound) and two suspected African bites, one victim losing his arm;
  but "No records of bites in southern Africa have been proven" for H. hahni, and its venom's effect on people is
  unknown. S. thomisoides was "not considered a dangerous spider for humans", and its venom was weaker than the
  Chilean recluse spider's, but the 2020 study concludes it should be treated as dangerous in Chile.
- **Habitat and fit:** S. thomisoides: desert and semi-desert, from the Atacama Desert to central Chile. H. hahni:
  desert, grassland, fynbos, Karoo and savanna, "beneath stones in sandy areas, buried in sand at the entrances of
  animal burrows and at the base of rock overhangs"; Namibia, South Africa and Zimbabwe. Fits Scorpion Rocks in the
  sand at the foot of ledges, the antlion's pockets.
- **Needs gone animals?** No. A gecko is occasional prey; insects carry its diet.
- **Adds:** the zone's hidden deadly bite: a spider buried in the sand at the foot of a ledge that the player only
  sees when it moves; it fits the "deadly venom" theme without a third scorpion.
- **Size:** S. thomisoides 12–20 mm body; females 2–3 cm and up to 5–8 cm with legs. Hexophthalma 8–11 mm body.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexophthalma_hahni ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexophthalma ;
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicariidae ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7694614/ (Arán-Sekul et al.
  2020, Toxins)
- **Not confirmed:** what H. hahni eats; how long it can go without food (no figure given); egg numbers; how long
  either species lives (the fifteen years is for "some" of the family); which spider gave the confirmed 1992 bite (the
  page does not say); whether it buries itself to ambush or only to hide (the pages say camouflage and stillness).

### 2c. Jerusalem cricket — Stenopelmatus (the family Stenopelmatidae) — fits loosely: dry country, not desert
- **Eats:** "primarily dead organic matter, but can also eat other insects"; it digs under moist soil "to feed on
  decaying root plants and tubers".
- **Breeds:** each species drums its own mating song by beating its abdomen against the ground; females, often the
  larger, "will often eat their male counterparts after mating". Nymphs moult up to ten times over one to two years,
  then live two to six months as adults.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** (1) the drumming, a sound the player could hear through rock; (2) it hisses by rubbing
  its hind legs against its abdomen and gives off a foul smell; (3) a night-time digger under rocks and wood.
- **Danger to people:** "not venomous", but "capable of inflicting a painful bite".
- **Habitat and fit:** "sand dunes, chaparrals, grasslands, woodlands, and forests hiding underneath debris, such as
  rocks, wood, etc. and digging underground burrows"; the western United States, Mexico and Central America. The
  pages read do not call it a desert animal, so it suits the zone's edges better than its heart.
- **Needs gone animals?** No. Its predators on the page are all gone (foxes, skunks, opossums, owls, kestrels, bats).
- **Adds:** a big, harmless-looking digger that bites and stinks, heard drumming before it is seen.
- **Size:** adults up to 2–3 inches (about 5–7.5 cm).
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem_cricket ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stenopelmatus
- **Not confirmed:** any species in true desert; egg numbers; what share of its food is other insects.

## 3. Deadly Ants outpost (the red imported fire ant's galleries, foraging up into a shallow swamp)

The ant-decapitating fly is already here. Three fire ant guests or enemies that are not ants were checked; all three
are tied to fire ants (Solenopsis) by the sources, and all three fit. The fire ant page itself names its predators
only as groups ("arachnids, birds, and many insects including other ants, dragonflies, earwigs, and beetles"), so no
named spider or beetle hunter of fire ants was found; the roster's dragonflies are on that list.

### 3a. Fire ant scarab — Myrmecaphodius excavaticollis — fits
- **Which ant:** fire ants. A 1982 study found it carries hydrocarbons "acquired from one of its hosts, the ant
  Solenopsis richteri" (the black imported fire ant); moved into colonies of three other Solenopsis species, it took
  on each new host's own hydrocarbons. A 2019 paper names it "the brood predator beetle Myrmecaphodius
  excavaticollis", whose hard shell lets it "withstand initial interactions with Solenopsis invicta Buren workers". A
  1978 paper calls it a "fire ant myrmecophile" (title only).
- **Eats:** ant brood ("brood predator", 2019 paper). Anything else is not stated.
- **Breeds:** not stated.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** (1) it wears the smell of whatever fire ant colony it lives in, and loses it when taken
  out (1982); (2) its armour carries it through the workers' first attacks; the study says this "passive integration
  mechanism is coupled with the beetle's armored exterior to enable it to cope with multiple aggressive hosts"; (3)
  the adults fly: a 1978 paper studied their flight periods (title only, so how and when is not stated).
- **Danger to people:** not stated.
- **Habitat and fit:** inside fire ant nests. Fits the outpost's galleries.
- **Needs gone animals?** Not as far as the pages say (see "Not confirmed").
- **Adds:** an armoured beetle that walks into the fire ants' galleries, takes on their smell and eats their brood: a
  real natural enemy a player could bring in to weaken a fire ant nest.
- **Sources:** abstract of Vander Meer and Wojcik 1982, Science, and titles of Wojcik et al. 1978, read through
  https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:17771039&resultType=core ;
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6336292/ (Pérez-Lachaud et al. 2019, PLoS One) ;
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3271039/ (von Beeren et al. 2011, which cites the 1982 finding)
- **Not confirmed:** its size; its native range and whether it travelled to new countries with the fire ants; how it
  breeds and where its larvae live; whether any stage lives outside ant nests or needs dung (no page read says); how
  much brood it eats. There is no Wikipedia page for it, and the only sources read were two abstracts and single
  sentences in two papers, so this entry is thin. (A 2026 paper on "a myrmecophilous beetle associated with Solenopsis
  invicta in China" had no abstract available, so which beetle it means is not known.)

### 3b. Fire ant parasitoid wasp — Orasema (e.g. O. simplex) — fits
- **Which ant:** "A wasp (Orasema sp.) parasitic on the fire ant, Solenopsis invicta Buren, develops to the adult
  stage within the ant colony" (1989 study). Its subfamily parasitises Pheidole ants, "the fire ant in southern South
  America and the little red fire ant in the Caribbean" (family page). O. simplex is "a parasitoid of Solenopsis fire
  ants in Argentina" (2009), and Orasema wasps "have been considered as potential candidates for biological control of
  imported fire ants in the United States" (2010).
- **Eats:** the larvae are "ectoparasitic on ant pupae" (1989): they first hold onto an ant larva, feed little, and
  finish growing when it turns into a pupa, eating most of it (family page; 2019 paper).
- **Breeds:** the female lays rows of eggs into plant tissue, such as leaves and stems, away from the ant colony;
  each female carries 1,000 to 10,000 eggs. The tiny larvae that hatch search actively and ride foraging ants back to
  the nest, and sometimes ride other insects first (family page).
- **Behaviour worth playing:** (1) the eggs go into plants far from the nest, and the larvae hitch a ride home on the
  fire ants' own foragers; (2) inside, the young wasp takes on the colony's smell just by contact (1989), and the
  colony "grooms and feeds them as if they were part of the ants' brood" (family page); (3) the adults leave the nest
  in the morning, males first (family page).
- **Danger to people:** not stated.
- **Habitat and fit:** fits the outpost well, because the fire ants forage up into the shallow swamp: the swamp's
  plants would be where the wasps lay and where the foragers pick up the larvae, tying the swamp to the galleries.
- **Needs gone animals?** No.
- **Adds:** a real biological control the player can see working: tiny wasps at the swamp plants, larvae riding the
  foragers down, and a colony raising its own enemy; a way to weaken fire ants without fighting them.
- **Size:** "most eucharitids are between 2.0 and 5.4 mm long" (family page).
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucharitidae ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orasema (a stub) ;
  abstract of Vander Meer, Jouvenaz and Wojcik 1989, J Chem Ecol, read through
  https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:24272384&resultType=core ; abstracts of Varone
  and Briano 2009 and Varone, Heraty and Calcaterra 2010, Biological Control (same service, IDs IND44158147 and
  IND44421769) ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6336292/
- **Not confirmed:** which Orasema species lives with S. invicta (the 1989 study says only "Orasema sp."); which
  plants it lays in; how much harm it does to a colony; whether it lives in the United States today.

### 3c. Fire ant twisted-wing parasite — Caenocholax fenyesi — fits
- **Which host:** "larval stage males are endoparasites of Solenopsis invicta, the red imported fire ant"; the
  females "are endoparasites throughout their lifecycle and parasitize members of Orthoptera" (crickets and
  grasshoppers; which ones is not named). It belongs to the twisted-winged parasites (Strepsiptera).
- **Eats:** it lives inside its host; what it feeds on inside is not stated.
- **Breeds:** a female releases "up to 750,000 embryos" as first-stage larvae. A male larva "must reach an ant colony
  to parasitize larvae and pupae, which is accomplished by travelling with a foraging ant"; it bores through the ant
  young's skin and grows inside. Adult males come out and "immediately begin to fly and search for a female"; they
  live "a few hours, but may be up to a few days", and are choosy, ignoring a female already carrying well-grown
  embryos.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** (1) an infected fire ant will "climb to a high perch and assume a gaster flagging
  posture and remain in this position until C. fenyesi emerges": a strange sight a player can learn to read; (2) a
  split life, the males in fire ants and the females in crickets or grasshoppers, which ties the fire ants to the
  game's crickets and locusts; (3) the adult male's life is a few hours' flight.
- **Danger to people:** not stated.
- **Habitat and fit:** the southern United States (Texas, Florida, Louisiana), Mexico, Central and South America.
  Fits the outpost: infected ants standing frozen on the swamp's stems and stones.
- **Needs gone animals?** No.
- **Adds:** an eerie, real sign that a colony is sick, ants frozen on high perches with their abdomens raised, and a
  link between the fire ants and the crickets.
- **Size:** females 1.5–3.9 mm, males 1.5–6 mm.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caenocholax_fenyesi
- **Not confirmed:** which crickets or grasshoppers the females use; whether infected ants can still work; how
  common it is in a colony; only one page was read.

## 4. Underground River, deep (a deep underground river and sunken lake in the dark)

River crabs and glowworms continue here; round 1 suggested the dobsonfly for running water and the blind cave crayfish
for the Underground River. Three cave-water crustaceans were checked.

### 4a. Cave amphipod — Niphargus, e.g. N. ictus of the Frasassi caves — fits
- **What it is:** a genus of over 300 blind, white amphipods (small shrimp-like crustaceans) that "inhabit caves or
  groundwater"; also in mine water, river gravels, wells and springs (German page).
- **Eats:** "mostly predatory on the smaller inhabitants of the gaps in the groundwater body", and also detritus and
  films of bacteria (German page). In the Frasassi caves of central Italy, "a rare example of a freshwater ecosystem
  supported entirely by chemoautotrophy" (bacteria that live on sulfur, not sunlight), N. ictus is "the numerically
  dominant macroorganism" (2010 study).
- **Breeds:** not stated.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** (1) "At least some of the species are highly resistant to starvation and able to
  survive for more than 200 days without food" (English page); (2) N. ictus carries sulfur bacteria (Thiothrix) on its
  shell, which helps it live in sulfide-rich water (2010 study; German page); (3) N. ictus has a "remarkable swimming
  ability" that lets it "move upstream and across large bodies of deep water", while one of its lineages mostly crawls
  and swims poorly when disturbed (2010 study). Cave species grow much bigger than their surface relatives (German).
- **Danger to people:** not stated.
- **Habitat and fit:** cave streams, pools and lakes, groundwater; western Eurasia, from the central Mediterranean
  through central and eastern Europe to Ukraine, and the British Isles. Fits the deep river and the sunken lake.
- **Needs gone animals?** No. It hunts small water animals and grazes bacteria; nothing needs bats or surface animals.
- **Adds:** the deep water's everyday small life, a blind white swimmer that can go more than half a year without
  food; with the sulfur-bacteria species, a dark lake whose food web runs without sunlight, a true deep-world lesson.
- **Size:** about 2 mm in the smallest species to about 35 mm in the largest (English page); up to 30 mm, and many
  groundwater species under 5 mm (German page).
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niphargus ; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niphargus ;
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2896373/ (Flot et al. 2010, BMC Evol Biol) ;
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4407961/ (Fišer et al. 2015, PLoS One)
- **Not confirmed:** how it breeds and how long it lives; what N. ictus itself eats (the 2010 study gives the cave's
  food base, not its diet); whether it is ever a top hunter; N. ictus's own size (the sizes above are for the genus).

### 4b. Kentucky cave shrimp — Palaemonias ganteri — fits, but little is known
- **Eats:** "mainly on sediments that are washed into the cave by the movement of groundwater". Its close relative,
  the Alabama cave shrimp, feeds "by filtering bottom sediments through their mouthparts" in "cave pools with silty
  bottoms".
- **Breeds:** not stated.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** (1) eyeless, and its shell has no colour, so it "is nearly transparent"; (2) it was
  thought extinct after no sightings between May 1979 and February 1981, until cave divers found it "in deep pools of
  streams and in submerged passages", now called "Shrimp pools": a place a diving player could find; (3) it is
  killed by polluted groundwater, so it could show how clean a cave's water is.
- **Danger to people:** not stated.
- **Habitat and fit:** caves and underground streams of the Mammoth Cave region of central Kentucky; endangered, with
  "only several thousand remaining". Fits the deep river and its submerged pools.
- **Needs gone animals?** No. It eats sediment washed in by groundwater; no bat guano is mentioned.
- **Adds:** a glass-clear shrimp in hidden "shrimp pools" that only a diver finds, and a gentle sign of clean water.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kentucky_cave_shrimp ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alabama_cave_shrimp
  (the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service species page had no text, and the National Park Service page was not found)
- **Not confirmed:** its size, breeding, lifespan and behaviour; only one short page covers it.

### 4c. Venomous remipede — Xibalbanus tulumensis — fits loosely: it lives in salt water under fresh
- **Eats / hunts:** it "liquefies the body contents of other crustaceans" with venom; remipedes "catch and shake the
  prey while stabbing it with their venomous maxillules", and also filter-feed with constantly flapping small antennae
  (group page). The venom holds digestive enzymes and a paralysing toxin; in 2007 researchers found front claws "like
  hypodermic needles" with venom reservoirs (species page). Its venom is compared on the page to rattlesnake venom.
- **Breeds:** remipedes are hermaphrodites, and "mating behaviour has never been observed" (group page).
- **Behaviour worth playing:** (1) a blind hunter with needle claws: venom "was believed to be completely absent from
  the approximately 70,000 known species of crustacean until the discovery of X. tulumensis" (species page); (2) it
  swims freely with its many legs as oars, legs beating "even during periods of rest" (group page); (3) it lives in the
  dark below the line where fresh water floats on salt water.
- **Danger to people:** not stated.
- **Habitat and fit:** "anchialine caves on the Yucatán Peninsula", in "cenotes close to the sea, with salty seawater
  lying beneath the fresh water" (species page); remipedes live in coastal groundwater that is salty and poor in
  oxygen (group page). A fit for the sunken lake only if it is deep, coastal and salty at the bottom; a poor fit for a
  freshwater river.
- **Needs gone animals?** No.
- **Adds:** a blind, venomous swimmer in the salty dark at the bottom of the sunken lake: a deep-water danger that is
  real, and strange enough to examine.
- **Size:** 25–30 mm (species page); remipedes range from about 8–9 mm to 45 mm (group page).
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xibalbanus_tulumensis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remipedia
- **Not confirmed:** whether its venom can hurt people; what it eats besides other crustaceans; how it breeds.

## 5. Spider Vale West (far-north surface; gorse heath and dry sunny ground)

Already there: the Carolina wolf spider, Brazilian wandering spider, bold jumping spider, giant centipedes and
harvestmen, with field crickets proposed as prey. None of those spiders builds a catching web. The four spiders named
in the brief were checked, and one more found on the way that matches the heath best (5a).

### 5a. Ladybird spider — Eresus sandaliatus — fits, and is the best habitat match (it is a spider, not a ladybird)
- **Eats:** ground-living arthropods "such as beetles and millipedes" (German page).
- **Hunts:** it digs its own burrow, 50–100 mm deep, and spreads a flat mat of silk with woolly catching threads at
  its mouth; it feels the trapped prey's struggling, bites, often at a leg joint, and drags it into the burrow
  (German page).
- **Breeds:** males leave their webs from April to June and follow the females' scent (German page); the English page
  says males become adult in early September, spend the winter in their webs and look for females in May or June.
  The female lays 35–80 eggs; the young are fed liquid from her mouth, then "the female later seems to digest its own
  body, which leads to her death a few days later. The spiderlings then suck on the mother" (English page). The young
  spend their first winter together before dispersing (German page).
- **Behaviour worth playing:** (1) the burrow and silk mat: a trap on open ground that the player can spot and avoid;
  (2) the mother who becomes her young's first meal, a real and memorable fact; (3) the males' colours: black front,
  bright red-orange back with black spots, a warning pattern (both pages).
- **Danger to people:** a bite causes pain at the wound, fever-like symptoms and a racing heart for one to two hours,
  and sometimes headaches and tenderness for days (German page).
- **Habitat and fit:** "dry and sandy heathland", in sunny spots of low vegetation near shrubs that break the wind;
  the most northern of its genus, from southern Sweden through central Europe to southern England, Austria and
  northern France; very rare in Germany, and endangered in Britain (both pages). Fits a far-north gorse heath almost
  exactly.
- **Needs gone animals?** No. Its prey are beetles and millipedes, both in the game.
- **Adds:** the heath's own spider: a hidden burrow trap in the sun, a warning-coloured male the player sees wandering,
  and a life story the examine text can tell. Note that it is a burrow ambusher, like the trapdoor spider the owner
  cut, though it has no trapdoor and catches on an open silk mat.
- **Size:** males 6–9 mm and females 10–16 mm (English page); males 6–11 mm and females 8–20 mm (German page).
- **Lifespan:** at least four years for the whole life cycle (German page).
- **Season note:** the males' wandering is tied to spring and the young winter together; the game has no seasons.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eresus_sandaliatus ; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eresus_sandaliatus
- **Not confirmed:** whether it lives among gorse in particular (the pages say heath, shrubs and low vegetation); how
  many burrows share a patch of heath.

### 5b. Net-casting spider (ogre-faced spider) — Asianopis subrufa (formerly Deinopis subrufa) — fits
- **Eats:** "a variety of insects – ants, beetles, crickets and other spiders" (species page).
- **Hunts:** the family "catch[es] prey by stretching a web across their front legs before propelling themselves
  forward"; the web stretches to two or three times its relaxed size and tangles whatever it touches (family page).
  In Deinopis, the genus it was moved from: a small rectangular web is held by the long front two pairs of legs and
  dropped over passing prey, aimed using white droppings the spider places on the ground below (genus page).
- **Breeds:** the female makes a globular egg sac 10–12 mm across, light brown or fawn with black specks, holding 100
  to 200 eggs (species page).
- **Behaviour worth playing:** (1) the net cast over prey from above; (2) huge night eyes: the enlarged rear middle eyes
  "gather available light more efficiently than the eyes of cats and owls" (family page), and in Deinopis a large area
  of light-sensitive membrane is "manufactured within the eyes, and destroyed at dawn" each day (genus page); (3) in
  Deinopis, hairs and slits on the legs pick up sounds up to 2 m away (genus page), so it could hear the player coming.
- **Danger to people:** "They are not dangerous to humans" (species page).
- **Habitat and fit:** "often found on a few strands of web in forest, woodland and heathland, or on flat surfaces, for
  example on the outside of houses"; eastern Australia (Queensland to Tasmania) and New Zealand. Fits the gorse heath
  at night: it would take the field crickets.
- **Needs gone animals?** No.
- **Adds:** the vale's night hunter with a net: a spider the player sees hanging motionless with a web held between
  its legs, and the field crickets as its prey.
- **Size:** females about 25 mm in body length, males about 22 mm.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deinopis_subrufa (now titled Asianopis subrufa) ;
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deinopidae ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deinopis
- **Not confirmed:** whether this species aims with droppings, hears at a distance or remakes its eye membrane each
  night (stated for Deinopis); its daytime disguise; how cold a heath it can live on (Tasmania is its coldest range).

### 5c. Pirate spider — Ero furcata — fits loosely: it needs web-building spiders to eat
- **Eats:** only other spiders, especially web-building cobweb spiders (Theridiidae) (German page). Some pirate
  spiders "have been observed to feed on insects as well" (family page).
- **Hunts:** it plucks the threads of another spider's web to fake a trapped insect; when the owner comes, it grabs it
  with its front legs and bites it in a leg in a flash, and the venom at once leaves the prey unable to flee or fight
  (German page). Pirate spiders in general pluck threads to copy "either a trapped insect or a potential mate"
  (family page).
- **Breeds:** a distinctive oval egg sac, 4 mm high and 3 mm wide, hung on a silk stalk 12–15 mm long (German page).
- **Behaviour worth playing:** (1) a spider that tricks other spiders by playing the prey; (2) out at night and active
  all year (German page); (3) the stalked egg sacs as a sign it is about.
- **Danger to people:** none (German page).
- **Habitat and fit:** forests and forest edges, low vegetation near the ground and leaf litter, sometimes moorland and
  salt marsh; across the Palearctic from Europe to Japan (German page). It would need web spiders to hunt, and Spider
  Vale West's spiders build none; the roster's southern black widow is a cobweb spider, the family it prefers.
- **Needs gone animals?** No.
- **Adds:** in a zone of spiders, a spider that eats spiders by faking a fly in their webs.
- **Size:** females 2.5–4.8 mm, males 2.5–3.0 mm (German page); pirate spiders "usually 3 to 7 millimetres" (family).
- **Sources:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ero_furcata ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ero_furcata (a stub) ;
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimetidae
- **Not confirmed:** whether it lives on dry heath (the pages give woods, edges and sometimes moorland); its egg count.

### 5d. Spitting spider — Scytodes thoracica — fits loosely: in the north it lives in houses
- **Eats:** insects, hunted at night (species page names no prey).
- **Hunts:** it creeps up slowly, judges the distance from about 10 mm away, then "spits two silk threads, in 1/600 s,
  in a zigzag manner over the victim. The prey is immediately immobilized"; its silk glands are joined to its venom
  glands, so the threads carry both glue and venom.
- **Breeds:** the female "carries her eggs under her belly in a net of silk" and makes a nursery web for the young.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** (1) the spit, a glue-and-venom net thrown in an instant; (2) a very slow, careful
  stalker; (3) a mother carrying her eggs.
- **Danger to people:** not stated.
- **Habitat and fit:** "frequently found in houses, where it wanders about at night"; under stones and in dark places
  in South Africa's grassland and savanna; in Britain "its range rarely stretches beyond southern England". Native to
  Europe and Asia, introduced widely. Outdoors only where it is warm, so a poor fit for a far-north heath.
- **Needs gone animals?** No.
- **Adds:** a striking hunting move, but the vale's climate is wrong for it.
- **Size:** 3–6 mm.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scytodes_thoracica
- **Not confirmed:** what prey it takes; how far it can spit beyond the 10 mm stated; its bite.

### 5e. Bolas spider — Mastophora — fails for this zone: its lure works only on particular moths
- **Hunts:** adult females swing "a silken thread with one or more sticky drops at the end", rather than throwing it,
  at male moths drawn in by a scent that "mimics the sex pheromones emitted by the female of one or more species of
  moth"; in M. hutchinsoni the scent matches the bristly cutworm moth, about two-thirds of its food, and it stops
  making that scent later in the night when other moths fly (bolas spider page). Juveniles and adult males grab prey
  with their front legs; moth flies (Psychoda) are prominent prey for them.
- **Breeds:** mating in late summer or early autumn; one to three egg cases in temperate species, up to eleven in
  tropical ones; several hundred to several thousand eggs.
- **Behaviour:** by day adult females rest disguised, some "remarkably like a bird dropping".
- **Danger to people:** not stated.
- **Why it fails here:** its lure copies the females of particular moth species, and none of those moths is in the
  game (the roster's moths are the luna, death's-head hawkmoth, silk moth and others, and no page ties any of them to
  a bolas spider); its young need moth flies, which are not in the game; and bolas spiders "do not occur in temperate
  Eurasia" (the Americas, Africa, Australasia and Asia), reaching 45° north in Minnesota. It would not take the field
  crickets proposed as the vale's prey.
- **Size:** females 10–20 mm (19 mm in Mastophora); males under 2 mm.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolas_spider ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mastophora_(spider)
- **Not confirmed:** whether any bolas spider has been found luring a moth on the roster.
