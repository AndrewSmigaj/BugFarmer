# Bug mechanics in games and biology (2026-10-03)

Purpose: gather proven techniques — from real insect biology, shipped games and open-source simulations — for how Bug Farmer's ~58 real bug species should behave, with each technique judged against our limits (hundreds of bugs, a deterministic fixed-point simulation run identically on every player's computer, a cell grid, day/night and weather, no seasons).

**How to read each entry:** source (URL) → the concrete technique → what it costs to run → how it fits our limits. "Not confirmed" marks anything the source did not actually say. Entries were written one at a time as each source was read, so they follow reading order inside each family.

**Our limits, as built (checked in the code on 2026-10-03, so the fit notes rest on fact):** the per-bug simulation runs on every client (`BugFarmerClient/Assets/Scripts/Bugs/`), using a decimal fixed-point number (`FixedPoint.cs`: an int32 scaled by 1000), a counter-based random number generator hashed from (seed, tick, bug, purpose) (`DeterministicRandom.cs`), per-style movement classes (Brownian, Crawling, Darting, Gliding, Centipede), and a zone-wide ledger of "influence events" that every client replays at the same tick (`architecture_swarm_sync.md` §0: player cell enter, swarm target, bug removed, food, blocking cells, smoke-calmed `SWARM_SUBDUED`). Anything new the simulation reads must arrive through that ledger or the join snapshot.

## Summary

- **Recommended ant-trail design (Part 4, candidate B):** trails are scent values on cells, laid only by ants carrying food home (strongest near the food, deposits take the maximum so trails never saturate, a fixed integer fade on fixed ticks) and followed ant by ant with a three-cell sniff and a small per-ant stray chance. The bug stick is a two-step prod sent as one ledger event (first prod: the tapped ants stop and face you; second: they follow you, Reynolds-style, in slots behind you). Followers drop out if you get too far away, if they reach food, or when you release them. A follower that reaches food carries a piece home, laying fresh scent and remembering the spot, so a second trail can grow while most ants stay on the first; exhausted food gets a "no entry" scent and rain washes trails. It scored 17/20 and is the only candidate that meets the owner's example *and* lets a new trail form ant by ant; its main cost is a new piece of shared simulation state (hash, snapshot, late-join test).
- **The most useful techniques overall:** scent on cells with max-blend deposits and integer decay; the three-cell sniff; deliberate imperfect following plus founders' route memory; the two-step prod with recruit-N / release; leader-following with honest drop-out rules; a flight-distance ring per species, which makes herding work (the Strömbom collect-and-drive model); a species × species relationship table plus personality computed from the bug id; response thresholds and age jobs for colonies, with a messenger alarm and smoke that cancels it; a reaction ladder per species (freeze → flee → defend: roll up, play dead, a limited spray); activity curves per species with pre-rain behaviour; a crowding counter with hysteresis for locust swarming; shared flow fields instead of per-bug paths; and simulation detail chosen only from synced data. Part 3 has all 36 with where each came from and how it fits.

**Quotas.** 29 source entries (S1–S29) drawing on 69 documents (Wikipedia articles, open-access papers, game wikis, *Game AI Pro* chapters, developer articles). At least 18 of them were read end to end; for the long ones, every relevant section was read in full, and each entry says which. In addition, 5 open-source programs from 3 repositories were studied at source-code level (C1–C5: an ant-colony simulation, two NetLogo ant models, NetLogo flocking and moths, NetLogo predator–prey, a Unity C# ecosystem).

## Part 1 — Sources read, by mechanic family

### Family 1 — Trails, recruitment and redirection

**S1. SimAnt (Maxis, 1991) — the original manual plus the Wikipedia article.**
Sources: https://archive.org/details/simant_manual (OCR text of the boxed manual, read in the sections on the Yellow Ant, recruitment, behaviour control, castes, scents, hazards) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SimAnt
- **Technique — the player is one ant, the "initiator".** The player controls the Yellow Ant and teaches the colony by example: carrying food home automatically lays a trail; the colony's other ants then "follow the trail and bring back all the food", so the player only has to *show* them the food once.
- **Technique — recruit a fixed number, release them.** The Yellow Ant menu has **Recruit 5**, **Recruit 10** and **Release All**. Recruited ants "rush to the Yellow Ant, and follow it around in an agitated state", attack any enemy near them, and "stay recruited until the Yellow Ant dies or intentionally releases them"; once released they go back to the jobs set in the Behavior Control window (a released group near food picks food up and carries it home). This is almost exactly the owner's "lead 5 ants away" example, shipped in 1991.
- **Technique — three kinds of scent, each a simple field on the ground.** Nest markers (ants follow them home and recognise the enemy colony's), trail markers (laid only by ants carrying food home), alarm scent (laid by the player; ants that touch it "mill around the scented area, and defend it" until it fades — used to "post guards"). **Trail markers are not directional:** an ant picks a direction and turns around if it ends up back home. **Unused trails fade away; rain washes trails and alarm scent away.**
- **Technique — job allocation by one control.** A triangle "joystick" splits the workforce between forage / dig / nurse by percentage; a separate Caste Control sets what fraction of new ants are workers, soldiers, breeders. Not enough nurses → eggs do not hatch.
- **Design lesson from the designer:** the manual says outright "Recruitment in SimAnt is not very realistic … we've simplified it a great deal", and Will Wright later called the game "too simple" and said he failed to convey "how cool ants are" (Wikipedia). The trail idea itself was strong enough that Wright reused it to drive the needs of The Sims.
- **Cost:** tiny. Scents are values per map square; recruited ants are a "follow the leader" state with a leader id.
- **Fit:** very good. A trail value per cell, decaying per tick and wiped by a rain event, is integer arithmetic on our grid. "Recruit N / Release" maps onto a per-bug follow state keyed to a player id, and both the recruit and the release are player actions — so they can travel as ledger events (one event per action, not per bug). Lesson to keep: SimAnt's player could only lead by menu; Bug Farmer's "prod with the bug stick" is a more physical version of the same verb.
- **Not confirmed:** the exact fade rate of trails and the radius from which "Recruit 5" pulls ants — the manual does not give numbers.

**S2. Empires of the Undergrowth (Slug Disco, 2017 early access, 2024 release) — the community wiki's "Basic Mechanics" page plus Wikipedia.**
Sources: https://empires-of-the-undergrowth.fandom.com/wiki/Basic_Mechanics · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empires_of_the_Undergrowth
- **Technique — you never command one ant; you place a scent marker for a group.** Ants from the same brood chamber form a "control group"; up to five control groups go into numbered "pheromone groups" (I–V). The player picks a group and right-clicks the map to drop a **pheromone marker**; "You don't control individual ants. Ants in the respective pheromone group will move toward the marker." A number under the marker shows how many ants are responding.
- **Technique — ants walk in a trail, and some peel off.** "Ants must form a trail while they are moving toward a pheromone marker. A small percentage of ants will stop midway and go back to the nest entrance" (surface) or to the queen (underground). Big groups respond more slowly. Stranded ants that cannot find the way back to the queen die.
- **Technique — automatic local behaviour under the group order.** Ants carry any food in sight back to storage and attack enemies in sight on their own; the player can only toggle "gather", "attack" and "mount" off per group. Nest jobs (dig, build, tend brood) are done automatically in a fixed order by the nest group.
- **Technique — brood as a visible pipeline.** Workers carry eggs from the queen to brood tiles; each brood tile incubates one ant at a time and needs food; the population cap is the number of brood tiles.
- **Technique — readable danger underground.** Hidden creatures in unexcavated ground show as marks: green "?" for allies and docile creatures, red "!" for aggressive ones, mark size = strength.
- **Cost:** the marker is one point per group; ants path to it (the wiki says "the shortest path is always chosen"). Cheap per order; the per-ant cost is ordinary pathing.
- **Fit:** good as a *pattern* — player intent goes in as one marker, the bugs keep their own local rules (grab food, fight, drift home). The "small percentage peel off and go home" rule is a cheap, deterministic way to make a led group feel alive (a per-bug roll from our counter RNG). The difference from Bug Farmer: EotU is an RTS where the player owns the colony; our player is a farmer acting on wild or penned bugs, so markers fit a *trained/owned* colony better than wild ants.
- **Not confirmed:** what share "a small percentage" is; how the game draws or stores the trail the moving ants form.

**S3. Stigmergy and the ant mill — Wikipedia.**
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stigmergy · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ant_mill
- **Technique — the environment is the colony's memory.** Stigmergy (Grassé, 1959): a trace left by one action stimulates the next action by the same or another agent, so very simple agents with no memory and no awareness of each other build complex structures. Ants lay pheromone "on their way back to the nest when they have found food"; the trail network "functions as a shared external memory for the ant colony". Termites build pillars and arches with one rule: carry a mud ball, drop it where nestmates' pheromone is strongest — the bigger the heap, the more attractive it is (positive feedback).
- **Technique — the ant mill, a failure that is also a spectacle.** Army ants that lose the pheromone track start following the ant in front and form a rotating circle, a "death spiral"; one mill was 370 m round and each ant took 2.5 hours per lap (Beebe, 1921). It "has been reproduced in laboratories and in ant colony simulations"; it also happens in processionary caterpillars.
- **Cost:** stigmergy moves cost from agents into the grid: each agent reads and writes a few cells, there is no agent-to-agent messaging.
- **Fit:** excellent in principle — a cell grid of trace values is exactly what our sim can keep identical on every client, since every write comes from the deterministic sim itself. The ant mill is worth knowing two ways: (a) a "follow the bug in front" rule without a trail field will produce it (a risk for any follow-the-leader design), (b) as a rare, deliberate spectacle (processionary caterpillars on the roster could do it on purpose).

**S4. How real ants use (and leave) trails — two open-access *Lasius niger* papers, read in full.**
Sources: Wenig, Bach & Czaczkes 2021, "Hard limits to cognitive flexibility: ants can learn to ignore but not avoid pheromone trails", *J Exp Biol* (doi 10.1242/jeb.242454) — https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/8214833 · Czaczkes, Weichselgartner, Bernadou & Heinze 2016, "The effect of trail pheromone and path confinement on learning of complex routes in the ant *Lasius niger*", *PLoS One* (doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0149720) — https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/4784821.
*Lasius niger* (the black garden ant) is relevant because it is on our roster.
- **Following is strong but never total.** On the first visits to a Y-maze with one arm marked by a strong, realistic trail, only ~6.5% of ants chose the unmarked arm — i.e. about 93% followed the pheromone (2021 paper). But in other species following is much looser: "only 70% of Pharaoh's ants … chose a branch marked by hundreds of nestmates"; only 32% of honey bees that watched a waggle dance found the advertised food (2016 paper's review). Biologists have suggested these "errors" may be tuned for exploration.
- **Memory beats the trail.** "Often, route memories are followed over pheromone trails, if route memories are available." Ants quickly *learned to ignore* a trail that led to punishment (within ~10 visits) — but could never learn to actively *avoid* it, even with electric shock; they solved the maze instead with a side habit ("always choose one side and correct if wrong"). A plain odour (lemon) paired with punishment was avoided after one trial.
- **What an ant does when it steps off the trail:** it "reduce[s] speed, increase[s] sinuosity, perform[s] more U-turns, and reduce[s] pheromone deposition". Ants that made a mistake lay *more* pheromone on the way home (to help the next ant); ants unsure of the way lay *less* on the way out.
- **Negative feedback keeps trails flexible.** Ants lay less pheromone where the trail is already strong and after meeting nestmates (crowding); the 2016 paper says the nestmate-encounter feedback "was found to have a strong colony level effect". *L. niger* always lays more pheromone going home (fed) than going out.
- **Leading one-to-one exists in nature:** "tandem running" — some ants physically lead a naive nestmate to food, sometimes laying trail as they go.
- **Cost:** none in itself; these are rules, not algorithms.
- **Fit — this is the biological backing for the owner's example.** A prodded ant really would (a) leave the trail confused — slower, wiggly, U-turning — and (b) if it is then shown a new place, keep its *own* route memory over the colony trail, and lay trail on the way home that others can follow. So "lead 5 ants to a new food → a second trail grows, the old one keeps most ants" is realistic, not a game cheat. Per-ant state needed: a remembered target cell (2 ints) and a "confused" timer. The 93% / 70% follow rates give a believable per-step "stay on the trail" probability to tune from.
- **Not confirmed:** a decay time (half-life) for *L. niger* trail pheromone — neither paper gives one; the classic laboratory figures are in papers that are not open access, so no number is quoted here.

**S5. Herding a group with one "dog" — Strömbom et al. 2014, "Solving the shepherding problem: heuristics for herding autonomous, interacting agents", *J R Soc Interface*.**
Source: https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/4191104 (read in full, including the model and parameter table)
- **Technique — the agents (sheep):** if the shepherd is farther than `rs`, an agent grazes (stands still, moves randomly with probability 0.05 a step). If closer than `rs`, its new heading = weighted sum of (repulsion from neighbours within `ra`, weight 2) + (attraction to the centre of its `n` nearest neighbours, 1.05) + (repulsion from the shepherd, 1) + inertia (0.5) + noise (0.3), normalised; then it moves one step. The ordering "repulsion > attraction > fear of shepherd > inertia" is chosen deliberately so the flock tightens rather than scatters when the dog comes.
- **Technique — the shepherd: two modes and one rule.** If every agent is within `f(N) = ra·N^(2/3)` of the group's centre → **drive**: go to a point behind the flock, opposite the goal. Otherwise → **collect**: go to a point just behind the agent farthest from the centre. If within `3·ra` of any agent → stop (a dog that comes too close splits the flock). The familiar side-to-side weave of a sheepdog *emerges* from switching modes; it was never coded. It matched GPS data from a real kelpie and 46 sheep.
- **Limits measured:** works reliably when each agent reacts to at least about half of the group; below that the flock splits into stable sub-groups. A "local" shepherd that brings in 20 at a time moved 80% of 201 agents on average and succeeded outright 56% of the time; time to finish grew linearly with distance.
- **Cost:** per agent per step: a neighbour query (n nearest) plus three vector sums — affordable for a few hundred bugs if the neighbour query uses our cell grid (count bugs in the 3×3 cells instead of a true "n nearest").
- **Fit:** strong for herding bugs into pens. The player is the shepherd (no AI needed); the bugs only need the agent half: "flee the player within rs, stay with neighbours, otherwise graze". The paper's ordering of weights is a ready recipe for "herdable" species (beetles, ants, aphids) versus "scattering" species (weaker neighbour attraction → they split and the player must collect them one by one). Deterministic as long as the player's position comes from the ledger (we already have `PLAYER_CELL_ENTER`). The `3·ra` "too close splits the group" rule is a natural skill ceiling for players.
- **Not confirmed:** how the result changes on a grid with cell-resolution player positions (the paper is continuous space).

**S6. Locust crowding and swarm order — Ariel & Ayali 2015, "Locust collective motion and its modeling", *PLoS Comput Biol* (review, read in full).** *(Family 10 — swarm phase changes; filed here because it is the main source for that family.)*
Source: https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/4675544 (doi 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004522)
- **Biology — two phases from one genome, switched by crowding.** Crowding makes the gregarious phase (attracted to other locusts, more active, marches in bands, flies in swarms); isolation makes the solitary phase (avoids other locusts, sedentary, cryptic). The switch is continuous and reversible, and behaviour switches first: solitary nymphs take on most gregarious behaviour within **4–8 hours of crowding**, and **30 minutes** in a small crowd is enough for the change to persist through 24–72 h of isolation. Colour changes only show at the next moult(s). It is positive feedback: gregarious locusts aggregate, which crowds more locusts.
- **Biology — what a marching band looks like.** Bands march in a forenoon and an afternoon bout, depending on temperature, cloud, rain and cold wind (field note: just before noon until two hours before sunset). At any moment as few as **10%** of hoppers are walking — individuals walk, stop, walk ("pause-and-go"). Hoppers that get ahead of the band's edge stop or turn back; the front is dense, density decays toward the back. Locusts eat dead or injured band members (cannibalism mainly of hatchlings, freshly moulted, injured or dead).
- **Technique — the order/disorder switch in one rule (Vicsek / Czirók family).** Each agent takes the average heading of neighbours within a radius, plus noise. Below a critical density the group is disordered; above it, it suddenly aligns and marches — a true phase transition, where noise plays the role of temperature. Variants: keep your own heading with probability α (O'Loan & Evans; Buhl used α = 0.66), or the **pause-and-go** model: a standing agent starts walking at a low rate, jumping to a high rate when more than a threshold number of neighbours are walking; when it starts it aligns with the moving crowd with a probability that rises with how ordered that crowd is.
- **Counting beats averaging.** The review's own judgement: averaging neighbours' headings is "not realistic" because insects react more to abrupt changes; the pause-and-go rule (start walking when *the number of walking neighbours* passes a threshold) may be more realistic — and in its coarse-grained analysis it is the only model where the disordered state stays stable alongside the two marching directions at high density (bands can sit still, then suddenly go).
- **Technique — 3 zones (Aoki 1982 / Reynolds 1987):** repel inside r1, align inside r2, attract inside r3 — the common boids-style rule.
- **Technique — gregarisation as a population field (Topaz et al.):** densities of solitary and gregarious animals convert into each other at rates that depend on local density.
- **Cost:** the alignment rule is one neighbour average per agent per step; the pause-and-go version is cheaper (only walking agents move or are counted, and only 10%+ walk at a time). Phase can be one counter per bug ("crowding exposure") updated from the bug count in its cell neighbourhood.
- **Fit:** excellent, and a showpiece for "zones get more dangerous outward". A per-bug integer "crowding" value that rises when many of its own kind share nearby cells and falls when alone, with hysteresis (fast to rise, slow to fall — the 30-minute/24-hour asymmetry), gives a believable solitary→swarming switch that a player can *cause* by over-stocking a pen. Everything is integer counting on cells, so it is deterministic. Pause-and-go is also a cheap way to make hundreds of bugs look alive while moving only a fraction each tick.
- **Not confirmed:** game-ready numbers for the critical density — the review gives model densities (e.g. ρ = N/L in a ring), not field densities per square metre.

**S7. Pikmin (Nintendo, 2001–2023) — the community wiki's Whistle, Dismiss, Squad, Swarm and Idle pages.**
Sources: https://www.pikminwiki.com/Whistle · https://www.pikminwiki.com/Dismiss · https://www.pikminwiki.com/Squad · https://www.pikminwiki.com/Swarm · https://www.pikminwiki.com/Idle
- **Technique — a growing call circle.** Holding the whistle button grows a circle around the cursor, up to a size and time limit; every idle creature inside joins the leader's squad. Bumping into an idle Pikmin also adds it. The whistle also instantly cures panic states (on fire, drowning, poisoned, stuck in a web).
- **Technique — two-step pull for busy workers (Pikmin 3 Deluxe).** A *short* whistle makes a Pikmin that is carrying, digging or knocking down a gate only **pause, freeze in place and face the leader**, then resume its work after a few seconds; to actually take it off the job the player must whistle *longer* or *a second time* while it is frozen. This protects workers from accidental calls.
- **Technique — leaving the squad.** Squad members drop out when killed or knocked down, when stuck on a wall (they try to path round it, then "stop, look around, and then become idle"), when "too far away from the leader … after some seconds", and in the first two games when distracted by nectar or rubble. **Dismiss** sends the whole squad (or all but one type) a short distance away, sorted into tidy groups by type, idle; in Pikmin 2 a dismissed Pikmin cannot be re-bumped into the squad for ~8.5 s (10 s in Pikmin 4).
- **Technique — idle creatures stay useful.** An idle Pikmin stands still with a glowing halo and bored animations, and joins any task that appears within a radius (carrying, attacking, breaking gates) — so players park idle Pikmin as guards or next to future work.
- **Technique — group steering.** "Swarm" moves the whole squad as a blob toward the stick direction (ellipse lengthens with tilt) without the leader moving; members interact with whatever they touch. Pikmin 3 replaced it with "charge" (everyone rushes a locked-on target).
- **Technique — enemies use the same verbs against you.** The Antenna Beetle makes a sound that captures Pikmin in range to follow *it*; Swarming Sheargrubs flee together if one is attacked; Scornets fly formations commanded by a Scornet Maestro.
- **Cost:** per follower: a leader id, a follow offset and a timer; the call is one radius test per creature when the button is used.
- **Fit — the closest game model for "lead 5 ants away".** (a) The two-step pull maps neatly onto the bug stick: first prod = the ant stops and turns to you (alarm/curiosity), a second prod (or holding) = it follows you. That keeps a stray swipe from wrecking a trail. (b) "Too far for a few seconds → drop out" and "distracted by food → drop out" are the honest ways a led bug escapes; for ants, dropping out should mean "go back to the nearest trail", per S4. (c) The dismiss cool-down stops a player re-grabbing the same bugs every frame. Deterministic if "follow" is set by a ledger event (the prod) and broken by simulated distance and timers. The difference: Pikmin are loyal servants; our bugs are animals, so the follow should be weaker and species-dependent.
- **Not confirmed:** the exact whistle radius and timings in seconds (the wiki gives them in a table this extraction did not keep).

**S8. Lemmings (DMA Design, 1991) and Black & White (Lionhead, 2001) — the Wikipedia articles' gameplay sections, read in full.**
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemmings_(video_game) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_%26_White_(video_game)
- **Lemmings — steer a crowd by changing one member.** Every lemming follows one rule ("walk in one direction ignoring any other lemming in its way (except Blockers), falling off any edges and turning around if it hits an obstacle"). The player cannot steer the crowd directly; they give a limited number of *skills* to individuals. A **Blocker** stands still and every lemming that touches it reverses; Builders, Bashers, Miners and Diggers change the terrain that the rest then walk through. The crowd's route is redirected by editing its environment and by turning a few individuals into fixed obstacles.
- **Black & White — lead one big creature, teach it by reward.** The creature is commanded with three **leashes**: one makes it go to a place (and can be tied to a building to restrict where it roams), one makes it "pay attention when actions are demonstrated", the others nudge it toward kind or cruel acts. It learns by reinforcement — a slap discourages what it just did, a stroke encourages it; "the creature remembers the response to various actions and gradually changes its behaviour". Lionhead built this on a belief–desire–intention model (desires as simple perceptrons, opinions as decision trees). Villagers "become bored with repetitive attempts to impress them" — repeating the same trick loses its effect, forcing variety.
- **Cost:** Lemmings: one state per lemming, no neighbour checks except the Blocker (a cell flag). B&W: one creature with learned weights — not meant for crowds.
- **Fit:** Lemmings' lesson is the most useful for us: **players redirect a stream best by placing things the stream reacts to**, not by commanding each bug. For ants that means a bait, a scent wipe or a blocking stone across the trail cell does more than micromanagement — and a "blocking" cell is something we already sync (`OCCUPANT_BLOCKS_BUGS`). B&W's leash tied to a building is a good model for *penned* large bugs (a tether radius to a post); its stroke/slap learning suits one tamed pet beetle, not herds. The "bored with repetition" rule is a useful anti-exploit idea for bait and smoke (habituation, see S17).
- **Not confirmed:** nothing load-bearing; the B&W AI description is Wikipedia's summary of Lionhead's account.

### Family 2 — Colonies, nests and hives

**S9. Minecraft's bee (Mojang, 2019 "Buzzy Bees" update) — the Minecraft Wiki "Bee" page, behaviour section read in full.**
Source: https://minecraft.wiki/w/Bee
- **Technique — a hive is a home with a capacity and a schedule.** Each hive or nest houses up to 3 bees. Bees range about 22 blocks from their hive for nectar (farther if they must), and **go home at night and when it rains** (even in deserts where it never rains), staying in at least a minimum time. Homeless bees wander (generally north-west) looking for a hive with room. Bees enter a hive from any side but exit only from the front (players build one-way gates from this).
- **Technique — the work loop is visible.** A bee circles a flower for a while to collect nectar, shows nectar spots on its body, drips particles on crops it flies over (each pass has ~5% a tick chance to grow a crop a stage, up to 10 times per load), then flies home; each loaded bee that enters and leaves adds 1 to the hive's honey level (1% chance of 2); at level 5 the player can harvest.
- **Technique — group anger with a clear trigger list.** All nearby bees anger when one is hit (unless killed in one blow), when honey is taken, or when the hive is destroyed; angry bees get red eyes and swarm the player. **A campfire within five blocks below the hive stops the harvest from angering them** — Minecraft's version of the beekeeper's smoker. Anger lasts a random 20–39 s; a bee that stings poisons the player, loses its stinger and dies about a minute later; a bee that never lands a hit calms down when its counter runs out.
- **Technique — lure and breed with a held item.** Bees follow a player holding a flower; if the player stands still the bees settle and rest facing them; feeding two flowers makes them breed (babies grow up faster when fed). Bees can be put on a lead even when angry.
- **Cost:** tiny — a home id, a state (forage/return/in-hive/angry), an anger timer. "Every bee in range angers" is one radius query per trigger.
- **Fit:** almost all of it transfers directly and cheaply: home + range + "go home at night and in rain" (we have day/night and weather), visible loads, a hive fill level, a group-anger event with a timer, and **smoke as the counter to anger** (we already have `SWARM_SUBDUED`). The held-item lure ("tempt") is a cheap, readable herding tool. What to improve on: Minecraft's anger is all-or-nothing per bee; real bees escalate with alarm pheromone (S10), which gives the player warning.
- **Not confirmed:** the in-hive minimum time and baby growth time (the wiki shows them through templates this extraction dropped).

**S10. The honey bee colony and the beekeeper's smoke — Wikipedia's "Western honey bee" (life cycle, castes, behaviour sections), "Swarming (honey bee)" and "Bee smoker", read in full.**
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_honey_bee · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swarming_(honey_bee) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bee_smoker
- **Castes from food, jobs from age.** A female egg becomes a queen or a worker depending only on diet (royal jelly throughout vs switched to "bee bread" after three days). Workers change job with age: days 1–10 clean and feed larvae, then build comb, days 16–20 receive and store food, after day 20 forage for life. Drones (males) do nothing but mate. Brood: egg → larva fed by nurses → sealed as pupa after about a week → adult after another week. About 1% of workers die per day; the whole workforce turns over in about four months.
- **The colony replaces its queen for three reasons** (crowded hive → swarm; failing queen → supersedure; sudden death → emergency queens raised from young larvae). Queens control workers with "queen scent".
- **Swarming = the colony's reproduction, with a visible decision.** About two-thirds of the workers leave with the old queen (the same article elsewhere says about half), hang as a cluster on a branch a few metres from the hive, and send 20–50 scouts to look for cavities. Scouts dance for sites; the swarm commits when about 80% of scouts agree or a quorum of 20–30 scouts is at one site, then flies there (up to a kilometre or more) with scouts streaking overhead to lead it. A swarm carries ~3 days of food and is *less* defensive (no brood or honey to guard). Beekeepers catch clusters by shaking them onto a sheet in front of a box — the bees march into the first dark opening.
- **Weather and temperature gate activity.** Bees need a 35 °C body to fly; they are immobile below 7–10 °C and slow above 38 °C; best foraging air is 22–25 °C. A colony absconds (leaves entirely) on water in the hive, heat, fire nearby, food shortage or too much disturbance.
- **Why smoke works.** Smoke masks the alarm pheromones (e.g. isopentyl acetate) released by guards and injured bees, interrupting the colony's defensive response, and "initiates a feeding response in anticipation of possible hive abandonment due to fire". Smoke is of little use on a swarm (no honey stores to gorge on) and on a hanging cluster it *agitates* the bees instead of settling them.
- **Cost:** none in itself; these are rules. Age-based jobs are one age counter per bee.
- **Fit:** (a) *alarm as a spreading signal* → the right model for hive defence is "a sting or a broken hive releases alarm at that spot; alarm spreads to nearby cells and fades; guards inside the alarm turn aggressive" — a scent field like S1's, which also makes **smoke = a field that cancels alarm** in its cells instead of a global calm flag. (b) The *swarm cluster on a branch* is a great catchable moment for a bee-farming game (calm, catchable, a timer before it leaves), and "the hive is full → it swarms" gives players a reason to add room. (c) Age-based job changes give every bee a visible role without a planner. All of it is counters and timers — cheap and deterministic.
- **Not confirmed:** the article gives no figures for how far or how fast alarm pheromone spreads or how long it lasts.

**S11. How colonies split the work without a boss — Wikipedia, "Task allocation and partitioning in social insects" (read in full) and "Polyethism".**
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Task_allocation_and_partitioning_in_social_insects · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyethism
- **Technique — response thresholds.** Each worker has its own threshold for each task's stimulus (waiting time to unload nectar, smell of sick brood, …); the workers with the lowest threshold take the task first. It only produces a sensible split if thresholds *vary* between workers (in bees, from the queen mating many males).
- **Technique — age jobs (temporal polyethism), but flexible.** Young workers work inside (brood, nest), older ones outside (foraging, defence, corpse removal) — so the bugs with the least life left do the riskiest work; injured workers start foraging early. Remove the young workers and foragers revert to nursing.
- **Technique — size castes.** Larval feeding sets adult size; ~1 in 6 ant genera have two distinct castes (minors, majors/"soldiers", some *Pheidole* a third "supersoldier" caste); in bumblebees the largest workers are ~10× the smallest, and large ones forage while small ones tend brood. Honeypot ants turn their largest workers into living food stores ("repletes").
- **Technique — trail networks prune themselves.** Argentine ant super-colonies first lay a dense web of trails between nests, then drop the extra links, ending with a short, efficient network.
- **Cost:** a threshold per bug per task (a few bytes); the stimulus is usually a colony-level number (food stored, brood waiting, alarm level), so each bug compares two integers a few times a second.
- **Fit:** excellent. Response thresholds are the cheapest believable way to make "the colony sends more foragers when food is short, more guards when attacked" happen without a planner, and they are deterministic if each bug's threshold is drawn once from the seeded RNG at birth. Age jobs and size castes give visible variety (bigger ants on the trail, small ones at the nest) at no cost. The trail-pruning fact supports a decaying trail grid: unused links fade on their own.
- **Not confirmed:** the article gives no numbers for thresholds or switching rates.

**S12. Grounded (Obsidian, 2020/2022) — the community wiki's Black Worker Ant, Black Soldier Ant, Aphid and Pets pages, read in full.**
Sources: https://grounded.fandom.com/wiki/Black_Worker_Ant · https://grounded.fandom.com/wiki/Black_Soldier_Ant · https://grounded.fandom.com/wiki/Aphid · https://grounded.fandom.com/wiki/Pets
- **Technique — two castes with two attitudes.** Workers roam, hunt weevils, haul food (crowding round large food to cut it up) and are *neutral* — they "chirp and act curious towards a nearby player" and steal food from the player's storage and roasting spits. Soldiers stay at the anthill, are hostile, and "rush out to defend nearby threatened Worker Ants or to patrol".
- **Technique — call for help that travels.** A worker that is attacked "may run back to their anthills and signal other Black Soldier Ants to invade the area they were attacked"; soldiers then go to investigate that spot (players are told to kill a hurt worker before it gets home). Aggression also triggers when a different ant *sees* one being attacked, or sees the player taking eggs, and lasts until the player runs far enough or the ant is near death (then it flees).
- **Technique — the colony refills its losses.** "If the number of Black Soldier Ants is reduced enough, Black Worker Ants lay Black Ant Eggs in the deeper chambers" — population replacement tied to losses, visible as eggs the player can steal.
- **Technique — prey that flees in a readable way.** Aphids drop honeydew while grazing; a startled aphid squeaks, jumps, flees, and may climb a grass stalk and hang there; it calms down after "several seconds". Ladybugs chase aphids over long distances.
- **Technique — taming by bait, with a catch.** Drop the right slurry on the ground and back off; the creature approaches and eats it slowly, but flees if the player or another bug comes too close; taming is not guaranteed per slurry; if the player is too *far* the slurry is eaten without taming. Tamed pets carry items (10–20 slots), follow, give a buff, have cosmetic "happiness" (an older version let unhappy pets wander off — removed as too strict). One pet at a time.
- **Cost:** per bug a small state machine; the "go tell the soldiers" behaviour is one bug walking home, then a target position handed to the soldiers.
- **Fit:** good, and very close to Bug Farmer's premise. The *messenger* rule (a hurt worker runs home and brings the soldiers back to where it was hurt) is cheap, readable and deterministic — the soldiers' target is just a cell. Bait taming with a "not too close, not too far" band is a good skill test for our bait items. Lesson from their history: harsh upkeep (pets leaving when unhappy) was softened — keep tamed-bug upkeep light.
- **Not confirmed:** numbers (how many soldiers count as "reduced enough", how far "far enough" is).

**S13. Don't Starve (Klei, 2013) — the community wiki's Spider Den, Beefalo, Bee and Killer Bee pages (behaviour, herd, domestication and combat sections read in full).**
Sources: https://dontstarve.fandom.com/wiki/Spider_Den · https://dontstarve.fandom.com/wiki/Beefalo · https://dontstarve.fandom.com/wiki/Bee · https://dontstarve.fandom.com/wiki/Killer_Bee
- **Technique — a nest that grows in tiers and spreads.** A Spider Den grows Tier 1 → 2 → 3 over in-game days (more spiders and tougher "warriors" each tier), lays **sticky webbing** on nearby tiles that slows everything except spiders, and at the end of Tier 3 (once a player has been nearby for 1–2 minutes) turns into a **Spider Queen**; as she emerges she leaves a new Tier 1 den behind only if fewer than 3 other dens or queens are within 60 m (a density cap), and after wandering for a few days she settles into a Tier 1 den herself — so nests spread. Spiders come out at dusk and go home at dawn. One spider respawns per 90 s.
- **Technique — pull a few, not all.** Stepping on the webbing makes **1–3 spiders come out to investigate**; if they find no one within 6 m they go back. Attacking the den (or a spider near it) brings everything out, plus **one bonus spider per nearby player** (capped at 4 / 8) — the game scales the fight to the number of players present. Players deliberately lure 1–3 at a time away from the den's "emergency radius".
- **Technique — off-screen growth is capped (a level-of-detail rule).** A den out of every player's range (80 m) "cannot grow more than 1 stage between each load", however long it was away; baby beefalo spawn only off-screen.
- **Technique — herds as an average.** Up to 12 beefalo form a herd; the **herd centre is the average of member positions, refreshed every 6–8 s**; members more than 20 m out are dropped from the average (but stay in the herd); herds under 4 merge with another within 40 m. Beefalo wander 20 m from the centre by day, 5 m at night. When one is attacked, up to 5 within 30 m help. Calves follow their parent and flee when approached; if the parent dies they walk to where it died.
- **Technique — taming as two meters.** *Domestication* (slow, ~5% a day while fed or ridden; decays after neglect; a player hitting it costs 30%) and *Obedience* (fast: +40% brushing, +10% feeding, −50% a day; below 50% it refuses a rider). How you treat it during taming sets its "tendency" (fighter, fast, calm) — shown on its face. A **bell** keeps a bonded beefalo within 18 m.
- **Technique — bees with a home link.** Bees leave one at a time in the morning, visit flowers, go home at dusk or after 6 flowers; honey comes faster when flowers are near the box. Taking honey angers only the bees *inside* (harvest while they are out and it is safe). A caught bee loses its home link forever and becomes a "homeless" bee that plants new flowers instead. Hives defend their own members only; bees outside help within 30 m, bees inside all pour out; chasers give up after 8 s.
- **Cost:** low — averages refreshed every few seconds, radius checks on triggers, timers. The herd-average trick avoids neighbour searches entirely.
- **Fit:** very strong for a multiplayer sandbox. (a) *Nest tiers + creep + density-capped spreading* is a ready template for dangerous outer-zone nests. (b) *Investigate 1–3, all come out when the nest is hit* is exactly the pull-and-fight rhythm we want around wasp and ant nests, and the **per-player bonus** is a fair multiplayer scaler — deterministic if "players near the nest" comes from ledger player cells. (c) The **herd centre as a periodic average** is the cheapest herding foundation there is: bugs steer toward a number recomputed every few seconds, deterministic in fixed point. (d) Two-meter taming (slow trust + fast obedience) fits penned livestock bugs. (e) Capped off-screen catch-up is a safe pattern for our unwatched zones.
- **Not confirmed:** nothing load-bearing; all numbers are as the wiki states them for the base game and may differ in Don't Starve Together.

### Family 3 — Hunting styles

**S14. Ambush, traps and lures — Wikipedia's "Ambush predator" (read in full), "Antlion" (life cycle + ecology) and "Bolas spider" (prey capture).**
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambush_predator · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antlion · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolas_spider
- **Trapdoor spider — tripwires on cells.** It seals its burrow with a camouflaged silk door and "spins silk fishing lines, or trip wires, that radiate out of the burrow entrance"; prey touching a line makes the silk vibrate and the spider opens the door and strikes. Trade-off named by the article: burrows hide well but restrict vision.
- **Antlion — a pit that does the work.** The larva digs a cone ~5 cm deep, 7.5 cm wide in loose, dry, rain-sheltered sand, steepened to the angle of repose. Prey that steps over the edge slides down; if it climbs, the larva **throws sand** that collapses the wall and brings it back. After feeding it flicks the husk out and re-steepens the pit. Larger larvae dig larger pits and catch larger prey. Larvae can survive months unfed and **play dead for minutes up to an hour when disturbed** (raised survival 20%). The adult is a flimsy flying lacewing-like insect that lives ~25 days.
- **Mimics and lures.** Flower mantises and crab spiders look like flowers and attract pollinators (the orchid mantis "attracts its prey … more effectively than flowers do"). The bolas spider rests disguised as a bird dropping by day and at night **emits a copy of a female moth's sex pheromone**, then swings a sticky ball on a thread at the male moths that come; it switches its scent blend through the night to match which moth species is flying (one before ~10:30 pm, another after 11 pm). Detects prey by wing sound. Eats an unused bolas after ~30 minutes.
- **Strike rules.** Ambushers must detect, judge size, and strike when prey is "in exactly the right place"; the strike is not modifiable once launched. Sheetweb spiders sit for long periods; orb weavers move web sites often (closer to active foragers).
- **Cost:** ambushers are the *cheapest* hunters to simulate: they do not move; each one checks a few cells (its pit, its tripwire cells, its strike radius) per decision tick, or better, the prey's own movement checks "am I on a trap cell?".
- **Fit:** excellent for hundreds of bugs and very readable for players. Trap cells (pit, tripwire lines, web) are static zone data that only change when built or destroyed, so they are easy to keep identical everywhere. The antlion's "fall in, try to climb, get pulled back" is a natural mini-game for a player or a herded bug that steps in. A **lure that attracts one species** (bolas spider) is a clean model for player bait items and for predator lures that pull *specific* prey out of a group. Time-of-night switching fits our day/night clock.
- **Not confirmed:** nothing load-bearing.

**S15. Pursuit hunters and the dragonfly — Wikipedia's "Pursuit predation" (read in full) and "Dragonfly" (behaviour, flight, temperature, feeding sections).**
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pursuit_predation · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragonfly
- **Interception, not chasing.** "Unlike classical pursuit, in which the predator aims for the current position of their prey, dragonflies predict the prey's direction of motion, as in parallel navigation." Success is very high (97% in one account; up to 95% in an enclosure study where success rose with prey density and erratic prey flight caused escapes at low density). Perching dragonflies stake out prey-dense spots and launch only when prey subtends about 1–2°, preferring larger prey even though it escapes more.
- **Territory.** Males hold perches or stretches of sunlit shallow water against rivals for minutes to hours; boundaries use landmarks; some flag ownership with colour (the common whitetail raises its white abdomen like a flag); fights are aerial chases. Some use **motion camouflage** — flying so as to appear stationary to the rival (6 of 15 encounters).
- **Temperature gates flight.** They bask to warm up (vertical perch in the morning), wing-whirr before dawn, perch in shade or do an "obelisk" handstand to avoid overheating.
- **Group pursuit and raids.** Group hunters pace themselves and split off a weak individual; Matabele ants raid termite mounds with soldiers in front and workers butchering and carrying — and take only some termites so the mound recovers; Asian giant hornets raid bee colonies as a team because a lone hornet gets "cooked" by a ball of bees.
- **Prey's answer — the confusion effect.** In big groups of similar-looking prey, predator success drops with group size; an odd-looking individual (white-painted horns in one study) is picked out far more often.
- **Cost:** interception = a few multiplies per hunter per decision (aim at prey position + prey velocity × lead time) — cheap in fixed point; no search beyond the chosen target.
- **Fit:** good. Interception is a one-line upgrade to our existing chase (`MoveToward` the predicted point), and it makes dragonflies feel lethal but fair. "Perch, watch, launch when prey is close and big" is a cheap per-hunter schedule. The confusion effect suggests a lovely rule: a hunter's chance to lock on falls with the size of the prey group *unless* one member is different (hurt, carrying something, marked) — cheap (a count) and gives players a reason to keep herds tight. The "take only some" raid rule matches our ecology goal of oscillating, not crashing, populations.
- **Not confirmed:** the two success figures (97% vs 95%) come from different studies as Wikipedia reports them; neither is needed as a game number.

**S16. Army ants — Wikipedia's "Army ant" (behaviour, foraging and nesting sections read in full).**
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_ant
- **Leaderless group raids instead of scouts.** "Most ant species will send individual scouts to find food sources and later recruit others … army ants dispatch a cooperative, leaderless group of foragers to detect and overwhelm the prey at once." Raids can carry 3,000 prey items an hour; a colony can eat up to 500,000 prey animals a day; columns can be over 20 m wide and 100 m long.
- **Lanes from a pheromone gradient.** Returning ants lay more pheromone, so the trail centre is strongest: outbound ants take the two weaker outer lanes and laden returners take the centre — two-way traffic without collisions. Ants build living scaffolds on slopes over 40°.
- **A colony clock.** Nomadic phase (~15 days: march by day, new bivouac each dusk, feeding larvae) alternates with a stationary phase (2–3 weeks: larvae pupate, the queen swells and lays). Colonies split when large (about every three years); workers follow the queen whose pheromone they prefer; queenless workers merge into another colony within days or backtrack old routes looking for her.
- **Raids flush other animals.** Prey flee the swarm front; birds follow raids to catch them; ant mills happen when ants lose the pheromone track.
- **Cost:** a raid is a moving front, best simulated as a group with a target cell plus individuals near the front; the lane rule is free if outbound and returning ants read the same trail cells with different preferences (outbound prefers "trail but not peak", returners "peak").
- **Fit:** good for a dangerous outer-zone event: a raid front that sweeps a few hundred cells, flushes every other bug ahead of it (a fleeing wave the player can exploit), and then moves on. The nomadic/stationary clock gives the raid a schedule players can learn and plan around. Lanes are a cheap visual polish for any busy trail.
- **Not confirmed:** whether army ants use the same lane rule in every species (the article states it generally).

### Family 4 — Prey defences

**S17. The prey's toolkit — Wikipedia's "Anti-predator adaptation" (read in full) with "Apparent death", "Aposematism", "Deimatic behaviour", "Bombardier beetle" and "Armadillidiidae" (insect parts read in full).**
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-predator_adaptation · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparent_death · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aposematism · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deimatic_behaviour · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombardier_beetle · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armadillidiidae
- **A defence happens at a stage of the attack** — avoid detection → ward off → fight back → escape. This ordering is a ready-made per-species response ladder.
- **Freezing ≠ playing dead.** Freezing comes *early*, before the predator has spotted the prey (stay upright, still, camouflaged — a primary defence). Tonic immobility ("playing dead") comes *after* detection or contact (rigid, changed posture — a secondary defence); the animal stays aware and takes escape chances; most predators lose interest in "dead" prey. It is widespread in arthropods: beetles, moths, mantids, cicadas, crickets, spiders, wasps, bees, pill bugs, ants. In beetles its length is heritable and selected (longer = safer). Wasps can be tipped into it by tapping antennae or abdomen; young fire ants use it (4× more likely to survive a fight) and lose the habit with age; stressed lacewing larvae do it more.
- **Rolling up (conglobation).** Pill bugs roll into a ball when triggered by vibration or pressure; it also saves water. They live in cool, damp, shaded places, mostly active at night, eat decaying plants (and seedlings — a crop pest after rain).
- **Chemical spray with a limited magazine.** The bombardier beetle's spray reaches ~100 °C with a popping sound, can be aimed widely, and its glands hold enough for **about 20 shots**; each shot is ~70 pulses in a fraction of a second. It hunts at night and gathers with others of its kind when not feeding.
- **Warning colours depend on predator memory.** Aposematism works because "a bird that has once experienced a foul-tasting grasshopper will endeavor to avoid a repetition"; warned species are often gregarious, slow and tough, and free to forage in the open. Brighter usually means more toxic (honest). Batesian mimics copy the look without the defence; **deimatic** (startle) displays — mantises flashing coloured wings with a hiss, eyed hawkmoths, peacock butterflies — are bluffs that predators *learn to ignore*, whereas a real warning keeps working after tasting.
- **Safety in numbers:** dilution (per-individual attack rate falls with group size — shown for water striders), selfish herd (move to the middle), improved vigilance (one spooks, all flee), confusion; predator satiation (rare mass emergences). **Escape:** erratic zigzag flight, dropping or diving; autotomy (shed a leg, regrow over moults).
- **Cost:** each defence is a state with a timer; "learned avoidance" is one small per-predator-species memory (or per individual hunter).
- **Fit:** excellent — this is the richest single source for giving 58 species distinct reactions cheaply. Concretely for us: (a) a per-species **reaction ladder** (freeze when the player is first near → flee → defence when touched: roll up / play dead / spray / sting); (b) **play dead** is perfect for the bug stick and catching (a prodded beetle that goes limp for N seconds can be picked up — and "most predators lose interest" means hunters skip it); (c) the **20-shot magazine** gives sprayers a resource the player can exhaust; (d) **warning colours + hunter memory**: a wasp that grabs a bitter ladybird drops it and avoids that species for a while — a cheap per-hunter avoid-list that makes the food web visibly smart; (e) startle displays that hunters (and players' tamed hunters) learn to ignore give "bluff" species a believable decline in protection. All deterministic with timers and the counter RNG.
- **Not confirmed:** typical durations of insect death-feigning in seconds (the article gives none for insects; antlion larvae: minutes to an hour, S14).

Sources: https://nookipedia.com/wiki/Bug · https://nookipedia.com/wiki/Net · https://nookipedia.com/wiki/Wasp · https://nookipedia.com/wiki/Snail · https://nookipedia.com/wiki/Tarantula

(b) **Per-species skittishness** (rarer = shyer) is one number per species. ### Family 5 — Schedules: day, night and weather

**S19. When insects are active — Pellegrino et al. 2013, "Weather forecasting by insects: modified sexual behaviour in response to atmospheric pressure changes", *PLoS One* (read in full); Wikipedia's "Insect thermoregulation" and "Crepuscular animal" (read in full).**
Sources: https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/3788776 (doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0075004) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insect_thermoregulation · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crepuscular_animal
- **Insects act on the storm before it arrives.** A rapid pressure drop (>4 mbar) precedes storms; in the hours before, cucumber beetles became much less active, armyworm moth females stopped "calling" (releasing pheromone from exposed perches), and potato aphids stopped calling at *any* pressure change (they stand on four legs at leaf edges to call, and wind would knock them off). Under a falling barometer, 63% of male beetles near a female skipped courtship and mated at once (5 min vs 16 min). Field note quoted: *Diabrotica* flight drops sharply below 15 °C, above 32 °C, above 2 m/s wind and in rain (≈30% of days).
- **Warm-up before flight.** Large insects shiver their flight muscles (both sets at once, no wing movement) to heat the thorax before take-off; male moths smelling a female warm up *faster* to beat rivals. Butterflies bask (heliotherms). Hot beetles make and roll dung balls faster and win fights over cooler beetles. Japanese honey bees kill a hornet by balling around it and heating to >46 °C.
- **Activity windows.** Crepuscular animals are active at dawn and/or dusk (some also on bright moonlit nights or dull days); reasons are predator avoidance, prey availability, and avoiding midday heat; predators shift their hours when competitors share the daytime.
- **Cost:** zero per bug beyond a schedule lookup: an activity curve per species over the day clock, multiplied by a weather factor.
- **Fit:** excellent and cheap — with one wiring job. Our weather is rain only (v1), and `architecture_weather.md` states that *nothing* in weather or time of day feeds the bug simulation today: rain is applied on receipt (display-only), while the time of day is computed from the synced tick. The rain *schedule*, however, is already decided ahead: at each day rollover the server rolls a 30% chance of one shower at a chosen future tick. So: (a) **pre-rain behaviour** — in the in-game hour before a shower, flying bugs land and hide, callers stop calling, ants close up — becomes possible and deterministic once that scheduled start (and the stop) is published as a frontier-gated ledger event instead of a display message (see Open risks); (b) **per-species activity curves** (diurnal, nocturnal, crepuscular, bimodal) drive spawning and moving rates; (c) **warm-up** — at dawn big fliers sit and "shiver" before they can fly, a short catch window for players; (d) temperature can be faked from time of day until a temperature system exists.
- **Not confirmed:** pressure-sensing thresholds per species (only the three studied).

### Family 8 — Ecosystem and food-web design in games

**S20. Rain World (Videocult, 2017) — the community wiki's "Behavior" (relationships, trackers, reputation, personality) and "Lineage System" pages, read in full.**
Sources: https://rainworld.miraheze.org/wiki/Behavior · https://rainworld.miraheze.org/wiki/Lineage_System
- **Technique — a relationship table instead of hand-written AI per pair.** When a creature notices another, a *RelationshipTracker* assigns a relationship type plus an intensity 0–1: Ignores, Eats, Afraid, Attacks, Uncomfortable (can build up into Attacks), Antagonizes (non-lethal), AggressiveRival, Pack, PlaysWith, StayOutOfWay, SocialDependent (decided by opinion of the player), DoesntTrack. "Eats" even lets a creature track a *dead* target and carry it to its den. Behaviour modules ("trackers") read these: ThreatTracker (Afraid), PreyTracker (Eats/Attacks), **RainTracker (fear of rain)**, InjuryTracker, NoiseTracker (investigate sounds), FriendTracker (tamed lizards follow a friend).
- **Technique — relationships change with what you do.** Intensities move with contact and events (a lizard's "Eats" toward the player rises on contact and after being hit; a scavenger turns from Afraid to Pack after a gift and to Afraid after theft). A hidden per-region, per-community **reputation** (−1…1) shifts starting relationships; kindness raises it, violence lowers it, low overall reputation makes losses bigger.
- **Technique — personality per individual from its id.** Aggression, Bravery, Dominance, Energy, Nervousness, Sympathy are rolled per creature and change behaviour and even looks (dominant scavengers lead the pack, carry more spears, have bigger frills; nervous ones follow the leader closely and startle harder; brave ones investigate noises).
- **Technique — "lineage" dens escalate as you kill.** A den has a chain of stages (e.g. green lizard → 10% chance per kill to be permanently replaced by pink → 50% → blue → 20% → red); empty dens can roll a chance each cycle to gain a creature. A creature the player killed is allowed back at the next cycle only with a fixed chance (1/3; 1/2 in some campaigns) — creatures killed by anything else always return — and it comes back as a new individual with new personality values.
- **Creatures are "realised" near the player and "abstracted" elsewhere** (the wiki mentions abstracted creatures retreating to dens with prey) — a level-of-detail split.
- **Cost:** a table lookup per noticed pair (species × species → type, intensity); personality = a few bytes per creature from its id.
- **Fit:** excellent. A **species × species relationship table** (Eats, Afraid, Ignores, Pack, Rival…) is the cleanest way to define a 58-species food web as *data* that both the Go server and the client read; intensity per pair plus small per-event changes gives memory without per-pair AI code. Personality from the bug id fits our counter RNG exactly (deterministic, no storage — recompute from the id). Lineage dens are a ready pattern for "zones get more dangerous outward" and for over-hunting consequences. Reputation per zone could track how the local bugs regard a player (e.g. a player who keeps smashing nests).
- **Not confirmed:** how Rain World's abstraction layer works in detail (only mentioned in what was read).

**S21. Bees with no bees in them — Stardew Valley's Bee House (Stardew Valley Wiki, read in full) and Apico's bee traits (Apico wiki "Bees", read in full).**
Sources: https://stardewvalleywiki.com/Bee_House · https://apico.fandom.com/wiki/Bees
- **Stardew — fully abstract bees.** A Bee House makes honey every 4 nights outdoors (never indoors). Its flavour comes from **the closest fully grown flower within 5 tiles** in a diamond (60 tiles); ties are broken by a fixed order ("the flower at the horizontal left takes priority, followed by the next clockwise flower"); the flavour is decided at the moment of harvest. Not one bee is simulated.
- **Apico — a bee is a set of traits.** Each queen carries Lifespan, Productivity, Fertility (offspring at the end of her life), Stability (lower = more mutation in hybrid offspring), **Behaviour = the hours she works (diurnal, nocturnal, crepuscular, or "cathemeral" 24/7)** and Climate (temperate, hot, cold). New species come from cross-breeding; hybrids may mutate; species are tiered 1–5.
- **Cost:** zero per bee — a timer and a neighbourhood scan per *box*.
- **Fit:** a key lesson for "hundreds of bugs": **livestock in a box does not need to be simulated as agents.** A hive, an ant farm or a pen can be a counter with traits (population, health, mood, output) and only a handful of *display* bugs flying around it; the agents return only when the player opens it or it swarms. Stardew's fixed tie-break order is exactly the determinism rule we already follow (sorted, fixed order — never "whichever comes first"). Apico's "behaviour = active hours" as a breedable trait is a neat idea for bred giant bugs (a night-working strain). The owner has already chosen not to cut content for cost; this is about where simulation is *worth* spending.
- **Also checked, not usable:** Eco (Strange Loop Games) — its Wikipedia article only says over-harvesting can drive species extinct, and its wiki blocked automated reading; Equilinox's wiki pages were stubs; Terraria's wiki blocked reading. No claims are made from them.

### Family 9 — Cheap, deterministic techniques for many agents

**S22. Craig Reynolds, "Steering Behaviors For Autonomous Characters" (GDC 1999) — read in full.**
Source: https://www.red3d.com/cwr/steer/gdc99/
- **Technique — three layers.** Action selection (what to do) → steering (which way) → locomotion (how the body moves). Steering returns one vector; the "simple vehicle" is a point with velocity, `max_force`, `max_speed`, integrated each step: `steering = truncate(dir, max_force); velocity = truncate(velocity + steering/mass, max_speed); position += velocity`.
- **The behaviours, each a few lines:** seek/flee (desired velocity toward/away from a point minus current velocity); **pursuit/evasion** (seek the predicted position `target + target_velocity·T`, with `T` proportional to distance — "moth buzzing around a light bulb" is plain seek); offset pursuit (fly-bys); **arrival** (slow down inside a radius); obstacle avoidance (keep a cylinder of free space ahead, steer from the nearest intruder); **wander** (keep a steering direction and nudge it randomly each step — "a random walk from one direction to another" — rather than twitchy fresh random forces); **path following** (predict position, project onto the path's spine; correct only if outside the path radius — people drift down a corridor, not rails); containment; **flow-field following** (sample a vector field at the predicted position; artists can paint the field); unaligned collision avoidance; **separation** (sum of repulsions, 1/r weighting), **cohesion** (toward the neighbours' average position), **alignment** (toward neighbours' average velocity) = boids, nine parameters (a weight, a distance and a view angle for each); **leader following** (arrive at a point just *behind* the leader; step sideways out of the leader's path if in front of it; separate from other followers).
- **Technique — combining.** Weighted sum works but components can cancel; alternatives are **priority** (use the first non-zero behaviour: avoid obstacles before evading) and **"prioritized dithering"** (evaluate the top-priority behaviour only with some probability each step, else the next) — and momentum smooths the result, so only one component need be computed per step.
- **Cost:** each behaviour is O(1) except the neighbour behaviours, which need a neighbour query; Reynolds recommends "spatial partitioning or caching" instead of an exhaustive search.
- **Fit:** foundational and fully compatible with fixed point — every formula is adds, multiplies, a normalise (we already have fixed-point `Normalize`/`Sqrt` and no `Atan2`, `architecture_swarm_sync.md` §14.3). Most useful for us: **leader following** is the right motion for "five ants following the player" (aim behind the player, sidestep out of their path, keep apart from each other) — far better than plain seek, which makes followers pile onto the player; **path following with a radius** is exactly "walk along a trail but not on rails"; **flow-field following** is how a trail grid turns into movement; **prioritized dithering** is a cheap way to run many behaviours on hundreds of bugs (one behaviour evaluated per bug per tick, chosen by the counter RNG).
- **Not confirmed:** nothing — this is the primary source.

**S23. Elijah Emerson, "Crowd Pathfinding and Steering Using Flow Field Tiles", *Game AI Pro* (2013), ch. 23 — the Supreme Commander 2 system, read in full.**
Source: http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter23_Crowd_Pathfinding_and_Steering_Using_Flow_Field_Tiles.pdf
- **Technique — three grids per 10×10 sector.** A *cost field* (8-bit: 1–254 = cost to cross, 255 = wall; a shared all-ones field for clear sectors — 50–70% of their maps), an *integration field* (cost-to-goal, filled by a wave front spreading out from the goal like a ripple, Eikonal-style, 4-neighbour), and a *flow field* (8-bit: a direction index to the cheapest of 8 neighbours plus flags). A line-of-sight pass first marks cells that can see the goal (agents there steer straight at it), avoiding diamond-shaped artefacts.
- **Technique — share work.** A* runs only over the coarse portal graph; flow tiles are cached by portal and reused by every unit and every path that crosses that sector ("merging A*" makes sources prefer already-built portals). Walls and buildings mark sectors dirty; rebuilding is time-sliced through a priority queue. "Cost stamps" let placed structures rewrite costs. One cost field per movement type (land, hover). Multiple-goal fields are noted as "perfect for zombies chasing heroes".
- **Technique — steering on the field.** Agents read the direction under them, blend new directions in as they cross cells (smoothing), and are free to be pushed by physics or each other because nothing has to re-path.
- **Cost:** the field is built once per goal per sector and read by any number of agents (one array lookup each); CPU is capped by limiting tiles integrated per tick. "Hundreds to thousands of agents".
- **Fit:** excellent for our grid. A **colony "home field"** (cost-to-nest, built once per nest, rebuilt only when blocking cells change via `OCCUPANT_BLOCKS_BUGS`) lets every ant find home with one lookup and is deterministic because it is computed from synced data with integer costs and a fixed neighbour order. The same machinery gives **"flee to cover" fields** (multi-goal: every shelter is a goal) and **food fields**. A *trail* is different (it is laid by the bugs themselves), but a trail cell grid can be read the same way (gradient = direction). Integer fields, fixed tie-break order → identical on every client.
- **Not confirmed:** nothing load-bearing; numbers are Supreme Commander 2's.

**S24. Michael Ramsey, "A Control-Based Architecture for Animal Behavior", *Game AI Pro* (2013), ch. 44 — from World of Zoo, read in full.** *(Family 7 and 9.)*
Source: http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter44_A_Control-Based_Architecture_for_Animal_Behavior.pdf
- **Problem it solves:** in World of Zoo players rebuild enclosures under the animals' feet "and in some cases will even actively try to 'mess with them'", so pre-scripted behaviours break. Fixed behaviour sequences cannot cope; animals must keep correcting toward a goal.
- **Technique — each drive is a negative-feedback controller** (like cruise control): perceive a value, compare with a reference ("where I want to be"), act on the error. Controllers stack: higher ones set the reference of lower ones.
- **Technique — Hediger's flight zone.** An animal does not flee at the sight of a predator; it moves away only when the threat comes *within a specific distance*, "to recreate the desired space" — defence as continuous spatial assessment. Other controllers: personal space, biological urges (food, water, sleep), spatial orientation.
- **Technique — choose the controller by urgency.** The gosling example has four controllers — stay near mother (priority grows with distance from her; imprinting à la Lorenz), seek food (priority grows with hunger), flee predators (very high, but only when a predator is inside the flight zone), explore (low, always on) — and the most urgent one drives at each moment.
- **Cost:** a handful of comparisons per animal per decision; no planning.
- **Fit:** strong and very cheap. "Flight distance per species" (and per tamed level) is the right primitive for bugs reacting to players and predators: skittish bugs keep a big ring, tame ones a small ring, and herding (S5) emerges because the player pushes on that ring. "Urgency-weighted drives" (hunger, home, safety, curiosity) is the simplest believable action selection for 58 species, and deterministic with integer urgencies and a fixed tie-break. The imprint/follow-mother controller is a ready model for larvae or young following an adult, and for a bug "imprinted" on a player.
- **Not confirmed:** World of Zoo's actual numbers (the chapter gives none).

**S25. Ben Sunshine-Hill, "Phenomenal AI Level-of-Detail Control with the LOD Trader", *Game AI Pro* (2013), ch. 14 — read in full.**
Source: http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter14_Phenomenal_AI_Level-of-Detail_Control_with_the_LOD_Trader.pdf
- **Technique — LOD by "would the player notice?", not by distance.** Three kinds of break in realism: an *unrealistic state* seen at a glance (eating from an empty plate, running into a wall), a *fundamental discontinuity* against the player's memory (a character frozen in place for hours while the player was away, a broken limb healed), and *unrealistic long-term behaviour* noticed only by watching for a while (random wandering instead of purpose). Each has a criticality model: observability × attention; memory × return time; attention × memory × duration — smoothed with exponential moving averages.
- **Technique — trade detail under a budget.** Probabilities of being noticed are handled as `x = −ln(1−p)` so they add; a greedy upgrade/downgrade trader picks per-character, per-feature detail levels that minimise expected noticed breaks within a CPU budget. An "existence" feature replaces the classic simulation bubble (deleting far characters) with a costed choice. **Multiplayer: criticality is summed over every player observing a character.**
- **Measured:** in a free-roaming game with hundreds of characters and 8 controlled features, the trader ran in 57 µs a frame (0.17% of the frame) with 48 bytes per entity; a blinded study found viewers noticed fewer breaks than with distance-based LOD, which had to use thresholds so close that low-quality movement was visible.
- **Cost:** tiny for the decision; the savings come from what it lets you skip.
- **Fit — with a hard caveat for us.** The *categories* are the useful part: bugs out of every player's view may be simplified as long as (1) nothing is visibly wrong when a player arrives, and (2) the state they arrive at is consistent with what the player remembers (the "frozen for hours" failure — the reason Don't Starve caps off-screen catch-up at one stage, S13). But the LOD Trader's *budget-driven* choices must **not** decide anything our shared simulation reads: CPU budgets differ between computers, so two clients would simplify different bugs and diverge. For Bug Farmer, simulation LOD must be a pure function of synced data (e.g. "no player cell within N chunks of this swarm" from the ledger), identical on every client; only *visual* detail (animation, particles, sprite count) may follow each client's own budget.
- **Not confirmed:** the specific game is not named in the chapter.

**S26. Tarn Adams, "Simulation Principles from Dwarf Fortress", *Game AI Pro 2* (2015), ch. 41 — read in full.** *(Family 8.)*
Source: http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter41_Simulation_Principles_from_Dwarf_Fortress.pdf
- **Four principles:** (1) don't over-plan — get something running and iterate, because emergent results can't be predicted; (2) break the system into its basic elements — Dwarf Fortress builds biomes from separate fields (temperature, rainfall, elevation, drainage) instead of painting biomes directly, and "certain problems solve themselves"; (3) don't over-complicate — "operate at the level of what the player sees or one layer below"; (4) base the model on real-world analogues (rain shadows and drainage fixed their maps).
- **Fit:** a sound rule set for the 58-species behaviour sheets: model the *fields* bugs respond to (scent, light, warmth/time, wetness, danger) separately and let species weights combine them, rather than scripting each species' behaviour per place; keep each species to the few variables a player can see or infer. (Note: "iterate" here means design iteration inside the build, consistent with the project's "build the good version once" rule — not a throwaway prototype.)

**S27. Running one simulation on every machine — Bettner & Terrano, "1500 Archers on a 28.8: Network Programming in Age of Empires and Beyond" (2001, read in full) and Glenn Fiedler, "Floating Point Determinism" (2010, read in full).**
Sources: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/1500-archers-on-a-28-8-network-programming-in-age-of-empires-and-beyond · https://gafferongames.com/post/floating_point_determinism/
- **Why lockstep:** sending each unit's position, status, action, facing and damage would have capped Age of Empires at ~250 moving units on a 28.8k modem; instead every PC runs the same simulation from the same commands, executed two 200 ms "communication turns" after they were issued. Turn length adapts to the slowest machine and worst ping (rising fast, settling slowly) because players preferred a *steady* 500 ms delay to a jumpy 80–500 ms one.
- **The hard part is divergence, and ecosystems amplify it:** "A deer slightly out of alignment when the random map was created would forage slightly differently — and minutes later a villager would path a tiny bit off, or miss with his spear and take home no meat." Rules they learned: the code path "must not depend on any local factor (such as having free time, special hardware, or different settings)"; the number of random calls must match on every machine; **cosmetic randomness (e.g. terrain sounds) must not touch the simulation's random generator.** Players issued a command every 1.5–2 s on average (spikes of 3–4 a second); filtering repeated clicks at the same spot cut network load sharply.
- **Determinism pays twice:** Age of Empires 2 turned it into recorded games (replays of commands) — popular with players and "invaluable" as exact bug repros; for the next engine they added per-system sync tags (e.g. one for the random generator) that log file and line, and multiplayer-synced console commands and config flags for testing.
- **Floats:** deterministic only with the same compiler, same instruction set, controlled precision and rounding modes, and care around library calls (Gas Powered Games shipped Supreme Commander this way); transcendental functions differed between AMD and Intel in Battlezone 2; physics libraries whose solver iteration count depends on CPU speed break lockstep. The sources Fiedler collects add: transcendental instructions (`fsin`, `fcos`) and the fast inverse/inverse-square-root instructions differ between implementations; compilers' automatic fused multiply-add and re-association change results; reproducibility needs a deliberate programming method. His conclusion: cross-platform float determinism is possible only with great effort — which is why fixed point is the safer choice.
- **Cost:** network traffic scales with player commands, not with bug count — the reason hundreds of bugs are affordable.
- **Fit:** this is the model Bug Farmer already uses (authority client + ledger + replay, fixed point, counter RNG). Lessons that bear on the new mechanics: (1) **a bug-stick prod is a command** — one ledger event per prod, with the affected bug ids computed deterministically at the event tick (or listed in the event), never "whatever bugs this client saw"; (2) budget prod events like AoE commands and **filter repeats** (spam-prodding the same spot); (3) any budget- or frame-rate-based simplification must stay out of the sim (S25); (4) visual-only randomness (wing flutter, idle twitches) must use a separate generator from the sim's counter RNG — already our rule.
- **Not confirmed:** nothing load-bearing.


### Family 6 — Visible life stages

**S28. Insect life stages — Wikipedia's "Holometabolism" (developmental stages, read in full) and "Hemimetabolism" (read in full); with Pikmin's visible maturity stages (Pikmin wiki "Maturity", read in full) as the game reference.**
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holometabolism · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemimetabolism · https://www.pikminwiki.com/Maturity
- **Two development plans, each with a distinct look.** *Complete* (holometabolous: beetles, flies, bees/wasps/ants, butterflies/moths, antlions): egg → larva (several moults/instars; body types: grub, caterpillar, maggot, wireworm, active "campodeiform"; larvae never have wings) → sometimes a non-feeding but mobile **prepupa** → **pupa** (quiescent, non-feeding, mostly motionless, usually in a protected site or cocoon; *obtect* chrysalis, *exarate* with free legs, *coarctate* inside the old larval skin) → adult (most adults barely grow; some never feed). *Partial* (hemimetabolous: aphids, cicadas, true bugs, grasshoppers/locusts/crickets, mantises, cockroaches, termites, earwigs): egg → **nymph** that looks like a small wingless adult → adult, no pupa. Egg stages are usually a few days; aphid eggs can hatch inside the mother.
- **Pikmin — stages a player can read at a glance and that change play.** Leaf → bud → flower on the stem; flowers move about twice as fast as leaves. Maturity rises with time in the ground (one stage per 2 minutes) or instantly with nectar; it can drop when knocked down in battle (random chance) or from specific enemy attacks. Notable design choices: in Pikmin 1/2/4 buried Pikmin only mature **while the player is in the area**; Pikmin 3 lets them mature overnight; flowers left too long in Pikmin 1/2 wilt back to a leaf.
- **Cost:** a stage index and a timer per bug (or per batch of eggs).
- **Fit:** strong and cheap. Stage = one small integer per bug driving sprite, speed, diet and vulnerability; growth timers in ticks are deterministic. Game value the biology hands us: **larvae and adults of one species can eat different things and live in different places** (the facts files list this per species — e.g. predatory horse-fly larvae, leaf-eating caterpillars of non-feeding moths), so a species can occupy two niches; **pupae are motionless, defenceless and hidden** — the natural *catchable/stealable* stage (a chrysalis or ant cocoon is a pick-up item); nymphs are a cheap stage to art (a small adult). The Pikmin "matures only while a player is near" rule is one of two valid off-screen policies (the other is capped catch-up, S13); either must be decided from synced data (Open risks, item 10).

### Family 7 (continued) — Penning

**S29. Slime Rancher (Monomi Park, 2017/2022) — the wiki's "Corral (Slime Rancher)", "Corral (Slime Rancher 2)", "Slimes" and "Tarr" pages, read in full.**
Sources: https://slimerancher.fandom.com/wiki/Corral_(Slime_Rancher) · https://slimerancher.fandom.com/wiki/Corral_(Slime_Rancher_2) · https://slimerancher.fandom.com/wiki/Slimes · https://slimerancher.fandom.com/wiki/Tarr
- **Technique — pens are upgradeable answers to specific escape and comfort problems.** A corral holds most slimes (some species need a pond or an ash trough instead). Upgrades: *higher walls* ("even the bounciest of slimes will have difficulty escaping"), a *music box* that "greatly reduces slime agitation", an *auto-feeder* (6 food every 3/6/9 hours), an *air net* over the top (blocks a few hits, then recharges), a *plort collector*, a *solar shield*. "All standard farmable slimes are subject to hunger and agitation, and can experience fear."
- **Technique — mixing species in a pen carries a real risk.** A largo slime that eats a plort (the slimes' product) it doesn't produce becomes a **Tarr**: hostile, it eats other slimes and *clones itself* on each kill, makes slimes afraid, grabs them with a tentacle; water kills it (one splash by day, two at night); Tarr despawn after about an in-game hour. Fruit and vegetables touching a Tarr decay instantly.
- **Cost:** per-pen counters (hunger, agitation) and per-pen upgrade flags; the dangerous outbreak is the only agent-heavy part.
- **Fit:** strong for penning giant bugs. The pattern "each escape route has a matching upgrade" (jumpers → taller walls; fliers → a net; bored/agitated → a calming item; hungry → a feeder) gives the player a clear progression for pens, and maps cleanly to our cells (wall height and a roof flag on a pen's cells, an agitation counter per pen or per bug). The Tarr rule is a strong template for a *consequence of careless mixing* in a 2126 bug farm (e.g. predators penned with prey, or crowding a locust pen into the swarming phase, S6).
- **Not confirmed:** the exact agitation rules (what raises it, the escape threshold) — the pages read do not state them.

## Part 2 — Open-source code studied

Five implementations were read at the source level (not the README). For each: what the code actually does, what it costs, and whether it is deterministic as written.

**C1. johnBuffer/AntSimulator (C++/SFML, MIT, ~2.4k GitHub stars) — `world_grid.hpp`, `ant.hpp`, `worker.hpp`, `ant_mode.hpp`, `config.hpp` read in full.**
Source: https://github.com/johnBuffer/AntSimulator (files under `include/simulation/`)
- **Grid of scent per colony.** Each `WorldCell` holds, per colony, `intensity[3]` for three marker kinds (ToHome, ToFood, ToEnemy), a `permanent` flag (nest cells never fade), a `repellent` value, food count, wall flag, and a `wall_dist` from a precomputed distance field.
- **Decay is linear, deposits take the max.** `ColonyCell::update` subtracts `dt` from every intensity each frame (permanent ToHome excepted) — linear evaporation, not a percentage. `addMarker` sets `intensity = max(existing, new)` — deposits do **not** add up, so a trail cannot saturate no matter how many ants walk it.
- **Deposits encode distance from the source.** An ant lays `MARKER_INTENSITY · exp(−coef · internal_clock)` (MARKER_INTENSITY = 8000; coef 0.05), where `internal_clock` resets when it leaves home or finds food — so the marker is strongest next to the source and fades with distance walked. Outbound ants (looking for food) lay *ToHome* markers; homebound ants lay *ToFood* markers. Following "the highest value" therefore climbs toward the source: two crossing gradients, no explicit pathfinding.
- **Sensing by random sampling.** Every 0.25 s an ant samples **32 random points** in a forward cone up to 40 px away (ray-cast against walls), keeps the strongest marker (weighted by wall distance²) and turns to it; with a per-ant `liberty_coef` (0.001–0.01, set at birth) each sample may "break" early — individual ants randomly abandon the search, which keeps exploration alive. Heading noise ±0.02π per update.
- **"No entry" for empty food.** The ant that takes the last food unit switches to `ToHomeNoFood` and lays **repellent** (cooldown up to 128 s; a 300 s repellent at the empty source). Foragers that smell repellent turn randomly with probability 0.3·(1 − trail strength/max) and stop reading markers for a while; foragers also erode trail cells they follow (×0.99 with probability 0.2) so unused trails die faster.
- **Lifetime.** `autonomy` grows each second and refills at the nest; an ant out for more than `max_autonomy` = 300 s dies (lost ants don't wander forever). Default 3,000 ants on a 1920×1080 world.
- **Determinism as written:** no — floats, `expf`, a global RNG, and frame `dt`.
- **Fit:** the strongest single code reference for our trail. Transferable as-is to integers: per-cell `uint16` intensities, linear decay per tick, **max-blend deposits** (no saturation, no overflow), **distance-encoded deposits** (a lookup table instead of `expf`), repellent for exhausted food, per-ant "liberty" drawn once from our counter RNG, and a max-time-away rule. Replace the 32 random samples (RNG-heavy and costly) with a fixed 3–5 cell sniff ahead (see C2).

**C2. NetLogo "Ants" (Wilensky 1997) and "Ant Lines" (Wilensky 1997) — full model code read.**
Source: https://github.com/NetLogo/models — `Sample Models/Biology/Ants.nlogox`, `Ant Lines.nlogox`
- **Ants: one chemical layer on patches (grid cells).** An ant carrying food adds **+60** chemical to its patch each step and climbs a precomputed static **nest-scent** gradient (`200 − distance to nest`) home. Every tick the world runs `diffuse chemical (diffusion-rate/100)` (each patch shares that fraction equally with its 8 neighbours) and then `chemical × (100 − evaporation-rate)/100` (multiplicative evaporation). Defaults: 125 ants, diffusion 50%, evaporation 10%.
- **The three-sensor rule.** A searching ant "sniffs" the patch one step ahead, 45° right and 45° left; if left or right beats ahead it turns 45° that way. It only follows chemical between **0.05 and 2** — below that it can't smell it, above that (very close to a strong source) it ignores it so ants don't jam. Every step adds a wiggle (`rt random 40, lt random 40`). The model notes that the colony exploits the nearest food first, that distant food needs a larger "critical number" of ants to hold a trail against evaporation, and that a finished source's trail disappears on its own, freeing ants for the next.
- **Ant Lines: follow the ant in front.** A leader wiggles toward food; each follower simply faces the ant ahead of it (`face turtle (who − 1)`) and steps; followers leave the nest after a staggered delay. The chain smooths the leader's wiggly path into a clean curve (pursuit curves) — and is the mechanism behind ant mills if the head ever ends up following the tail (S3).
- **Cost:** O(cells) per tick for diffusion + evaporation (one pass over the chemical grid), O(1) per ant (3 cell reads). Ant Lines is O(1) per follower.
- **Determinism as written:** floats and NetLogo's RNG, but the rules themselves are integer-friendly.
- **Fit:** the **3-sensor sniff on cells** is the right per-ant rule for us: 3 array reads, no RNG needed for the choice, and it works directly on our cell grid. Diffusion widens trails into believable "roads" but costs a full grid pass per tick; on our sizes (256×256 zones) a *colony-local* window or a sparse active-cell list keeps it cheap. The "ignore above a ceiling" rule and the "critical number of ants" observation are tuning knobs for how many led ants it takes to found a new trail. Ant Lines shows that a **chain-follow** gives the nicest-looking led column and must be anchored to the player (not to another ant) to avoid mills.

**C3. NetLogo "Flocking" (Wilensky 1998) and "Moths" (Wilensky 2005) — full model code read.**
Source: https://github.com/NetLogo/models — `Sample Models/Biology/Flocking.nlogox`, `Moths.nlogox`
- **Flocking = boids with turn limits and priority.** Each bird finds flockmates within `vision` (5); if the nearest is closer than `minimum-separation` (1) it *only* separates (turn away up to 1.5° a tick); otherwise it aligns (≤5°/tick toward the mates' average heading) and coheres (≤3°/tick toward their centre). Separation overrides the others (priority, not a weighted sum); every turn is capped. 300 birds by default; movement happens in 5 sub-steps per tick.
- **Moths = a precomputed light field plus a handedness.** Each patch stores `light-level` = Σ over lamps of intensity·scale/r² (computed once at setup). A moth faces the brightest neighbouring patch; if the next patch is brighter than its comfort ratio, it turns by a fixed angle in its own **lifelong direction (±1, chosen at birth)** — so moths circle lamps, some clockwise, some anticlockwise; with 1-in-25 chance a tick it flutters randomly (its chance to escape the light); in darkness it flutters randomly.
- **Cost:** flocking is O(neighbours) per bird (NetLogo's `in-radius`); moths are O(1) per moth after the one-time field build.
- **Determinism as written:** floats and `atan`; the moth rule is easy to make integer-only.
- **Fit:** the turn-capped, priority-ordered flocking is a good fit for fixed point if headings are vectors (our sim has no `Atan2`); neighbour search must use our cells. **The moth model is directly usable**: lamps (our lighting system) stamp an integer light field on nearby cells when placed; night fliers climb it and orbit with a per-bug handedness from the counter RNG; a small escape chance per tick keeps lamps from collecting every moth forever. A lamp is a natural *player-built lure* for moth farming.

**C4. NetLogo "Wolf Sheep Predation" (Wilensky 1997) — full model code read.**
Source: https://github.com/NetLogo/models — `Sample Models/Biology/Wolf Sheep Predation.nlogox`
- **Technique — energy budgets and a regrowing resource.** Every animal has `energy`; moving costs 1 a tick; sheep gain 4 by eating a green patch (which turns brown and regrows after a 30-tick countdown); wolves gain 20 by eating one random sheep on their patch; each animal reproduces with a fixed chance per tick (sheep 4%, wolves 5%) and splits its energy with the child; energy below zero = death. The model's own notes: wolves + sheep alone is "ultimately unstable"; adding **grass that regrows on a timer** makes it "generally stable", close to Lotka–Volterra oscillations, and agent-based versions show realistic extinctions that equation models underestimate.
- **Cost:** O(agents) plus one countdown per cell.
- **Determinism:** floats-free in the core rules (integers), RNG for movement/reproduction.
- **Fit:** confirms a rule the ecology already relies on: **predator–prey pairs need a slowly regrowing base resource (plants, rot, dung) to oscillate instead of crashing** — every new hunter species should be checked against what its prey eats and how fast that regrows. Per-cell regrowth countdowns are cheap and deterministic.

**C5. SebLague/Ecosystem-2 (Unity C#, the code behind Sebastian Lague's "Coding Adventure: Simulating an Ecosystem") — `Animal.cs`, `Environment.cs` (sensing and wandering), `Map.cs` read in full.**
Source: https://github.com/SebLague/Ecosystem-2 (`Assets/Scripts/`)
- **Action choice on a tile grid.** Animals move tile to tile and choose an action after each tile (or every 1 s while busy). Hunger and thirst rise linearly (death at 1.0 after 200 s); rule: "eat if hunger ≥ thirst, or if already eating and thirst < 0.7" — a two-drive utility with **hysteresis** so animals don't flip between food and water every step. Food is chosen by sorting visible prey by a preference function (squared distance by default) and taking the first with line of sight; paths are grid paths.
- **Cheap correlated wandering.** `GetNextTileWeighted`: with probability 0.2 keep going straight; otherwise draw 3 random walkable neighbours and take the one best aligned with the current heading — a persistent, natural-looking random walk on a grid with no angles.
- **Spatial buckets.** `Map` splits the world into square regions with a list of entities each; queries visit only regions within view, sorted by distance, with an early-out once the closest found is nearer than the next region's edge. One map per species, plus a precomputed "nearest visible water" per tile.
- **Determinism as written:** no — an unseeded `System.Random` and floats (the spawn layout alone uses a seed).
- **Fit:** the region buckets and per-species maps are exactly the neighbour structure our cells already give us; the **two-drive hysteresis rule** and the **best-of-3-neighbours wander** are tiny, integer-friendly patterns we can adopt directly (with the counter RNG replacing `System.Random`). It also shows what *not* to copy: per-animal A* paths on hundreds of bugs (use shared fields, S23).

## Part 3 — Techniques worth using

Each line: the technique → where it came from → how it fits (and which building block it serves in `architecture_bug_behaviour.md` §3, B1–B14). All of them can be written with integers, our counter random generator and cell lookups.

**Trails, leading and herding (B1, B2, B3, B14)**
1. **Scent on cells with max-blend deposits and integer decay** — AntSimulator (C1), NetLogo Ants (C2), SimAnt (S1). One small integer per cell per colony; a deposit sets `max(old, new)` so trails never saturate; decay subtracts a fixed amount on fixed ticks. The same structure serves trail scent, alarm scent (S10), nest scent and "no entry" repellent.
2. **Distance-encoded deposits** — C1. A homebound ant lays a value that falls with steps walked since the food, so "go to the stronger cell" climbs toward the food with no pathfinding. A lookup table replaces `exp`.
3. **The three-cell sniff** — C2. Read the cell ahead, ahead-left and ahead-right; turn toward the strongest if it beats ahead. Three array reads per decision, no random number needed for the choice.
4. **Imperfect following on purpose** — biology S4 (≈93% follow a strong trail at a fork in *Lasius niger*; 70% in Pharaoh's ants), C1's per-ant "liberty". Each ant gets a fixed "stray chance" from its id; strays explore and find new food, which is what keeps colonies flexible.
5. **"No entry" for exhausted food and rain that washes trails** — C1 (repellent laid by the ant that takes the last piece), SimAnt (rain washes trail and alarm scent, S1). Hooks onto events we already have (`FOOD_CONSUMED` reaching 0) and onto rain once rain becomes a ledger event.
6. **Prod = a two-step command; recruit a fixed number; release** — Pikmin 3 Deluxe (first whistle freezes a worker, a second pulls it, S7), SimAnt's Recruit 5 / Recruit 10 / Release All (S1). One ledger event per prod; the tapped ants are chosen by a fixed rule at the event tick.
7. **Leader following, not seek** — Reynolds (S22): followers arrive at a point just behind the leader, step sideways out of its path, and keep apart. Avoids followers piling onto the player; anchor the chain to the player, never ant-to-ant, to avoid ant mills (S3, C2 Ant Lines).
8. **Honest ways to lose a follower** — Pikmin (S7): too far for a few seconds, stuck, or distracted by food → drop out; a short no-recapture cool-down after release. For ants, "drop out" means "go back to the nearest scent".
9. **A readable "lost" state** — biology S4: off-trail ants slow down, wiggle more, U-turn more, lay less. Gives the player visible feedback that a prod worked.
10. **Founders remember their own route** — biology S4 ("route memories are followed over pheromone trails"). Led ants that found food keep a remembered target cell and re-walk it, laying trail home, so a new trail survives its fragile first minutes.
11. **Flight distance per species** — Hediger via Ramsey (S24), Strömbom's `rs` (S5), Animal Crossing's scare distance and creep (S18). One radius per species (and per tameness level) drives catching, herding and fleeing.
12. **Collect-and-drive herding with tuned group cohesion** — Strömbom et al. (S5). Order the forces "keep apart > stay with neighbours > flee the player > keep heading"; herdable species have strong neighbour attraction, scattering species weak. The player is the sheepdog; no herding AI needed.
13. **Herd centre as a periodic average** — Don't Starve beefalo (S13). Recompute the group's average position every few seconds; members steer toward it; drop far stragglers from the average. Removes neighbour searches.

**Colonies, nests and hives (B5)**
14. **Response thresholds plus age jobs** — S11, S10. Each worker's threshold for each colony stimulus (food stored, brood waiting, alarm) is drawn once at birth; young work inside, old forage. Cheap, visible division of labour without a planner.
15. **Messenger alarm** — Grounded (S12). A hurt worker runs home and the soldiers go to the cell where it was hurt; plus an alarm scent field around a broken nest (S10) that guards read — and **smoke as a field that cancels alarm** in its cells (S10, Minecraft's campfire S9).
16. **Nest tiers, creep and density-capped spreading; pull-a-few vs all-out** — Don't Starve spider dens (S13). Treading on the creep sends 1–3 to investigate; hitting the nest empties it, with a bonus per nearby player (multiplayer scaling from synced player cells).
17. **Lineage dens** — Rain World (S20). A nest whose occupant can be permanently replaced by a tougher species when cleared — escalation for outer zones and a consequence of over-hunting.
18. **Swarm cluster as a catch window** — honey bee swarming (S10). A full hive splits; the cluster hangs nearby for hours, calm, before leaving — the moment a player can catch a colony.

**Hunting and defence (B8, B9, B10)**
19. **Trap cells** — trapdoor spider tripwires, antlion pits, webs (S14). Static cells that prey movement checks; the cheapest hunters to simulate.
20. **Interception pursuit** — dragonflies (S15), Reynolds' pursuit (S22). Aim at prey position + prey velocity × lead time.
21. **Confusion and odd-one-out targeting** — S15, S17. A hunter's lock-on chance falls with prey group size unless one prey is different (hurt, carrying, marked).
22. **A per-species reaction ladder** — S17: freeze (before being spotted) → flee (erratic) → defend when touched (roll up, play dead, spray with a limited ~20-shot "magazine", sting). Play dead is a natural catch moment for the bug stick.
23. **Hunters learn warning colours** — S17. A short per-hunter avoid-list after tasting a bitter bug; bluff displays lose effect with repeats, true warnings don't.

**Schedules, stages and swarms (B4, B6, B13)**
24. **Activity curves per species, pre-rain behaviour, dawn warm-up** — S19, S9, S13, S18, Apico's "behaviour = active hours" (S21). Per-species curve over the day clock times a rain factor; bugs settle in the hour before a scheduled shower.
25. **Stage index + timer per bug, with different niches per stage** — S28. Larvae and adults eat and live differently; pupae are motionless, catchable items; nymphs are small adults.
26. **Crowding counter with hysteresis → swarm phase** — locusts (S6). Rises fast when many of the same species share nearby cells, falls slowly when alone; past a threshold, the group aligns and marches (Vicsek-style alignment). Players can trigger it by over-stocking.
27. **Pause-and-go movement** — locusts (S6). Only a fraction of a group walks at any moment; standing bugs start when enough neighbours walk. Cheaper and more alive than everyone moving every tick.

**Lures, lights, penning and taming (B11, B14)**
28. **Light field with per-bug handedness and an escape chance** — NetLogo Moths (C3). Lamps stamp an integer light value on nearby cells; night fliers climb it and orbit; a small chance per tick lets them leave. A lamp is a moth lure.
29. **Bait with a distance band; held-item lure** — Grounded taming (S12), Minecraft's flower-following bees (S9). Too close scares, too far fails.
30. **Pens with upgrades matched to escape modes, and a cost to careless mixing** — Slime Rancher (S29). Walls vs jumpers, roof vs fliers, calming item vs agitation, feeder vs hunger; the Tarr shows how mixing can backfire.
31. **Two-meter taming** — Don't Starve beefalo (S13): slow trust that decays with neglect + fast obedience that decays daily; how you treat it shapes its temperament.

**Many agents, one shared simulation (all blocks)**
32. **Shared fields instead of per-bug paths** — Supreme Commander 2 flow fields (S23). One cost-to-home field per nest (or to shelter, multi-goal), rebuilt only when blocking cells change; every bug reads one cell.
33. **Species × species relationship table** — Rain World (S20). Eats / afraid / ignores / pack / rival with an intensity, as data both server and client read; personality from the bug id.
34. **Livestock in a box need not be agents** — Stardew Bee House, Apico (S21). A pen or hive is counters plus a few display bugs; agents come out only when opened or when they swarm.
35. **Simulation detail chosen from synced data only** — LOD Trader (S25), Don't Starve's one-stage catch-up cap (S13), Pikmin's "matures only while a player is near" (S28), Age of Empires' "no local factor in the sim" (S27). Simplify unwatched bugs by a rule every client computes the same way — never by each machine's own budget.
36. **Prod hygiene** — Age of Empires (S27): budget player commands, filter repeats at the same spot, keep cosmetic randomness off the simulation's generator.

## Part 4 — The ant trail and the prod-to-redirect mechanic: candidate designs

**The owner's example (the gate every candidate must pass):** ants follow a trail; prodding some with the bug stick makes them veer off it; the player can lead, say, five of them somewhere else while most keep going down the old route. A good design should also let something *follow from* that act — e.g. the led ants founding a new trail if they find food — because that is what real ants do (S4) and what makes the verb worth using.

**What exists today (read in the code, 2026-10-03):** `nakama/modules/world/colony.go` — server-side, per-colony soft memory: scouts breadcrumb their walk, register food sites with a strength (+12 per scout sighting, +8 per worker arrival, minus `SiteDecayPerPass` = 0.75 every 30-tick nest pass — `entities/colony.go`; the comment in `world/colony.go` still says 1.5, the value before a tuning change), recruit workers who march the scout's remembered polyline as `SWARM_SET_TARGET` legs; groups, not individual ants, follow a route. The file's own comment records that scout routes in open terrain are "wander TANGLES" that sent marchers orbiting, fixed by falling back to a straight line when a waypoint makes no progress. There is no bug stick yet (B3: "none"). Per-bug positions live only in the client simulation; the project's standing rule is that per-individual bug behaviour stays on the client.

### The candidates

**A. Keep the route trails; add a per-ant leash.** The server's remembered routes stay the trail. A prod (ledger event) splits the tapped ants into a small group whose movement target is the player; releasing them next to food registers a new site on the server (as a scout sighting would), and recruitment does the rest.

**B. Scent on cells, followed ant by ant (stigmergy), with a two-step prod and leader-following.** Each colony keeps a grid of trail values on cells, written and read by the deterministic client simulation: homebound ants lay distance-encoded scent with max-blending; values decay by a fixed integer step on fixed ticks; outbound ants follow it with the three-cell sniff and a per-ant stray chance. A prod taps ants by a fixed rule; tapped ants leave the trail and follow behind the player; if they reach food they carry a piece home, laying fresh scent and remembering the spot, so a second trail can grow while the first keeps most of the traffic. Exhausted food gets a repellent; rain washes scent.

**C. Leader chain only.** "Recruit 5"-style: prodded ants form a conga line behind the player (each follows the one ahead, the first follows the player) on top of today's route trails; on release they walk back to the nearest route point. Nothing new forms.

**D. A placed scent marker instead of a prod.** The player lays a trail-pheromone item or line (Empires of the Undergrowth's markers, the bolas spider's lure); ants that cross it divert to it with some chance; the marker fades. Synthetic trails really are followed like natural ones (the 2021 study used gland-extract trails, S4).

**E. A flow field per food site.** Each known site gets an integer cost-to-site field (Supreme Commander 2 style, S23), optionally made cheaper along used cells; ants descend it; prodded ants follow the player; a new site gets a new field.

### Scores

Scored 1–5, higher is better on every axis (Cost 5 = cheapest to run; Determinism 5 = easiest to keep identical on every computer and to rebuild on a late join). The scores are judgements from the evidence above, not measurements.

| Candidate | Meets the owner's example? | New trail can form? | Fun | Realism | Cost | Determinism | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Routes + leash | Yes | Only as a server "site" | 3 | 2 | 5 | 4 | 14 |
| **B. Scent cells + two-step prod + leader-follow** | **Yes** | **Yes, ant by ant** | **5** | **5** | **4** | **3** | **17** |
| C. Leader chain only | Yes (literally) | No | 3 | 1 | 5 | 4 | 13 |
| D. Placed marker | No — no prod, no leading | Yes | 3 | 4 | 5 | 5 | 17 (fails the gate) |
| E. Flow field per site | Yes | Yes, as shortest paths | 2 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 11 |

Why the scores: **Fun** — B makes trails visible and contestable (you can steal workers, found a trail to your carcass pile, starve a trail, watch rain reset things), A and C make the prod a relocation with little aftermath, D is strategic but not tactile, E makes ants walk engineered shortest paths. **Realism** — B matches the biology read in S4 (strong but imperfect following, memory over scent, more scent laid on the way home, U-turns when lost, decay, trails dying when food runs out) and the three independent models C1/C2/S1; A's trail is one scout's walk (and tangles in the open, per the code comment); C is what SimAnt's own manual calls "not very realistic"; E's shortest paths are not how ant trails look. **Cost** — B adds one small grid per colony and three cell reads per ant decision; E rebuilds fields; A/C/D reuse existing machinery. **Determinism** — B adds new client simulation state that must enter the state hash and the join snapshot and pass a late-join gate with trails active; A/C/E stay mostly on paths we already sync; D is a placed object, the simplest of all.

### Recommendation: B, with D as an extra tool and A's server role kept for colony-level counts

**Why B wins:** it is the only candidate that satisfies the example *and* produces the real consequence (a second trail that competes with the first), it is the model that three working implementations and the biology agree on, and its running cost is small. Its one real cost is engineering discipline around a new piece of shared state — a known, gated path in this project (the `frontier-sync` recipe; the food registry and hunt dictionaries were added the same way).

**Why the others lose:** A cannot split a trail ant by ant, shows nothing on the ground, and builds on routes the code itself records as tangling in open terrain. C relocates five ants and ends there — no new trail, and a chain risks ant mills. D is a fine *second* tool (a crafted trail lure) but drops the hands-on prod the owner asked for. E makes paths look engineered and costs field rebuilds without adding anything players can read.

**The recommended design in detail** (a proposal to test in the Bug Lab — every number below is a starting point to tune, not a decision):

1. **Trail grid.** Per colony, one `ushort` per cell within the colony's foraging range (stored sparsely: only cells with a value). Kinds: trail-to-food, repellent; the nest's home direction comes from a shared cost-to-nest field (technique 32), not from scent. Homebound ants carrying food write `max(cell, Dmax − k·steps_since_food)`; every N ticks every active cell loses a fixed amount; cells at 0 drop out of the sparse set. All writes happen in bug-id order inside the simulation step; decay runs on fixed tick boundaries.
2. **Following.** At each decision tick (a few times a second; our sim runs 10 ticks a second), an outbound ant on scent does the three-cell sniff (ahead, ahead-left, ahead-right) and turns toward the strongest; with its own stray chance (fixed from its id; start near 5–10%, i.e. roughly the 93% follow rate measured for *Lasius niger* on a strong trail; Pharaoh's ants managed only 70% (S4), so weaker-trailing species can stray more) it ignores the trail this decision. Outbound ants never lay trail scent; only food-carriers do — so the trail always points at food that was really found.
3. **The prod (bug stick).** The prodding player's client sends "prod at cell, tick"; the server stamps it into the ledger. At the event tick every client applies the same rule: ants of that colony within radius r of the cell, sorted by distance then bug id, first k (k set by the tool's tier). **First prod:** tapped ants stop, rear up and face the player for a moment (alert). **Second prod within a short window:** they switch to *follow player P*. (Pikmin 3 Deluxe's two-step pull, S7 — a stray swing never wrecks a trail.)
4. **Following the player.** Reynolds' leader-following (S22): each follower aims at a slot just behind the player's direction of travel (from the player's last cells, which the simulation already receives as `PLAYER_CELL_ENTER`), steps aside if in front, keeps apart from the other followers. The chain is anchored to the player, never ant to ant.
5. **Leaving the player.** A follower drops out when the player is more than D cells away for T seconds, when food is adjacent, when the player releases (a ledger event), or after a maximum follow time; then a short no-recapture cool-down. A dropped ant enters the *lost* state (slower, more wiggle, U-turns — S4) until it smells scent again or, failing that, walks home on the cost-to-nest field.
6. **Founding a new trail.** A follower next to food picks up a piece, turns home and lays scent all the way, and **remembers that food cell** (route memory beats scent, S4): it walks back to it on its next trips even while the new trail is faint. Other outbound ants meet the new scent where it crosses or nears their path and switch at their stray rate or when it out-smells the old trail. If the new food is closer or richer, traffic shifts on its own; the old trail keeps most ants until its food runs out.
7. **Endings.** When a food item reaches 0 (`FOOD_CONSUMED`), the ant that took the last piece lays repellent at the source and on its way home (C1) — the old trail dies quickly and its ants spread out. Rain (once it is a ledger event) halves or wipes scent (S1); founders re-lay trails first because they remember.
8. **What the player sees.** Trails rendered as a faint ground sheen (SimAnt let players toggle a scent view, S1); alert ants rear up; followers carry a small marker; lost ants zigzag. A future lure item (candidate D) can write trail scent directly.
9. **Multiplayer.** Each prod is one ledger event; all selection happens at the event tick from synced positions, so every client taps the same ants. Prods are budgeted and repeats at the same cell filtered (S27). Two players can fight over the same column — the second prod re-targets followers to the second player (a fun tug-of-war) or is ignored while followers are in cool-down (a design choice for the owner).
10. **Sync contract (per `architecture_swarm_sync.md` §0).** The trail grid and each ant's follow/lost/route-memory fields become client simulation state: included in `ComputeStateHash` (e.g. a hash over the sparse cells in index order) and in the join snapshot (sparse cell list; the per-bug fields ride the verbatim per-bug relay); the prod and release are new ledger events applied at their tick; tested by a non-vacuous two-client late-join run with trails and followers active.
11. **Cost estimate (to verify by profiling).** A colony of ~100 ants deciding 3–4 times a second is a few hundred to ~1,200 cell reads a second; deposits are at most one write per carrier per decision; decay touches only active cells (a trail of a few hundred cells). Sparse snapshot cost ≈ 4 bytes per active cell — a few KB per colony, against today's ~230 KB raw village snapshot.
12. **Where the server keeps a role.** Colony-level decisions stay where they are: how many workers leave the nest, nests and population (the existing recruitment numbers and food registry). Which cells the trail occupies is decided by the ants themselves on the clients — consistent with the project's rule that per-individual behaviour lives on the client.

## Part 5 — Open risks

**Risks to the recommended trail design**
1. **New shared state.** The trail grid and the per-ant follow/lost/route-memory fields are new client simulation state. They must enter the state hash and the join snapshot with their own carrier, and need a two-client late-join test that actually runs with trails and followers active — otherwise a late joiner rebuilds different trails and diverges permanently (the 2026-07 hunt-state bug in `architecture_swarm_sync.md` §0 is the precedent).
2. **Divergence amplifies through the grid.** A trail is written by every ant's past positions, so any one-bug mismatch spreads into the scent and then into every follower (Age of Empires' deer, S27). The drift detector and resync cover recovery, but integer-only maths, bug-id ordering and fixed decay ticks have to be held strictly.
3. **Who chooses the tapped ants.** Choosing at the event tick is deterministic, but the ants will have moved a little between the player's swing and the event tick, so the player may occasionally tap a neighbour of the ant they aimed at. Needs a forgiving radius and play-testing; the alternative (the prodding client names the ids) is faster-feeling but uses that client's own, slightly stale view.
4. **Follower motion from cell-resolution player positions.** The simulation only knows the player's cell (`PLAYER_CELL_ENTER`), so followers aim at cell centres; it may look steppy without visual smoothing (display-only, so safe).
5. **Positive feedback can lock a colony onto its first trail.** Max-blending prevents saturation, but the biology also has negative feedback (ants lay less where the trail is strong or crowded, S4). If tuning shows colonies never switch to better food, add a crowding cut on deposits.
6. **Ant mills.** Any follow-the-bug-in-front rule can circle forever (S3, C2). Keep chains anchored to the player and give lost ants a timeout to the home field.
7. **Relationship to today's colony memory.** `colony.go`'s routes and recruitment are server soft state outside the hash; moving per-ant pathing to client scent changes what the server decides. Which parts stay on the server (recruitment counts, sites for the population pass) is a design decision for the build plan, not settled here.
8. **Abuse.** Endless leading of workers into a catcher, or prod-spam. Budget and filter prods (S27); consider habituation (bugs that are prodded often respond less, as Black & White villagers grew bored of repeated tricks, S8).

**Risks to the wider set of techniques**
9. **Weather and time are not simulation inputs today.** `architecture_weather.md`: nothing in weather or time of day feeds the bug simulation; rain is applied on receipt. Every weather reaction (bees home in rain, scent washed, pre-rain settling) needs rain start/stop — and, for pre-rain behaviour, the already-scheduled start tick — published as frontier-gated ledger events. Time of day is computed from the synced tick, but the debug day offset arrives on receipt; activity curves must read only the frontier-safe part.
10. **Level of detail must never come from a machine's own budget** (S25, S27). Any "simplify far bugs" rule has to be a function of synced data, applied identically everywhere.
11. **Game-time scaling of biological timings.** One in-game day is 14 minutes. Biological durations (locusts gregarise in 4–8 hours; bees switch jobs at day 10/20; pupae take weeks) need an explicit mapping to game time per mechanic; quoting them raw would make some mechanics invisible and others absurdly fast.
12. **Ecology stability.** Every new hunter needs its prey's food base to regrow on a delay, or the pair crashes instead of oscillating (C4). New behaviours that change catch rates (interception, confusion effect) shift existing balance and need the `ecology-tuning` loop.
13. **Biology facts are flavour, not law.** The project's recorded design stance is that Bug Farmer is a game first: the facts above justify and inspire behaviours, but they should not be used to cut content or to stop players from tipping the ecosystem.

**Not confirmed (collected from the entries)**
- A decay time or half-life for *Lasius niger* (or any species') trail pheromone — not in the open papers read; the classic laboratory figures are in papers that were not reachable. No number is quoted.
- SimAnt's trail fade rate and the radius "Recruit 5" pulls from; Empires of the Undergrowth's share of ants that peel off a march; Minecraft's in-hive time and bee growth time; Pikmin's whistle radius in units; Animal Crossing's scare distances; Slime Rancher's agitation and escape rules; Grounded's "far enough" distances.
- How Rain World's off-screen ("abstracted") simulation works in detail.
- Game-ready numbers for locust crowding thresholds (the review gives model densities only).
- The exact behaviour of honey bee alarm pheromone in space and time (spread, lifetime).

**Limits of this research**
- Web search was out of budget for this session; sources were fetched directly (Wikipedia, open-access papers via Europe PMC, game wikis via their APIs, Game AI Pro chapters, GitHub). Pages that blocked automated reading — the Eco wiki, the Terraria wiki, the Minecraft wiki's API (its raw page worked) — and stub pages (Equilinox) were not used; Eco and Equilinox are therefore not covered beyond a note, and Spore, Niche and Bee Simulator were not researched.
- Developer postmortems specific to bug games (e.g. Grounded's or Empires of the Undergrowth's AI) were not reachable without search; their mechanics are taken from community wikis, which describe behaviour as observed by players, not as coded.
