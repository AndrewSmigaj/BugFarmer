# Bug ecology facts, part A (2026-10-03)

These real-world facts were gathered for the bug behaviour and ecology design, from the sources listed under each species. ★ marks a bug that is on the game's roster (docs/gdd/bug_lineups.jsonl, every row not cut). The game has no seasons, so seasonal facts are noted only as context.

### Common fly — Musca domestica (house fly)
- **Eats (adult):** liquid or semi-liquid food, and solids softened with its saliva: animal matter, carrion, feces, milk, sugary things, rotting fruit and vegetables; can feed on blood opportunistically [WP]. Cannot swallow particles larger than 0.045 mm [ADW].
- **Eats (young):** maggots feed where the eggs were laid: food waste, carrion, feces; fresh pig and chicken manure suit them best; cattle, goat and horse manure give fewer, smaller pupae [WP]. Larvae also filter-feed on masses of bacteria, and will develop in moist paper and cloth [ADW].
- **Eaten by:** birds, reptiles, amphibians, insects and spiders in general [WP]; hister beetles (e.g. Carcinops pumilio) and the dump fly Ophyra aenescens eat the young; macrochelid mites (Macrocheles muscaedomesticae eats about 20 eggs a day); parasitic wasps (Muscidifurax uniraptor, Spalangia cameroni); a nematode (Habronema muscae); a killing fungus (Entomophthora muscae); a virus (SGHV) [WP, ADW]. None of the named predators is on the roster. Roster bugs whose sources list flies as a group (none names the house fly): ★ Paper wasp, ★ Yellowjacket, ★ European hornet, ★ Fire ants (flies were 58.5% of the insect remains in their waste piles), ★ Stone centipede, ★ Blue dasher (see those sections).
- **Prey on the roster:** none (it eats no living bugs).
- **Breeds:** eggs on decaying organic matter — food waste, carrion, feces — preferably moist and in the light; 75–150 eggs a batch, 5–6 batches over 3–4 days, about 500 in a lifetime; a female usually mates once and stores the sperm; no care after laying [WP, ADW].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg hatches in 8–24 h → maggot about 5 days [ADW], or two weeks at best to 30+ days when cool [WP] (the sources differ) → pupa about 4 days (2–6 days at 35 °C, 20+ days at 14 °C) → adult; whole cycle 7–10 days at best, up to two months when cold. Adults live about 15–25 days (2–4 weeks), at most about 60 days [WP, ADW].
- **Active:** by day; busiest 2–4 pm, the hottest, driest part of the day [ADW]. At night it rests high up — ceilings, beams, overhead wires — or outdoors in foliage, long grass, shrubs and trees [WP]. (Context: overwinters in cracks, gaps in woodwork and curtain folds [WP].)
- **Lives:** wherever people live, Arctic to tropics; most active between 10 and 26.6 °C [WP, ADW].
- **Social:** solitary.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** sees about seven times faster than people do, so it is hard to swat [WP]; lands on a ceiling with a half-roll, all six legs up [WP]; tastes food by walking on it [WP, ADW]; flies up to 15 mph (24 km/h) [ADW]; gathers in numbers on high resting spots at night [WP].
- **Danger to people:** no bite or sting; it carries more than 100 disease germs on its body (typhoid, cholera, dysentery, salmonella, tuberculosis, anthrax, eye infections) plus parasite cysts and worm eggs [WP, ADW].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** its best breeding food is pig and chicken manure, also cattle, goat and horse manure and carrion — all from mammals and birds; milk (a mammal product) is one adult food. Food waste, rotting fruit and vegetables, people's waste and moist paper or cloth remain. Its named vertebrate predators (birds, reptiles, amphibians) are gone.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housefly · [ADW] https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Musca_domestica/
- **Not confirmed:** which roster bugs prey on it is cross-checked under those species, not on the fly's own pages; use as fish feed: Wikipedia says the larvae are "as nutritious as fish meal" and could feed farmed fish and livestock — it does not say how widely this is done today.

### Horse fly — Tabanus atratus (black horse fly)
- **Eats (adult):** females cut the skin open and drink blood, which they need to make eggs; they also take nectar [ID, WP-HF, NCSU]. Males do not bite; they drink nectar and plant sugars [ID, NCSU, UF].
- **Eats (young):** the larvae are predators: other aquatic insects and worms [ID]; Tabanus larvae eat insect larvae, crustaceans and earthworms [UF]; horse-fly larvae are often cannibals in captivity [WP-HF].
- **Eaten by:** tiny parasitic wasps (eggs); birds, tachinid flies, fungi and nematodes (larvae); the horse guard wasp Stictia carolina and birds (adults) [WP-HF]; cattle egrets and killdeer are named natural enemies [UF]. None of these is on the roster.
- **Prey on the roster:** none named by any source (the larvae take aquatic insect larvae and crustaceans in general).
- **Breeds:** egg masses on plant stems, leaves or stones on vertical surfaces over or near water; 100–1000 eggs a mass (200–500 per NC State); the larvae drop into the water or wet soil [UF, NCSU, WP-HF]. Tabanus larvae prefer drier mud than deer-fly larvae [UF]. No parental care stated.
- **Life stages and timing:** egg 5–7 days → larva a month to a year or more, moulting 6–13 times → pupa 1–4 weeks → adult; usually one generation a year, two or three years in larger species; adults live 30–60 days [UF, NCSU, WP-HF].
- **Active:** by day, in sunlight, avoiding shade; inactive at night; Tabanidae peak at sunrise and about two hours before sunset [WP-HF, UF].
- **Lives:** eastern North America, near water [ID]; horse flies are common along trails, lanes and woodland edges [NCSU].
- **Social:** solitary.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** a big (20–28 mm), fast flier [ID]; females find hosts mostly by sight, also by carbon dioxide and odour [UF], and are drawn to shiny black objects and to polarised light reflected from dark surfaces and water [WP-HF, NCSU]; horse flies often bite the legs [NCSU]; male eyes meet in the middle, female eyes are apart [ID].
- **Danger to people:** a painful bite that can become infected [ID]; horse flies can carry anthrax mechanically [UF, WP-genus].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** females need a blood meal to make eggs; their usual hosts are livestock and other large mammals [WP-genus] — only people remain. Larvae eat earthworms (gone). Named bird enemies (egrets, killdeer) are gone.
- **Sources:** [ID] https://www.insectidentification.org/insect-description.php?identification=Black-Horse-Fly · [WP-HF] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse-fly · [WP-genus] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabanus_atratus (redirects to the genus Tabanus page) · [NCSU] https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/horse-flies-and-deer-flies · [UF] https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/IN155
- **Not confirmed:** THIN SOURCES — no university or encyclopedia page on this exact species could be fetched; most facts are for horse flies or Tabanus in general, and [ID] is a hobby identification site. Not confirmed for T. atratus itself: clutch size, larval duration, "swamp mud" as its larval home, and diving at moving objects.

### Cave fly — Conicera tibialis (coffin fly, a scuttle fly)
- **Eats (adult):** not stated by any source fetched.
- **Eats (young):** maggots eat the flesh of buried human corpses, preferring lean muscle [WP]; found on corpses buried 3–5 years, 18 years and 28 years after death, including soap-like (saponified) corpses [EPMC, FR].
- **Eaten by:** no predator named for this species. (Scuttle flies in general: none named on the pages fetched.)
- **Prey on the roster:** none.
- **Breeds:** adult females dig down through soil — up to about 2 m — and get into coffins to lay eggs on or near the body; several generations can follow one another entirely underground without surfacing [WP, FR]. Found in a concrete coffin 1.8 m down and a zinc coffin about 2 m down [FR].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg → maggot → pupa (puparia found in graves) → adult [EPMC]; durations not stated.
- **Active:** not stated.
- **Lives:** buried bodies and other closed, underground spaces; three forensic studies call it "typically associated with buried bodies" with a "preference for underground environments or closed environments" [EPMC, FR]. Scuttle flies as a family live in many places, including the nests of social insects [WP-Ph].
- **Social:** solitary.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** scuttle flies escape by running fast across a surface in abrupt bursts rather than flying — the trait that names them [WP-Ph]; tiny bodies let them find food in confined spaces other insects cannot reach [FR]; this species can dig 2 m through soil [WP, FR].
- **Danger to people:** none stated for this species; a few cases of scuttle flies opportunistically infesting living people (myiasis) are reported for the family [WP-Ph].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** its only confirmed breeding food is buried corpses — human ones in every source. People remain in 2126, so this is not a mammal dependency; but no source confirms it breeding on dead bugs or anything else.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conicera_tibialis · [WP-Ph] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoridae · [FR] https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12862066/fullTextXML (Frontiers in Insect Science 2025 review, "Wings on concealed corpse") · [EPMC] https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22Conicera%20tibialis%22&resultType=core&format=json (abstracts: Martín-Vega et al. 2011; Bourel et al. 2004; Merritt et al. 2007)
- **Not confirmed:** THIN SOURCES. Its life in CAVES and ANT NESTS — the roster's premise — is NOT confirmed: a literature search for Conicera with caves or ants found nothing (only the family Phoridae is said to use social insects' nests). Adult food, timing, activity, predators: not found.

### Ant-decapitating fly — Pseudacteon tricuspis
- **Eats (adult):** in the field it is said to feed only on its host; in the lab, sugar makes adults live 2–3 times longer, and in the wild they could get sugar from nectar or honeydew [WP].
- **Eats (young):** the larva lives inside a fire-ant worker, feeding mainly on the ant's blood (hemolymph); it moves into the head by day four and eats the head's contents, including the jaw muscle [WP, WP-G].
- **Eaten by:** no predator or parasite named in any source fetched.
- **Prey on the roster:** ★ Fire ants (Solenopsis invicta) — its only host; one egg per worker ant [WP].
- **Breeds:** the female hovers 3–5 mm above a worker ant, aims, then darts in and injects one egg into the ant's thorax ("shoulder joint"); 8–35% of attempts succeed; she carries 100–300 eggs; workers only, never winged ants; the host's size decides the fly's sex — females (much larger than males) need the bigger hosts, so females are rarer, and females fight each other over the biggest ants [WP, WP-G]. The flies are only 0.9–1.5 mm long [WP]. Males and females both home in on fire-ant odour and mate near the ants [WP].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg → larva in the ant; after about two weeks the ant becomes a "zombie", leaves the nest and dies in leaf litter or a crack; the larva releases an enzyme that dissolves the joint and the head falls off → pupa inside the empty head for 2–6 weeks → the adult crawls out through the ant's mouth. Egg to adult 5–12 weeks; adults live only 3–5 days [WP, WP-G].
- **Active:** by day — low in early morning, rising to a peak at midday [WP]. (Context: all year in the tropics and subtropics [WP-G].)
- **Lives:** South America (Argentina, Brazil) in rain forest, swamp and dry country; released in the US South-east [WP].
- **Social:** solitary.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** finds fire ants by their chemical scent from up to 50 m away [WP]; when the flies are present, fire ants cut their foraging and food carrying by almost 84% [WP]; the decapitation itself; a living "zombie" ant wandering away from the nest before its head drops [WP-G].
- **Danger to people:** none stated.
- **Biocontrol (fact for the design):** released at eight sites over fire-ant mounds in North Florida, 1997–1999; six sites held through the first winter, and by autumn 2000 the sites had merged into one large occupied area [WP].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none — it depends only on fire ants, which are on the roster.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudacteon_tricuspis · [WP-G] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudacteon
- **Not confirmed:** predators of the fly; danger to people; exact egg-to-head timing beyond "day four"; how fire ants physically defend themselves against it.

### Paper wasp — Polistes dominula (European paper wasp)
- **Eats (adult):** sugar from flower nectar, fruit juice, plant sap and the honeydew of sap-sucking bugs; protein from insects, which adults often eat for themselves [WP, ADW]. It bites the skin off juicy, sugary fruit and spreads yeasts and fungi on it — a fruit pest [WP].
- **Eats (young):** chewed-up prey brought by adults: caterpillars above all [ADW, PSU], but a generalist — beetles, grasshoppers and their kin (Orthoptera), true bugs, other wasps/bees/ants and adult flies [WP].
- **Eaten by:** birds, which find it less attractive than other paper wasps because of its warning colours [WP]; a twisted-wing parasite (Xenos vesparum) inside it, mites on it, and parasitoid moths, wasps and flies in the nest; workerless "cuckoo" paper wasps (e.g. Polistes semenovi) that take over a colony by killing the dominant founders [WP]. On the roster: ★ Giant hornet — the European paper wasp is on its recorded prey list (see the giant hornet). ★ European hornet attacks Polistes nests, but the species named is P. nimpha (see the European hornet).
- **Prey on the roster:** ★ Monarch — observed hunting monarch caterpillars in Kentucky, taking one from a milkweed flower head [WP]. Adult flies in general (no species named) [WP].
- **Breeds:** an open, single paper comb like an upside-down umbrella, hung by a short stalk, cells visible from below; under eaves, in attics, wall voids and other sheltered spots, on branches too; a few reach 8 inches (20 cm) across [WP, PSU]. One egg per cell [PSU]. Founded each year by one queen or a small group of overwintered queens; once 80+ wasps were seen reusing an old nest [WP].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg hatches in 3–5 days → larva → pupa → adult, about 40 days egg to adult at best (larva to adult 3–4 weeks) [ADW, PSU]; faster than native US paper wasps [WP]. Workers live under a year; queens longer [ADW]. (Context: a founder spends about a month building in spring; mated queens overwinter under bark, in hollow trees and wall voids [WP, PSU].)
- **Active:** by day, back at the nest at night [ADW]; starts foraging earlier in the day than native paper wasps [WP].
- **Lives:** native to southern Europe, North Africa and temperate Asia; introduced to the Americas, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa; forest, grassland, scrub, farms and towns, close to people [WP, ADW].
- **Social:** colony; a dominant queen lays most eggs, subordinate females forage and rarely lay; if the queen is removed the next-ranked female takes over; about 35% of a colony can be unrelated [WP].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** a female's face pattern signals her rank, and a male's abdomen spots signal his [WP]; the queen performs "abdomen stroking" more than the subordinates do [WP]; males gather on high points — fences, walls, roof peaks, trees — where females come to choose them [WP]; it flies with its legs dangling (yellowjackets tuck theirs) [PSU].
- **Danger to people:** stings; in southern Europe a frequent cause of allergic reactions [WP]; no more painful than other paper wasps, but nests in hidden spots get disturbed by accident [PSU].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none in its food; its main named predators (birds) are gone.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polistes_dominula · [ADW] https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Polistes_dominula/ · [PSU] https://extension.psu.edu/european-paper-wasp
- **Not confirmed:** that it "stays calm unless you touch its nest" — no source says how readily it attacks away from the nest; cell count of a typical nest; foraging range.

### Yellowjacket — Vespula vulgaris (common wasp)
- **Eats (adult):** sugars for its own energy: nectar, sweet and rotten fruit, honeydew; tries to steal honey from honeybee nests; fights honeybees for honeydew [WP, WT].
- **Eats (young):** freshly killed, chewed-up insects — caterpillars, bees, ants and other wasps, flies — and recently killed spiders [WP, BW].
- **Eaten by:** the honey buzzard (a bird) digs out nests for the larvae; robber flies and spiders; hoverfly larvae (Volucella pellucens) living in the nest eat young and dead adults; parasitoids — the beetle Metoecus paradoxus, the conopid fly Leopoldius coronatus, the wasp Sphecophaga vesparum, the roundworm Pheromermis pachysoma; a mite [WP, BW]. On the roster: ★ European hornet hunts "yellowjackets" (the American name for Vespula) and ★ Giant hornet takes Vespula wasps — neither source names the common wasp itself (see those species).
- **Prey on the roster:** ★ Honeybee — raids hives for honey [WP] (bees are also listed as larval prey in general). Flies, caterpillars, ants and other wasps in general, no species named [WP].
- **Breeds:** nests mostly underground, with entrance tunnels up to 45 cm long; also roof spaces (nests up to 1.2 m across), hollow trees, wall cavities, rock crevices, or holes made by other animals [BW, WP]. Paper of chewed wood and saliva, brittle and tan [WP]. A mature nest averages about 7,500 small and 2,300 large cells and rears about 10,000 workers, 1,000 queens and 1,000 males [BW]; the queen lays 200–300 eggs a day [WP].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg about 5 days → larva → pupa; first adult about 23 days after laying; a queen pupa takes about a month [WP]. Adults live about two weeks [WP]. (Context: colonies run about April–October; some colonies overwinter [WP, BW].)
- **Active:** forages whenever it is above 2 °C, most in the early morning [WP].
- **Lives:** almost any habitat — grassland, forest, scrub, farmland, wetland, towns; native to Europe and Asia, invasive in New Zealand, Australia and South America [WP, WT].
- **Social:** colony of a queen, workers (12–17 mm) and males; the queen is about 20 mm [WP].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** stings again and again (it does not die after stinging) [WP]; threat pose: rises on its toes, head forward, abdomen down, wings constantly buzzing [WP]; usually stings only when provoked by sudden movement, but defends the nest vigorously [WP]; can learn human faces; marks the nest base with an ant-repelling chemical [WP]. In New Zealand beech forest, about 10,000 workers per hectare and more total weight of wasps than of all birds [WP, DOC].
- **Danger to people:** a painful sting, rarely life-threatening; venom can cause allergic reactions [WP, DOC].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** some nests use holes dug by other animals (rodent burrows, though no source names the animal) [WP]; its named bird predator (honey buzzard) is gone.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vespula_vulgaris · [BW] https://bwars.com/wasp/vespidae/vespinae/vespula-vulgaris · [WT] https://www.wildlifetrusts.org/wildlife-explorer/invertebrates/bees-and-wasps/common-wasp · [DOC] https://www.doc.govt.nz/nature/pests-and-threats/animal-pests/wasps/
- **Not confirmed:** that it chases an attacker over a distance (no distance given in any source); larva and pupa durations in days.

### European hornet — Vespa crabro
- **Eats (adult):** mostly a hunter of large insects; also fallen fruit and other sugary food, more so late in the colony's life [WP, PSU]; strips bark from twigs (ash, lilac) to drink the sap, which can kill the twig; takes aphid honeydew [WP, BW, PSU].
- **Eats (young):** insects the workers catch: beetles, wasps, large moths, dragonflies, mantises, honeybees [WP]; grasshoppers, flies, yellowjackets [PSU]; other social wasps, butterflies, moths and spiders, often taken from flowers and tree foliage [BW]. It also steals insects from spider webs [WP].
- **Eaten by:** robber flies (they prey on each other) [WP]; nests hold a rove beetle and other scavengers, predators and parasitoids [WP, BW]. None named is on the roster.
- **Prey on the roster:** ★ Honeybee (Apis mellifera is named, though healthy colonies are not usually threatened) [WP]; ★ Yellowjacket ("yellowjackets", an American name for Vespula) [PSU]. Paper wasps: it attacks Polistes nests, but the species named is P. nimpha, not P. dominula [WP]. Dragonflies and mantises in general, no species named [WP].
- **Breeds:** nests mainly in hollow trees, also attics, outhouses and wall voids; some at the base of grass tufts or in hollows in yellow meadow ant nests; a few fully underground [BW, PSU]. Paper of chewed decayed wood with an envelope and one entrance [WP, BW]. Most nests have 1,500–3,000 cells [PSU].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg → larva → pupa → adult; durations not given. Colonies are annual: only mated queens survive to found new nests [PSU]. (Context: queens out from April, workers from late June, new queens and males in September, colony dies by October–November [BW].)
- **Active:** flies at night — it can forage in moonlight [BW] and is the only night-flying wasp in Pennsylvania, drawn to porch lights [PSU]; its daytime pattern is not described separately.
- **Lives:** forests and their edges [PSU]; Eurasia from Britain to Japan; introduced to North America by the mid-1800s [WP].
- **Social:** colony; queen up to 35 mm, workers about 25 mm; 200–400 workers at peak, up to 1,000 in large nests; males cannot sting [WP, PSU].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** night flights to lights [PSU]; ring-barking twigs for sap [WP, BW, PSU]; generally avoids conflict and stings when stepped on or grabbed, but is defensive of its nest and can be aggressive around food, sometimes stinging without warning [WP]; robs spider webs [WP].
- **Danger to people:** can sting repeatedly; about as painful as a honeybee sting; most stings settle in hours without medical care unless the person is allergic or stung many times [PSU, WP].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none found — its food and nest sites do not depend on vertebrates, snails or worms.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_hornet · [BW] https://bwars.com/wasp/vespidae/vespinae/vespa-crabro · [PSU] https://extension.psu.edu/european-hornet
- **Not confirmed:** development times in days; any predator other than robber flies.

### Giant hornet — Vespa mandarinia (northern giant hornet)
- **Eats (adult):** adults cannot digest solid food: they drink tree sap (oak sap in spring), soft fruit, honey, the juices of their victims, and a protein-rich liquid the larvae give back to them [WP, ADW]. Fights break out at sap flows with beetles and butterflies [WP].
- **Eats (young):** chewed prey paste [ADW]: honeybees, other hornets and Vespula wasps, beetles, hornworm caterpillars, mantises [WP, ADW]. A North American prey list: flies, borer beetles, the western honeybee, three yellowjacket species, bald-faced hornets, the European paper wasp, golden paper wasps, two darner dragonflies, large moths and red admirals [WP].
- **Eaten by:** the crested honey buzzard (a bird); other giant-hornet colonies; mantises; a twisted-wing parasite (Xenos moutoni) in 4.3% of females [WP, ADW]. People in Japanese mountain villages dig up nests and fry the larvae [WP]. ★ The mantis is on the roster as a group, but no source names the Chinese mantis.
- **Prey on the roster:** ★ Honeybee (Apis mellifera) and ★ Asian honey bee (Apis cerana) [WP]; ★ Paper wasp (European paper wasp named) [WP]; Vespula wasps in general [WP] (common wasp not named); it dominated the ★ European hornet in a four-hornet experiment [WP] (not stated to eat it); dragonflies and mantises in general.
- **Breeds:** nests almost always underground, in rotten pine roots or rodent burrows, 6–60 cm deep, with entrance tunnels 2–60 cm long; sometimes tree hollows or buildings [WP, ADW]. Usually 4–7 combs; the largest measured about 49.5 × 45.5 cm with 1,192 cells [WP]. The queen raises a first clutch of up to 40 workers alone [WP, ADW].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg to adult worker about 40 days [ADW]. Workers live from spring until winter; males die soon after mating; queens live up to a year [ADW]. (Context: queens out in April, solitary until mid-July, about 100 workers and 500 cells by early August, new queens and males September–October [WP].)
- **Active:** by day [ADW].
- **Lives:** low mountains and forests; absent from plains and high ground; East and South Asia and the Russian Far East; found in North America in 2019 and eradicated there by December 2024 [WP, ADW].
- **Social:** colony; queens over 50 mm, workers 35–40 mm, males about 38 mm and stingless [WP].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** hive raids in three phases — hunting single bees, then "slaughter", then occupation; a scout marks a hive with a scent from a gland under its abdomen [WP, ADW]; one hornet can kill 40 bees a minute, and fewer than 50 can wipe out tens of thousands of bees in a few hours (5,000–25,000 bees in 1–6 hours) [WP, ADW]; hornets "do not counterattack" while in the bee-hunting or hive-attack ("slaughter") phases [WP]; it clicks its jaws as a warning [WP]; flies up to 100 km a day at up to 40 km/h, usually foraging 1–2 km from the nest, up to 8 km [WP, ADW].
- **Danger to people:** a 6 mm sting with nerve-acting venom; many stings can kill people who are not allergic; an estimated 30–50 deaths a year in Japan [WP, ADW].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** some nests are in rodent burrows (mammal-made) — others are in rotten pine roots [WP]; its named bird predator (crested honey buzzard) is gone.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vespa_mandarinia · [ADW] https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Vespa_mandarinia/
- **Not confirmed:** egg, larva and pupa durations separately; whether it eats the European hornet or the common wasp specifically.

### Tarantula hawk — Pepsis grossa
- **Eats (adult):** flower nectar; in a Texas park four plants made up 73.6% of adult feeding: two milkweeds (Asclepias texana, A. sperryi), Mexican buckeye and honey mesquite [WP-Pg, WP-TH].
- **Eats (young):** one paralysed tarantula, eaten alive from the inside: the larva avoids the vital organs as long as possible, keeping the spider alive [WP-TH, WP-Pg].
- **Eaten by:** few animals: kingbirds, the greater roadrunner (which beats it on the ground first, and also steals its spider) and the American bullfrog [WP-Pg]. None is on the roster.
- **Prey on the roster:** ★ Tarantula (Goliath) — only as "tarantulas" in general (family Theraphosidae); the named host is the Texas brown tarantula, Aphonopelma hentzi [WP-Pg]. No other spider is named.
- **Breeds:** the female finds a tarantula by sight or scent (perhaps smelling the silk curtain over its burrow), enters the burrow, stings it between the legs, then drags it back into its burrow or digs a new one, lays one egg on it and seals the chamber [WP-Pg, WP-TH]. She lays female eggs on big (usually female) spiders and male eggs on male spiders [WP-Pg].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg → larva feeding on the spider for "several weeks" → silk cocoon → pupa → adult, which emerges from the spider [WP-TH, WP-Pg]. Other durations not given.
- **Active:** females hunt at dusk and dawn, avoiding the intense daytime sun, flying low over the ground [WP-Pg]; tarantula hawks in general are "most active in the daytime in summer" but avoid high heat [WP-TH].
- **Lives:** southern USA (north to Kansas) through Mexico and Central America to northern South America and the Caribbean [WP-Pg].
- **Social:** solitary hunter, but forms mixed-species, mixed-sex gatherings that seem to be defensive and help with finding food and mates [WP-Pg].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** blue-black body and rust wings, jerky movements and a strong smell all warn predators off [WP-Pg, WP-TH]; males "hill-top" — perch on tall plants watching for females and defend the spot for hours [WP-TH]; only females hunt and sting [WP-Pg].
- **Danger to people:** one of the most painful stings of any insect (4.0 on the Schmidt pain index, second only to the bullet ant), "blinding, fierce, shockingly electric", but the intense pain lasts about five minutes; not dangerous; docile, rarely stings unless provoked [WP-TH, WP-Pg].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none for food or breeding (nectar plants and tarantulas); all its named predators (birds, a bullfrog) are gone.
- **Sources:** [WP-Pg] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pepsis_grossa · [WP-TH] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarantula_hawk
- **Not confirmed:** egg and pupa durations; lifespan; whether it ever uses a Goliath tarantula or other big spiders on the roster.

### Bumblebee — Bombus terrestris (buff-tailed bumblebee)
- **Eats (adult):** nectar and pollen from a very wide range of flowers — probably the widest diet of any British bumblebee [WP-Bt, BW]. Bumblebees can "rob" nectar by biting through the base of a flower, skipping pollination [WP-Bb] (not stated for this species specifically).
- **Eats (young):** nectar and pollen fed by the queen, later by workers [WP-Bt].
- **Eaten by:** birds (bee-eaters, shrikes, great tits), badgers that dig up whole nests, robber flies, beewolf wasps, crab spiders on flowers [WP-Bb, WP-Bt]; cuckoo bumblebees (Bombus vestalis, which parasitises only this species, and B. bohemicus) take over nests; conopid flies; bee-moth caterpillars (Aphomia sociella) eat the brood; gut parasites (Nosema bombi, Crithidia bombi) and deformed wing virus [WP-Bt, BW]. None of these is on the roster.
- **Prey on the roster:** none.
- **Breeds:** nests underground, usually in old mouse or vole nests and abandoned rodent burrows, out of direct sun [BW, WP-Bt, WP-Bb]. Several eggs per wax cell [WP-Bt]. Colonies average 300–400 bees, often over 500 [WP-Bt, BW].
- **Life stages and timing:** the founding queen lays a small batch of female eggs, feeds the larvae herself, they pupate, and the first workers come out about two weeks after pupating [WP-Bt]; about 30 days into the colony the queen switches to laying males, and workers 30+ days old may start laying eggs [WP-Bt]. (Context: queens hibernate in a dug "hibernaculum"; in mild winters with garden flowers a winter generation can fly [WP-Bt, BW].)
- **Active:** busiest in the morning, peaking around 7–8 am; foragers prefer about 25 °C [WP-Bt]. Bumblebees fly when honeybees stay home and soak up heat from weak sun; flight muscles must reach 30 °C, which they reach by shivering [WP-Bb].
- **Lives:** Europe, North Africa, the Near East; invasive in Japan, Chile, Argentina and Tasmania [WP-Bt].
- **Social:** small annual colony of a queen (20–22 mm), workers (11–17 mm, varying a lot in size) and males (14–16 mm) [WP-Bt].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** buzz pollination — it can pollinate greenhouse tomatoes where other pollinators cannot; bred commercially since 1987, over 1 million nests traded a year [WP-Bb, WP-Bt]; stores only a few days' food, so a colony starves quickly [WP-Bb]; a successful forager runs around the nest "frantically" to rouse others [WP-Bt]; leaves a scent mark on flowers it has emptied [WP-Bb]; can find its way back to the colony from up to 9.8 km away, though the return often takes several days [WP-Bt].
- **Danger to people:** its sting has no barbs, so it can sting repeatedly; bumblebees are not normally aggressive but sting to defend the nest or if hurt [WP-Bb].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** nests in old mouse and vole nests and rodent burrows (mammal-made) [BW, WP-Bb]; most named predators are birds and badgers (gone).
- **Sources:** [WP-Bt] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombus_terrestris · [WP-Bb] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bumblebee · [BW] https://bwars.com/bee/apidae/bombus-terrestris
- **Not confirmed:** egg and larval durations in days; whether B. terrestris itself robs nectar (stated only for "many species").

### Honeybee — Apis mellifera (western honey bee)
- **Eats (adult):** nectar (made into honey) and pollen (made into "bee bread"), stored honey, water, and secretions from nestmates; collects resin (propolis) from tree buds and sap flows to seal the nest [WP, ADW].
- **Eats (young):** all larvae get royal jelly for the first three days; worker larvae then get bee bread (pollen and nectar); queen larvae stay on royal jelly [WP].
- **Eaten by:** crab spiders, orb-weaver spiders, beewolf wasps, vespid wasps and hornets; bee-eaters, flycatchers and honeyguides (birds); bears, honey badgers, skunks, opossums (mammals); toads; wax-moth caterpillars and hive beetles in the nest; the Varroa mite (the worst threat) [ADW, WP]. On the roster: ★ Yellowjacket raids hives for honey and takes bees [WP-Vv]; ★ European hornet preys on it [WP-Vc]; ★ Giant hornet wipes out hives [WP-Vm]; ★ Death's-head hawkmoth raids hives for honey, a "minor pest" [WP-Aa].
- **Prey on the roster:** none.
- **Breeds:** in a cavity such as a hollow tree trunk, on combs of six-sided wax cells; queen cells are larger and hang vertically [WP, ADW]. Summer colonies hold 40,000–80,000 bees [WP]. The queen lays nearly all year: over 2,500 eggs a day [WP], or "as many as 1,000 eggs/day, and 200,000 eggs in her lifetime" [ADW]; she mates with up to 10 males in one flight [ADW]. Colonies reproduce by swarming: the old queen leaves with about two-thirds of the workers, and the hive raises one to two dozen new queens [WP]; colonies swarm once or twice a year [ADW].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg → larva about a week → pupa about a week → adult; queen 16 days, worker 21 days, drone 24 days [WP, ADW]. Workers live 2–4 weeks in the busy season, up to 11 months over winter; queens 2–5 years (up to 8); drones 4–8 weeks and die right after mating [ADW, WP].
- **Active:** by day; the hive works around the clock; no flying below about 10 °C or in heavy rain or strong wind; best foraging at 22–25 °C; flight needs a body temperature of 35 °C [ADW, WP].
- **Lives:** every continent but Antarctica; meadows, open woods and gardens with plenty of flowers, also grassland, desert and wetland with water and a nest cavity [WP, ADW]. Forages within about 3 km of the hive [ADW].
- **Social:** colony of one queen, tens of thousands of female workers, and stingless males (drones) [WP, ADW].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** the waggle dance tells nestmates the direction and distance of food; a "round dance" means food within 50 m [WP]; guards at the entrance check incomers by smell [ADW]; a swarm is unaggressive because it has no hive to protect [WP]; bees cool the hive by evaporating water and nectar [WP]; one subspecies (A. m. cypria) kills invading hornets by smothering them [WP].
- **Danger to people:** the worker's sting is barbed, so it usually stays in the skin and the bee dies (not always) [WP, ADW]; bee pollen, propolis and royal jelly can cause allergic reactions [WP].
- **Uses by people:** honey (does not rot), beeswax, pollen, propolis, royal jelly, edible brood; worldwide crop pollination [WP, ADW].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none for food or breeding; most of its named predators (birds, bears, honey badgers, skunks, opossums, toads) are gone.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_honey_bee · [ADW] https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Apis_mellifera/ · cross-checks: [WP-Vv] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vespula_vulgaris · [WP-Vc] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_hornet · [WP-Vm] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vespa_mandarinia · [WP-Aa] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acherontia_atropos
- **Not confirmed:** the egg stage in days (no fetched page states it); the size of a swarm in numbers.

### Killer bee — Africanized honey bee (hybrid of Apis mellifera with A. m. scutellata)
- **Eats (adult):** as the honeybee, but it gathers more pollen than European bees, starts foraging younger, responds to weaker sugar solutions, and forages over longer distances in desert areas [WP-AHB, WP-Sc].
- **Eats (young):** not stated separately (honeybee larval diet; see the honeybee).
- **Eaten by:** no predator named for the hybrid or the African parent on the pages fetched.
- **Prey on the roster:** none.
- **Breeds:** nests more often in ground cavities than European bees [WP-AHB]; an African parent nest is pictured in a rock crevice [WP-Sc]. Swarms more often and farther; "absconds" — the whole colony abandons the hive and its food to start over — under stress or long food shortage [WP-AHB, WP-Sc].
- **Life stages and timing:** not stated on the pages fetched (development times were not given).
- **Active:** not stated separately (the western honey bee it descends from is day-active [see Honeybee]).
- **Lives:** made in Brazil in 1956–57 when 26 swarms of a European × African cross escaped near Rio Claro; spread up to almost 2 km a day, reaching Mexico in 1985 and Texas in 1990 [WP-AHB]. It out-competes European bees in the tropics but cannot survive long food shortages, which keeps it out of places with harsh winters or very dry late summers [WP-AHB]; the African parent "is not well adapted for cold areas that receive heavy rainfall" [WP-Sc].
- **Social:** colony, as the honeybee, with a higher share of guard bees [WP-AHB].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** a larger alarm zone round the hive; reacts faster; sends out three to four times as many defenders; chases people up to 400 m; victims get about 10 times more stings than from European bees [WP-AHB, WP-Sc]. Looks almost the same as a European honeybee — said to be slightly smaller and darker, though the same page also says the hybrids are not smaller; reliable identification needs measurement [WP-AHB].
- **Danger to people:** has killed about 1,000 people and also horses and other animals; on average two or three deaths a year (the page does not say where) [WP-AHB]; an estimated 500–1,100 stings is a lethal dose for an adult [WP-Sc].
- **Value to a keeper:** a hive can make as much as 100 kg of honey; more resistant than European bees to Varroa mites, chalkbrood and colony collapse disorder [WP-AHB]. Beekeepers prevent or reverse it by "requeening" — replacing the queen with a mated European queen; most North American beekeepers requeen every year [WP-AHB].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none.
- **Sources:** [WP-AHB] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africanized_bee · [WP-Sc] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apis_mellifera_scutellata
- **Not confirmed:** development times; predators; whether it stays agitated for a set time after a disturbance; any university source (none could be fetched).

### Asian honey bee — Apis cerana (eastern honey bee)
- **Eats (adult):** pollen and nectar or honey; workers pass food mouth to mouth; it must store about a third of its nectar as concentrated honey to last the hard season [WP].
- **Eats (young):** not stated separately on the pages fetched (honeybee larval diet).
- **Eaten by:** hornets (vespine wasps) are "a major predator", worst when hornets are most numerous [WP]; ★ Giant hornet — Apis cerana is named prey of Vespa mandarinia [WP-Vm]; yellow hornets [WP-J]. Also the mite Varroa jacobsoni (which it coevolved with), the gut parasite Nosema ceranae, and sacbrood viruses that stop larvae pupating [WP].
- **Prey on the roster:** none.
- **Breeds:** several parallel combs in a cavity of about 10–15 litres, with a small entrance; up to 100,000 cells when complete [WP, WP-J]. A colony: one queen, 6,000–7,000 workers, several hundred drones [WP]. To swarm, the colony rears new queens and splits, half the workers leaving with the old queen; the swarm waits 20–30 m from home for a few days before moving on [WP].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg 3 days → larva 8 days → pupa (duration not given) → adult gnaws through the wax cap [WP].
- **Active:** time of day not stated on the pages fetched; keeps its winter cluster core at about 34 °C [WP].
- **Lives:** South, Southeast and East Asia, from the Russian Far East to eastern Indonesia; rainforest, savanna, steppe, grassland, deciduous forest and taiga [WP]. Brought deliberately to New Guinea in the 1970s, it spread to the Torres Strait Islands, the Solomon Islands and Australia (first found there in 2007) [WP].
- **Social:** colony of queen, workers and drones [WP].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** heat-balling: about 500 bees wrap a hornet and heat it to 47 °C, just under their own lethal 48–50 °C [WP] (the Japanese subspecies page says hundreds of bees and 46 °C, with high CO2 helping kill the hornet; some bees die too [WP-J]); the bees make carefully timed waves of wing "shimmering" when hornets approach, plus louder warning wingbeats [WP, WP-J]; smears plant material at the hive entrance [WP-J], and uses feces and even human urine to defend hives against hornet raids (a 2020 Vietnam study) [WP]; grooms mites off far more carefully than the western honeybee [WP]; "absconds" — abandons the nest and builds anew — more readily than the western honeybee, when pollen and nectar run short [WP].
- **Danger to people:** "less likely to sting than the western counterpart" [WP-J].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apis_cerana · [WP-J] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apis_cerana_japonica · cross-check [WP-Vm] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vespa_mandarinia
- **Not confirmed:** honey yield compared with the western honeybee (no figure on any page fetched); pupa duration; lifespans.

### Black ants — Lasius niger (black garden ant)
- **Eats (adult):** honeydew "milked" from aphids (also tends mealybugs), nectar — including from the extra nectar glands on broad bean plants — fruit; scavenges and hunts small invertebrates such as codling-moth caterpillars; drinks a sugary secretion from the caterpillars of the silver-studded blue butterfly [WP, BW]. Comes indoors for sweet things when outdoor food is scarce [BW].
- **Eats (young):** not stated separately.
- **Eaten by:** no predator named on the pages fetched. Temporary social parasites, the ants Lasius mixtus and L. umbratus, take over its colonies [WP].
- **Prey on the roster:** none named.
- **Breeds:** nests underground, under stones, paving slabs, wood, plastic or metal, in rotten wood and under roots; builds soft mounds where no stones are available [WP, BW]. One queen per colony; new queens found nests alone without eating, living off their own wing muscles (2–3 queens may start together) [WP]. Mating flights of thousands of winged males and queens on warm afternoons [WP, BW].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg to adult 8–10 weeks [WP]. Queens have lived up to 29 years, the longest recorded for any social insect; workers live a couple of months in the wild, at least four years in the lab [WP]. (Context: mating flights July–August in Europe [WP, BW].)
- **Active:** not stated (day or night) on the pages fetched; workers "forage boldly in the open" [BW].
- **Lives:** gardens, paving and open ground, nesting under stones or in mounds [WP, BW]; mating flights are recorded in both Europe and North America [WP].
- **Social:** colony of 4,000–7,000 workers (several hundred to 10,000 per BWARS; rarely up to 40,000); queen about 9 mm, workers 3–5 mm, males 3.5–4.5 mm; no soldier caste, but later generations of workers are bigger and stronger [WP, BW].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** guards aphids and drives off their predators, such as ladybirds [WP]; escorts silver-studded blue caterpillars, fending off wasps and spiders [WP]; aggressive to other ants; builds earth tunnels over its trails and up plant stems [BW]; as a formicine ant it has no working sting and defends itself by squirting formic acid from the tip of its abdomen [WP-F]; the colony is organised to slow the spread of disease, and infected workers spend more time foraging outside [WP].
- **Danger to people:** none stated beyond being a household nuisance [BW].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none of its food depends on vertebrates, snails or worms; but its main sugar source is aphid and mealybug honeydew, and aphids are cut from the roster (the roster row already has it eat nectar and fruit instead).
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasius_niger · [BW] https://bwars.com/ant/formicidae/formicinae/lasius-niger · [WP-F] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formicinae
- **Not confirmed:** day or night activity; any named predator; formic acid stated for Lasius niger by name (it is stated for all formicine ants, and Lasius is a formicine genus).

### Fire ants — Solenopsis invicta (red imported fire ant)
- **Eats (adult):** omnivore: nectar, sugar, sap, honeydew from sap-sucking bugs, seeds and grain, insects and other animal tissue, people's processed food [WP, ADW]. Waste piles show flies (58.5% of insect remains, adults, pupae and larvae), isopods (21.7%), beetles, crickets, spiders, wasps and bees, caterpillars, bugs, termites, millipedes, centipedes and thrips; it also eats dead birds, livestock and reptiles, and fence-lizard eggs [ADW]. Its venom can kill small vertebrates [ADW].
- **Eats (young):** not stated separately.
- **Eaten by:** spiders (yellow garden spider, trashline orb weaver, striped lynx spider), pyramid ants; scarab, clown and leaf beetles, seed bugs and silverfish eat brood inside the nest [ADW]; birds, dragonflies, earwigs, other ants [WP]; phorid flies (Pseudacteon), microsporidian parasites (Thelohania, Kneallhazia) and viruses [WP]. On the roster: ★ Black widow — the southern black widow, Latrodectus mactans, is named [ADW]; ★ Ant-decapitating fly (Pseudacteon) [WP]; dragonflies in general [WP].
- **Prey on the roster:** none named at species level; flies, crickets, spiders, wasps and bees, caterpillars, millipedes and centipedes are all on its menu as groups [ADW].
- **Breeds:** a mound 10–60 cm high and about 46 cm across, with no visible entrance; chambers 10–80 cm down; tunnels run up to 25 feet (about 7.6 m) out from the mound [WP]. Single-queen or many-queen colonies [WP, ADW]. A queen lays about 800 eggs a day, up to 1,000 at best, and 4,000–6,000 winged offspring a year for about 7 years [ADW]. Mating flights in warm, humid, calm weather (25–35 °C, 60–80% humidity, wind ≤ 8 km/h) [ADW].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg 7–10 days → larva 6–12 days → pupa 12–15 days → adult; winged forms mature in 50–90 days [ADW]. Workers live 60–150 days typically (91–375 days in some data); queens about 6.5 years on average [ADW].
- **Active:** foragers work mainly by day, when the ground surface is 27–42 °C [ADW]; foraging reaches 10 m past the end of the longest tunnels [ADW]. (Context: in winter colonies stay below the surface and stop foraging [ADW].)
- **Lives:** native to tropical central South America (south-eastern Peru to central Argentina and southern Brazil); accidentally introduced to the United States, Australia, New Zealand, Asia, the Caribbean and Europe [WP]; prefers freshly disturbed, sunny, human-altered ground — pastures, parks, roadsides, ditches [ADW, WP].
- **Social:** colonies of 500,000–2,000,000; workers 2.4–6.0 mm; major workers make up as much as 35% of the force and are about four times heavier than minors [WP]; the largest, oldest workers do the foraging [ADW].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** floods: the colony links into a floating ball or raft with workers outside and the queen inside, lasting up to 12 days — rafts are more aggressive and dangerous to touch; workers sometimes push the males off to drown [WP]; a forager that finds rich food recruits a crowd, which peaks within about 30 minutes [ADW]; it grips with its jaws, then drives its sting in, and "there seems to be no known mechanism" that stops it short of the target dying or the whole ant being pulled off [ADW]; single-queen colonies are "extremely aggressive" to neighbouring nests [WP]; dumps rotting food on waste piles outside [ADW].
- **Danger to people:** a burning sting, then sterile pustules for several days; over 14 million people stung a year in the US; 0.6–6.0% may suffer anaphylaxis; over 80 deaths recorded [WP]. Also a crop pest (e.g. 400–575 kg/ha lower soybean yields) [ADW].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none needed — it is an omnivore; vertebrate carrion and lizard eggs are only part of its diet; its bird predators are gone.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_imported_fire_ant · [ADW] https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Solenopsis_invicta/
- **Not confirmed:** whether the "major workers" have notably bigger heads (the roster's "big-headed soldiers") — the pages give only weight, not head shape; night activity.

### Stone centipede — Lithobius forficatus (brown centipede)
- **Eats (adult):** a hunter of insects, spiders and other arthropods, killing with venom from the claw-like front "jaw-legs" [WP-de]; prey listed as spiders, slugs, worms and flies [WP]; it efficiently killed fruit flies before they could lay eggs in a crop study [EPMC]. Stone centipedes in general also eat leaf litter [WP-L].
- **Eats (young):** not stated separately.
- **Eaten by:** none named for this species. Centipedes in general: mongooses, mice, salamanders, beetles, some specialist snakes, an African ant [WP-C]. As a group only: ★ Fire ants' waste piles contain centipedes (see Fire ants).
- **Prey on the roster:** none named from the wild; in a lab study they were kept on ★ House crickets (Acheta domesticus) [TD]. Flies and spiders in general.
- **Breeds:** the male spins a loose web and leaves a sperm packet; the female picks it up; she lays her eggs one at a time on the ground [WP-de]. No egg care stated.
- **Life stages and timing:** hatches with 7 pairs of legs and adds segments and legs at each moult to 15 pairs [WP]; about five stages with incomplete segmentation, then about five more with full segmentation [WP-de]. Lives 5–6 years [WP, WP-C].
- **Active:** at night [WP-de]; centipedes in general are mostly nocturnal and need humid places [WP-C].
- **Lives:** upper soil under stones, rotting logs and bark; gardens and compost heaps, open ground and woods; Europe, North America, Hawaii [WP, WP-de, WT, BM].
- **Social:** solitary.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** runs "extremely quickly" for cover when exposed [WP]; defence with its last legs: it swings leg pairs 12–15 up, holds the last pair upright for up to 20 s, and flings sticky droplets that string out into threads at least 3 cm long; the threads dry and leave an attacker stuck — attacking ants are paralysed and die [TD].
- **Danger to people:** harmless; too small to break the skin usually; a bite gives at most brief local pain [WP] (painful from large ones [WP-de]).
- **2126 stand-in needed:** slugs and worms are part of its listed prey [WP] (gone in 2126); insects and spiders remain.

### Tiger centipede — Scolopendra polymorpha (Sonoran desert centipede)
- **Eats (adult):** no prey named for this species on any page fetched. The genus Scolopendra feeds "primarily on insects and other invertebrates"; larger specimens of the genus take frogs, tarantulas, lizards, birds, snakes, rodents and bats [WP-S].
- **Eats (young):** not stated.
- **Eaten by:** no predator named for this species. (Centipedes in general: mongooses, mice, salamanders, beetles, specialist snakes [WP-C].) As a group only: ★ Fire ants' waste piles contain centipedes (see Fire ants).
- **Prey on the roster:** none confirmed — the roster's "it eats the ants" is not supported by any source fetched.
- **Breeds:** not described for this species. Scolopendra mothers guard their eggs (a pictured S. ovitora) [WP-SM]; centipede mothers that brood curl their bodies round the eggs and young and groom them, probably to remove fungi and bacteria [WP-C]. - **Life stages and timing:** egg → young (full set of segments) → adult; durations and lifespan not stated.
- **Active:** at night, and only when it is cool and moist — in the US Southwest during the cool months or the summer monsoon; the rest of the year it stays burrowed underground [WP].
- **Lives:** dry grassland, forest and desert of the US Southwest and northern Mexico, under rocks, in burrows and rotting logs; 10–18 cm long; colour varies a lot (its name means "many forms") [WP].
- **Social:** solitary.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** venom refill: after its venom is fully milked it regrows 65–86% of the venom volume but only 29–47% of the venom protein in the first 48 hours, then no more over the next 12 days; even 7 months later volume and protein were only back to 93% and 76% [VR]. About two-thirds of the venom it can give comes out in the first two pulses, and bigger centipedes yield more [EPMC].
- **Danger to people:** its venom damaged muscle in mice (necrosis, ragged red fibres) [WP, EPMC]. All Scolopendra can give a painful bite; most species' venom is not medically significant, though some cause intense, long-lasting pain and swelling [WP-S]. No human case for this species on the pages fetched.

### Giant centipede — Scolopendra gigantea (Amazonian giant centipede)
- **Eats (adult):** a "voracious" carnivore [ADW]: large insects (crickets, roaches), worms, snails, spiders, millipedes, scorpions and tarantulas; vertebrates — lizards, frogs up to 9.5 cm, toads, snakes up to 25 cm, sparrow-sized birds, mice and bats [WP, ADW]. Its venom can immobilise small mammals such as bats and mice [WP].
- **Eats (young):** not stated.
- **Eaten by:** large birds, spiders, and insect-eating mammals — coati, kinkajou, opossum [WP]. No roster species named. As a group only: ★ Fire ants' waste piles contain centipedes (see Fire ants).
- **Prey on the roster:** none at species level; its menu includes the groups ★ tarantulas, ★ scorpions, ★ millipedes, crickets and spiders [WP, ADW].
- **Breeds:** the male leaves sperm on a silk pad; the female picks it up, lays eggs and broods them until the young can feed themselves [ADW]. Egg number and brooding time not given.
- **Life stages and timing:** egg → brooded young → adult; lives about ten years [ADW]. Durations not given.
- **Active:** not stated for this species (centipedes in general are mostly nocturnal [WP-C]).
- **Lives:** northern South America and nearby islands (Venezuela, Colombia, Brazil, Trinidad, Aruba, Curaçao); tropical rain forest and dry forest, in dark, moist places — leaf litter, soil, under rocks, logs and bark, rotten wood; also caves, and trash piles, rubble, plant pots and crawl spaces near people [WP, ADW].
- **Social:** solitary.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** the world's largest centipede, over 30 cm [WP]; large ones catch bats in caves by climbing the ceiling and holding heavy prey with only a few legs still attached to the rock (recorded taking three bat species in a Venezuelan cave) [WP]; spiny rear legs ward off predators [ADW].
- **Danger to people:** a bite strong enough to seriously wound a person [ADW]; a four-year-old child in Venezuela died in 2014 after a bite from one hidden in a soda can [WP, WP-S].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** much of its famous prey is vertebrate — bats (its cave-ceiling hunt), mice, lizards, frogs, toads, snakes and birds — plus worms and snails; all gone. Its invertebrate prey (insects, spiders, millipedes, scorpions, tarantulas) remains.

### Garden millipede — Narceus americanus (American giant millipede)
- **Eats (adult):** decaying wood and leaf litter; also deer droppings and fresh fruit, which it prefers to its usual food when given the choice [WP].
- **Eats (young):** not stated separately.
- **Eaten by:** an unknown predator on Tuckernuck Island (Massachusetts) eats only the head and first few segments — apparently the only non-toxic part; voles and the eastern towhee (a bird) are the suspects [WP]. No roster predator named. As groups only: ★ Giant centipede and ★ Fire ants eat millipedes (see those sections).
- **Prey on the roster:** none (a detritivore).
- **Breeds:** unlike millipedes that lay 20–300 eggs, it lays just one egg in a nest of chewed leaf litter and its own droppings; the female wraps herself round the egg and nest until it hatches several weeks later [WP].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg (several weeks) → hatchling with 7 body segments and 3 pairs of legs, adding segments and legs at each moult → adult at 1–2 years; can live up to 11 years [WP].
- **Active:** feeds mostly at night [WP]; found in or under decaying logs [WP]; digs burrows to escape dry or cold surface conditions, taking 5–60 minutes to dig in [WP].
- **Lives:** woodland of eastern North America, west to central Texas [WP].
- **Social:** solitary.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** when threatened it curls up or releases a foul-smelling liquid rich in benzoquinones, which can irritate eyes and skin and leaves a temporary brown "millipede burn" stain [WP]; it can usually be handled safely [WP]; up to 10 cm long [WP].
- **Danger to people:** minor — skin staining and irritation, eye irritation [WP].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** deer droppings (a favoured food, from a mammal) are gone; leaf litter, rotting wood and fruit remain. Its suspected predators (voles, a bird) are gone.

### Forest scorpion — Heterometrus spinifer (giant forest scorpion)
- **Eats (adult):** mainly insects — cockroaches, crickets and locusts [WP].
- **Eats (young):** not stated.
- **Eaten by:** no predator named for this species. Scorpions in general: frogs, lizards, snakes, birds and mammals; also ants, spiders, sun spiders and centipedes [WP-S]. On the roster: ★ Giant centipede eats "scorpions" as a group [WP-Sg] (no species named).
- **Prey on the roster:** crickets and locusts as groups (★ house and field crickets, ★ desert locust are on the roster, but no species is named) [WP].
- **Breeds:** not described for this species. All scorpions give birth to live young (3 to over 100 depending on species), which ride on the mother's back until their first moult, 5–25 days later; courtship is a "promenade à deux" dance lasting minutes to hours [WP-S]. Some Heterometrus species can reproduce without males [WP-H].
- **Life stages and timing:** not given for this species. Scorpions in general moult about six times and mature at 6–83 months; some live 25 years [WP-S].
- **Active:** at night; hides by day in burrows it digs [WP]; the genus is "predominantly nocturnal" [WP-H].
- **Lives:** moist forest of Southeast Asia and South Asia (Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Cambodia, Vietnam, Sri Lanka, India), in the dark undergrowth under logs and debris, and in burrows [WP]; humid, vegetated places [WP-H].
- **Social:** solitary hunter, but the genus can be kept in pairs or small groups, unlike many scorpions [WP-H].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** attacks with its big pincers more than its tail [WP]; scorpions in general kill small prey with the claws alone and save the sting for large or dangerous prey, and sit and wait at the burrow mouth [WP-S]; "skittish and defensive" [WP] though the genus is called docile [WP-H]; 10–12 cm long [WP]; scorpions glow blue-green under ultraviolet light [WP-S].
- **Danger to people:** severe pain and mild numbness, not usually lethal [WP]; for the genus, mild symptoms lasting hours to a few days and no known human deaths [WP-H].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none in its food (insects); its named vertebrate predators are gone.

### Fat-tailed scorpion — Androctonus australis (yellow fat-tailed scorpion)
- **Eats (adult):** insects, small lizards and even small mammals; cannibalism is recorded in this species [WP]. Androctonus kept in captivity eat cockroaches, grasshoppers and crickets; they "will generally try to kill and eat anything which moves and is smaller than themselves", can go months without food, and take only liquids [WP-A].
- **Eats (young):** not stated.
- **Eaten by:** no predator named for this species. Scorpions in general: frogs, lizards, snakes, birds and mammals (the grasshopper mouse and the desert long-eared bat are immune to the venom); also ants, spiders, sun spiders and centipedes [WP-S]. On the roster: ★ Giant centipede eats "scorpions" as a group [WP-Sg].
- **Prey on the roster:** none named (crickets and grasshoppers only as groups, and only in captivity) [WP-A]; it eats other members of its own species [WP].
- **Breeds:** not described for this species. All scorpions bear live young (3 to over 100), carried on the mother's back until the first moult [WP-S].
- **Life stages and timing:** not given for this species (scorpions in general: about six moults, adult at 6–83 months, up to 25 years [WP-S]).
- **Active:** at night; hides in crevices or humid, dark places by day, which may also limit water loss [WP, WP-A]. In a Tunisian hospital series, 71.7% of stings were at night and this species caused 71.7% of cases [EPMC].
- **Lives:** deserts and arid places of North Africa [WP]; the genus ranges from North Africa through the Middle East to north-east India, mostly semi-arid and arid country [WP-A]. Its shell is covered in tiny dome-shaped bumps that resist sand-blasting in sandstorms [WP].
- **Social:** solitary (cannibalistic) [WP].

### Emperor dragonfly — Anax imperator
- **Eats (adult):** butterflies, other dragonflies and damselflies, and tadpoles; it flies high into the sky to hunt and eats smaller prey in flight [WP].

- **Behaviour worth playing:** "It rarely settles, even eating its prey in flight" [BDS]; flies with the tip of its abdomen bent slightly down [BDS]; 73–82 mm long with a 104 mm wingspan — one of the big ones [WP]; its invading larvae are thought to change which species live in a pond [WP].

### Queen butterfly — Danaus gilippus
- **Eats (adult):** mostly flower nectar and dead foliage; also rotting fruit, sweat, and dry or wet dung [WP]. Males seek out plants with pyrrolizidine alkaloids (heliotropes, Eupatorium, Senecio, Crotalaria) to make their courtship scent [WP].
- **Eats (young):** caterpillars eat milkweeds and dogbanes (family Apocynaceae) — e.g. butterflyweed (Asclepias tuberosa), bloodflower (A. curassavica), A. amplexicaulis, A. humistrata, A. nivea, Cynanchum laeve, Calotropis procera, Apocynum, Gonolobus, Stapelia [WP].
- **Eaten by:** birds find it distasteful; the alkaloids males gather deter spiders [WP]. No parasite named on the page. As a group only: ★ Emperor dragonfly eats butterflies (see Emperor dragonfly).
- **Prey on the roster:** none.
- **Breeds:** females scratch a leaf with their short forelegs to test the plant, then lay small white or pale green eggs one at a time on leaves, stems and flower buds of milkweeds [WP]. Females can mate up to 15 times; pairs may stay coupled for over an hour, often resting high in a tree [WP].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg → caterpillar through five instars → pupa (chrysalis) → adult emerges 7–10 days after pupating [WP]. Egg duration and adult lifespan not stated.
- **Active:** by day — males patrol all day for females; courtship and mating mostly in the afternoon [WP].
- **Lives:** meadows, fields, marshes, open woodland and desert; the southern US (Florida, Georgia, Texas, New Mexico, Arizona, California) south to Argentina, and Cuba [WP].
- **Social:** solitary.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** poisonous to eat because the caterpillar stores heart-poisons (cardenolides) from milkweed — how poisonous depends on its food plant; queens raised on one vine had none [WP]; it and the viceroy mimic each other [WP]; males brush scent "hair-pencils" from the abdomen onto the female's antennae [WP]; no long migration like the monarch's, only short trips to higher ground in dry seasons [WP].
- **Danger to people:** none stated.
- **2126 stand-in needed:** dung (one adult food) is mostly from mammals — gone, though people's waste and sweat remain; its bird predators are gone. Host plants are milkweeds and dogbanes.

### Monarch — Danaus plexippus
- **Eats (adult):** flower nectar — milkweeds, sunflowers, asters, coneflowers, goldenrods, Joe-Pye weed, ironweed; Mexican sunflower is especially attractive; also sips water and minerals from damp soil and gravel ("mud-puddling") [WP, ADW].
- **Eats (young):** milkweeds only — over 40 Asclepias species plus related plants (Calotropis, Gomphocarpus) [WP, ADW].
- **Eaten by:** despite its poison: birds that have learned to cope (black-backed orioles, black-headed grosbeaks, brown thrashers, grackles), the black-eared mouse (up to 40 monarchs a night; birds and mice eat about 14% of the monarchs wintering in Mexico), Asian lady beetles (eggs and newly hatched caterpillars); predatory stink bugs and assassin bugs; many spiders, grasshoppers, crickets, ants, flies and beetles take caterpillars or eggs; predatory wasps; parasitoid tachinid flies and braconid wasps that grow inside the caterpillar; a protozoan parasite (Ophryocystis elektroscirrha) [WP]. On the roster: ★ Chinese mantis eats the caterpillars after removing the gut, avoiding the poison [WP]; ★ Paper wasp (European paper wasp) takes caterpillars from milkweed [WP, WP-Pd]; ★ Dragonhunter takes adults [WP-Hb]; as a group, ★ Emperor dragonfly eats butterflies (see Emperor dragonfly); crickets and ants are on the egg-and-caterpillar list only as groups [WP].
- **Prey on the roster:** none.
- **Breeds:** eggs laid singly, usually under a young milkweed leaf; 300–500 eggs over two to five weeks [WP]. Pairs can stay coupled up to 16 hours [WP].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg 3–8 days → caterpillar, five instars of about 3–5 days each → pupa 8–15 days (usually 11–12) → adult; the whole cycle can take as little as 25 days when warm, up to seven weeks when cool [WP]. Breeding adults live 2–5 weeks; the migrating generation lives months, through the winter [WP].
- **Active:** time of day not stated on the pages fetched; flies at about 9 km/h [WP]; migrating monarchs gather in roosts of four to thousands [WP-Mig].
- **Lives:** open country with milkweeds — fields, pasture, prairie, roadsides, gardens; southern Canada to northern South America, plus Hawaii, Australia, New Zealand and other Pacific islands; needs dense tree cover to overwinter [WP, ADW].
- **Social:** solitary breeder; gathers in huge clusters on trees when migrating and wintering — whole oyamel fir trees in Mexico are covered with butterflies; roosting together cuts water loss [WP-Mig].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** the caterpillar stores milkweed heart-poisons (cardenolides) and the adult stays poisonous; the wings carry about three times the poison of the body because birds attack the wings [WP]; big caterpillars chew through a milkweed vein to drain the sticky latex before feeding above the cut [WP]; a caterpillar may walk up to 10 m from its plant to pupate [WP]; eastern monarchs migrate 1,200–2,800 miles to the mountains of central Mexico [WP]; the viceroy looks like it [WP].
- **Danger to people:** none stated.
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none for food or breeding (milkweed and flowers). Several of its named predators (birds, the black-eared mouse) are gone.

- **Not confirmed:** minimum temperature for flight; cluster sizes in numbers per tree.

### Purple emperor — Apatura iris
- **Eats (adult):** not flowers; aphid honeydew, sap oozing from oak trees, dung, urine and animal carcasses [WP, BC]; males also take salts from road surfaces and dung, moisture from mud on cars and sweat from people watching them [BC, UKB].
- **Eats (young):** sallows (willows): goat willow (Salix caprea) most of all, also grey willow and, rarely, crack-willow; poplars are used more in continental Europe [WP, BC, UKB]. Young caterpillars lie along a leaf's midrib and feed only at night [WP].
- **Eaten by:** tachinid flies lay eggs on the last-stage caterpillars [UKB]. No other predator or parasite named. As a group only: ★ Emperor dragonfly eats butterflies (see Emperor dragonfly).
- **Prey on the roster:** none.
- **Breeds:** the female comes down from the canopy only to lay, putting single eggs on the upper side of sallow leaves in half-shade, on small willow bushes in clearings and bridleways or toward the crown of taller sallows; eggs are blue-green, the base turning purple after a few days [WP, UKB]. A virgin female meeting a male flies up with him to mate in the canopy; a mated female drops to the ground and the male loses interest [UKB].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg 9–10 days → caterpillar about 300 days, five instars, growing two head "horns" after the second moult, hibernating on a silk pad in a sallow fork and turning from green to brown to match → pale green chrysalis like a leaf shoot, about 2 weeks → adult [UKB, WP]. One generation a year [UKB]. Adult lifespan not stated.
- **Active:** adults live high in the treetops; males come down to the ground in early morning and late afternoon (mid-morning per BC) to feed, sometimes staying over an hour in one spot [UKB, BC].
- **Lives:** large, dense broadleaved woodland, usually with oaks, plus willow scrub; Europe across to central and western China; in Britain only central southern England [WP, BC, UKB].
- **Social:** solitary, but males gather at "master trees" — the same high-point trees in a wood, year after year — to intercept females, and defend treetop territories from rivals [UKB, WP].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** the male's purple sheen comes from the structure of the wing scales and shows only when the wings are at a certain angle to the sun; females are dark brown [UKB, WP]; "extremely elusive", at low density over large areas [BC]; old collectors lured males down with rotting animal carcasses and used a net on a pole about 4.5 m long [WP]. Wingspan 70–92 mm (females larger) [WP].
- **Danger to people:** none stated.
- **2126 stand-in needed:** several adult foods come from vertebrates — dung, urine and carcasses (mammal) — and the collectors' bait was animal carcasses; aphid honeydew is another main food, and aphids are cut from the roster. People's sweat, oak sap and mud remain. Caterpillars need willows/sallows (the roster places it on the Thicket's oaks — the adults live in oaks, but the caterpillars need willows).
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apatura_iris · [BC] https://butterfly-conservation.org/butterflies/purple-emperor · [UKB] https://www.ukbutterflies.co.uk/species.php?species=iris
- **Not confirmed:** adult lifespan; eggs per female; predators other than tachinid flies.

## Summary

**Food or breeding that depends on something not in the 2126 world** (mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, snails, worms):
- **Horse fly** — the biggest gap: females need a blood meal to make eggs, and their usual hosts are livestock and other large mammals; only people remain. Larvae also eat earthworms.
- **Common fly** — breeds best in pig and chicken manure (also cattle, goat, horse manure) and carrion; milk is one adult food. Food waste, rotting fruit and vegetables and people's waste remain.
- **Giant centipede** — its signature prey is vertebrate: bats (the cave-ceiling hunt), mice, lizards, frogs, toads, snakes, birds; also worms and snails. Insects, spiders, millipedes, scorpions and tarantulas remain.
- **Bumblebee** — nests in old mouse and vole nests and rodent burrows. **Giant hornet** and **yellowjacket** — some nests in rodent burrows or holes dug by other animals (others in rotten roots, hollow trees, roofs).
- **Purple emperor** — dung, urine and animal carcasses are among its adult foods (and were the collectors' bait); its other main food, aphid honeydew, comes from aphids, which are cut from the roster.
- **Emperor dragonfly** (adults eat tadpoles, larvae eat newt larvae) and **blue dasher** (nymphs eat tadpoles; small fish remain).
- Smaller parts of the diet: **fat-tailed scorpion** (small lizards and mammals), **stone centipede** (slugs, worms), **garden millipede** (deer droppings, a favourite), **queen butterfly** (dung).
- Not vertebrate, but a roster gap: **black ants** live largely on aphid and mealybug honeydew, and aphids are cut.
- **Cave fly** — its only confirmed breeding food is buried human corpses (people remain, but nothing confirms it in caves or ant nests).
- Predators: nearly every named vertebrate predator is gone; the tarantula hawk loses all of its known predators (kingbirds, roadrunner, bullfrog).

**Species with thin sources:** horse fly (no page on the species itself; mostly horse-fly and Tabanus facts, plus a hobby site), cave fly (forensic papers only), tiger centipede (no prey, breeding or predators found), shocking pink dragon millipede (a short encyclopedia stub), giant African millipede, dragonhunter (one short article); single-source: garden millipede, queen butterfly; killer bee (two encyclopedia pages, no university page).

**Roster wordings the sources do not support (or contradict):**
- Cave fly living in caves and ant nests — not found. Tiger centipede eating ants — not found.
- Tiger centipede "hits weaker after a bite": it refills 65–86% of its venom volume (but only 29–47% of the venom protein) within 48 hours, and the researchers concluded its hunting and defence are "minimally constrained" by refill time.
- Blue dasher keeping wasps in check — no source names wasps as its prey.
- Fat-tailed scorpion preferring rock to sand — sources say only "deserts and arid places"; its shell is sandstorm-proof.
- Dragon millipede's poison "cloud" — sources say only that it makes hydrogen cyanide (almond smell). Giant African millipede in caves, and a "spray" — not found (it oozes from pores).
- Purple emperor on the Thicket's oaks — adults live in oak canopy, but the caterpillars need willows (sallows).
- Dragonhunter of the "deep river" — sources say streams. Fire ants' "big-headed" soldiers — sources give only that majors are four times heavier.
- Not stated either way: paper wasp "calm unless you touch its nest"; yellowjacket chase distance.
