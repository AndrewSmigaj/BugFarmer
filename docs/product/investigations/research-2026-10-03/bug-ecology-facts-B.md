# Bug ecology facts, part B (2026-10-03)

These real-world facts were gathered for the bug behaviour and ecology design, from the sources listed under each species. ★ marks a bug that is on the game's roster (docs/gdd/bug_lineups.jsonl, every row not cut). The game has no seasons, so seasonal facts are noted only as context. In the 2126 world only bugs (insects and other arthropods, crayfish and crabs included), fish and people remain: predators named below that are birds, bats, other mammals, reptiles or amphibians are gone.

### Luna moth — Actias luna
- **Eats (adult):** nothing: adults have vestigial mouthparts and do not feed; they live on what they ate as caterpillars [WP, MDC].
- **Eats (young):** leaves of host trees: walnut, hickory (and pecan [MDC]), sweetgum, white birch, American persimmon, sumac [WP, ADW, MDC]. Older books also list black cherry, eastern cottonwood, quaking aspen, white willow, red oak, white oak and tulip tree, but a feeding experiment found "very poor survival" on those seven [WP].
- **Eaten by:** "All stages provide food for predators" [MDC]; bats hunt the adults [WP, MDC]; brown anoles (lizards) eat caterpillars, the predator used in a study of caterpillar defences [WP]; the tachinid fly *Compsilura concinnata*, a parasitoid released in North America against the spongy moth, harms luna moth populations [WP]. None of these is on the roster.
- **Prey on the roster:** none (adults do not eat; caterpillars eat leaves).
- **Breeds:** around midnight the female "calls" males with a pheromone, which males pick up from miles away with their large antennae; mating starts after midnight and lasts several hours [WP, MDC]. She lays 200–400 eggs, singly or in small groups, on the undersides of leaves of the larvae's host trees, over several nights [WP, ADW]. No parental care is described.
- **Life stages and timing:** egg about a week (8–13 days [MDC]) → caterpillar, five instars of about 4–10 days each, 6–7 weeks in all → the fifth-instar larva goes down to the ground and spins a thin, single-layered silk cocoon bound in dead leaves → pupa about two weeks (about nine months when it overwinters) → adult lives 7–10 days [WP, MDC]. Context: one generation a year in the north, two to three farther south [WP].
- **Active:** night; not often seen by day [WP, ADW].
- **Lives:** deciduous woodland of eastern North America, east of the Great Plains, Florida to Maine and Saskatchewan to Nova Scotia [WP, ADW, MDC].
- **Social:** solitary [ADW].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** the long hindwing tails confuse bats' echolocation and draw attacks away from the body [WP, MDC]; caterpillars click as a warning and vomit their gut contents, which is confirmed to deter predators [WP, MDC]; adults sit still and pass for living or dead leaves, and flutter their wings dramatically when attacked [ADW]; wing eyespots are thought to confuse predators [WP].
- **Danger to people:** none known [ADW]; the caterpillar's green spines do not sting but can irritate on contact [WP].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none for food or breeding (host trees only). Its main named predators (bats, lizards) are gone; the tail defence evolved against bats.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actias_luna · [ADW] https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Actias_luna/ · [MDC] https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/luna-moth
- **Not confirmed:** eggs per batch beyond "singly or in small groups"; any roster bug eating it (no source names one); the measured success rate of the tail defence.

### Death's-head hawkmoth — Acherontia atropos
- **Eats (adult):** nectar and honey; it raids beehives for honey, and of the honey bees it invades only the western honey bee's colonies (★ honeybee, *Apis mellifera*) [WPg, BC, UKM, WT].
- **Eats (young):** nightshade-family plants (Solanaceae), potato especially, also *Physalis*, deadly nightshade and other nightshades [WP, BC]; also recorded on Verbenaceae (e.g. *Lantana*), Cannabaceae, Oleaceae, Pedaliaceae, Bignoniaceae [WP, WPg].
- **Eaten by:** no predator or parasitoid is named in the sources; the squeak "may be used to deter potential predators" [WP]. Honey bees (★) sting it when they discover it; its thick waxy cuticle protects it [WPg].
- **Prey on the roster:** none (it robs ★ honeybee hives of honey; it does not kill bees, per these sources).
- **Breeds:** eggs laid singly under old leaves of the host plants [WP]; no parental care described.
- **Life stages and timing:** egg → caterpillar up to about 120–130 mm, final instar green, brown or yellow → pupa in a chamber 20–25 cm underground → adult [WP]. Overwinters as a pupa in the north of its range [WP]; in Britain the pupae cannot survive the winter [WT, BC].
- **Active:** night; "seldom seen because it flies late in the night" [WP]; usually seen only in light traps and in beehives [BC].
- **Lives:** the Middle East, the Mediterranean and much of Africa, east to India; a migrant to Britain every year [WP, UKM]. In Britain: allotments, gardens and potato fields [BC].
- **Social:** no group behaviour is described.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** walks about inside hives undisturbed because it mimics the bees' scent [WPg]; when irritated it gives a loud squeak by pushing air through its proboscis, which vibrates like a reed, often while flashing its bright abdomen [WPg, BC]; one hypothesis ties the squeak to its hive raids (a mimic of the queen's piping) [WP]; the skull pattern on its back may deter or distract predators [WP]; caterpillars sit still and click their jaws or bite if threatened [WPg].
- **Danger to people:** "perfectly harmless except as a minor pest to crops and to beehives"; old tales of a deadly sting are false [WP]. A threatened caterpillar may bite [WPg].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none (honey, nectar and nightshade-family plants).
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acherontia_atropos · [WPg] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acherontia_(moth) · [BC] https://butterfly-conservation.org/moths/deaths-head-hawk-moth · [UKM] http://www.ukmoths.org.uk/species/acherontia-atropos/ · [WT] https://www.wildlifetrusts.org/wildlife-explorer/invertebrates/moths/deaths-head-hawk-moth
- **Not confirmed:** tomato and eggplant are not named as hosts in these sources (they say nightshades, potato especially; both are nightshades); number of eggs; stage durations; any named predator or parasitoid; whether honey bees kill it when its disguise fails; whether it raids the ★ killer bee (an *A. mellifera* hybrid) the same way.

### Silk moth — Bombyx mori
- **Eats (adult):** nothing: "All adult Bombycidae moths have reduced mouthparts and do not feed" [WP]. (ADW instead says adults have sucking mouthparts but does not say they eat.)
- **Eats (young):** "essentially monophagous, exclusively eating mulberry leaves", white mulberry preferred, other mulberries accepted [WP]; ADW adds Osage orange and lettuce; artificial diets have been developed [WP].
- **Eaten by:** no wild predator or parasitoid is named; it "lacks fear of potential predators" [WP]. Diseases named: pébrine (*Nosema bombycis*), grasserie (nuclear polyhedrosis), flacherie, muscardine fungi including *Beauveria bassiana* [WP]. Nothing on the roster is named.
- **Prey on the roster:** none.
- **Breeds:** the female releases the pheromone bombykol to attract males [WPb]; males answer with a "flutter dance" [ADW]; since the moths cannot fly, "the males need human assistance in finding a mate" [WP]. A female lays 300–500 eggs [WPs] and dies after laying them [ADW]. Females are two to three times bulkier than males, from carrying the eggs [WP].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg about 10–14 days [ADW, WP] → caterpillar (the silkworm), five instars, about 6 weeks of eating [WP, ADW] → spins a cocoon of one raw-silk thread 300–900 m long in about two to three days [WP, WPs] → pupa about 2 weeks inside the cocoon [ADW] → adult. For silk, cocoons are boiled, which kills the pupa [WP, WPs].
- **Active:** not stated.
- **Lives:** only in human care: domesticated from the wild silk moth *Bombyx mandarina* (northern India to China, Korea, Japan and the Russian far east); it "does not exist in the wild" [WP, ADW].
- **Social:** reared in crowds, but no social behaviour is described (ADW lists it as "social" without details).
- **Behaviour worth playing:** cannot fly and has lost its wild colouring [WP]; "entirely dependent upon humans for survival" [WP]; eats only mulberry, so it is tied to that tree [WP]; bluish fifth-instar larvae are healthier than reddish-brown ones, a visible health tell [WP].
- **Danger to people:** none stated.
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none (mulberry leaves only).
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombyx_mori · [WPb] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombykol · [WPs] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sericulture · [ADW] https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Bombyx_mori/
- **Not confirmed:** adult lifespan in days; time of day of activity; exact cocoon length (WP says 300–900 m, the sericulture page about 910 m and "one mile"); any parasitoid such as the uzi fly (not on the pages fetched).

### Burying beetle — Nicrophorus vespilloides
- **Eats (adult):** carrion: burying beetles locate vertebrate carcasses and bury them as food for their young; the adults digest the flesh [WP, WPg]. Carrion beetles in general feed on dead animals at every stage of decay [WPsil].
- **Eats (young):** the buried carcass, and liquid food the parents regurgitate into their mouths in response to begging [WP, WPg, WPsil].
- **Eaten by:** no predator is named. The mite *Poecilochirus carabi*, which rides on the beetles, has adults that eat the beetle's own eggs [WP]; mites on a carcass also shorten the male beetle's life and reduce the number of young [WPp]. Competitors for a carcass: bluebottle flies, ants, and other burying beetles [WPg]; "Flies are the major competitor of silphids" [WPsil]. Unspecified ants and flies only; no roster species is named.
- **Prey on the roster:** none named. Its mites eat fly eggs and larvae on the carcass (flies not identified to species) [WP].
- **Breeds:** on the carcass of a small vertebrate, "such as birds and rodents" [WPg]. The pair strips off the fur or feathers (used to line the crypt), rolls the body into a ball, buries it (about 8 hours) and coats it with antibacterial and antifungal secretions that slow decay and keep the smell from drawing competitors [WP, WPg]. The female lays her eggs in the soil around the crypt; both parents guard and feed the larvae [WP, WPg]. Parents kill some of their young early so the carcass can feed the rest [WPg]. Several pairs may bury a large carcass together and raise their broods communally [WPg]; an invading female can overpower the first and start a new brood with the male [WPsil].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg, a few days → larvae move into a pit in the carcass and take several days to mature → final-stage larvae go into the soil and pupate → adult [WPg]. Context: adults become active in spring, peaking in May [WP].
- **Active:** carrion beetles are "usually more active at night" [WPsil]; not stated for this species.
- **Lives:** Europe and Scandinavia to Siberia, China, Japan, and North America [WP].
- **Social:** a breeding pair with care from both parents; sometimes communal breeding on big carcasses [WP, WPg].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** club-shaped antennae smell a dead animal from a long distance [WPg, WPsil]; the beetle carries *Poecilochirus* mites from carcass to carcass, and the mites, once off, eat fly eggs and larvae that would compete with the beetle's young [WP, WPp]; parents feed begging larvae by regurgitation [WPg]; they cull their own brood to fit the food [WPg].
- **Danger to people:** none stated.
- **2126 stand-in needed:** yes. Its breeding depends on carcasses of small vertebrates, "such as birds and rodents" (mammals and birds, both gone); adults also need vertebrate carrion. The sources do not say it can breed on dead invertebrates or fish.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicrophorus_vespilloides · [WPg] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicrophorus · [WPsil] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silphidae · [WPp] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poecilochirus
- **Not confirmed:** number of eggs; stage durations in days; carcass size range; whether adults also prey on fly maggots; habitat type (forest, bog) for this species; whether it stridulates; its predators.

### Cave beetle — Leptodirus hochenwartii
- **Eats (adult):** organic material of animal and plant origin carried in from outside by seeping water, bat and bird guano, and carcasses [WP]; most often feeds on cave walls where seeping rainwater leaves nutrients, and on carrion [slWP].
- **Eats (young):** nothing: the larva lives on the food stored in the large egg and does not feed before moulting [WP, slWP].
- **Eaten by:** no predator is named in the sources.
- **Prey on the roster:** none.
- **Breeds:** females lay a small number of relatively large eggs that take a long time to develop [WP, slWP]; no parental care described.
- **Life stages and timing:** egg (long development) → a single larval instar that does not feed → adult [WP]; slWP says growth and reproduction are slow. No durations are given; "the maximum period of activity of adults is still unknown" [WP].
- **Active:** no day–night rhythm: the stable, sunless cave has caused "the loss of circadian rhythm"; its seasonal rhythm follows rainfall [WP].
- **Lives:** large, cold caves that stay at or below 12 °C, in Slovenia, Croatia and parts of Italy; it depends on the cave's constant temperature and humidity and cannot survive outside caves [WP, slWP].
- **Social:** not stated.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** blind and wingless, with no pigment, long legs and antennae, and a domed back (elytra) that stores moist air to breathe in drier spots [WP]; a sense organ on its antennae (the Hamann organ) reads air humidity [WP]; very sensitive to touch and vibration, which it uses to find food and avoid predators without eyes [slWP]; 8–11 mm long [slWP]. The first cave beetle described: found in 1831 by lamplighter Luka Čeč in Postojna Cave, described in 1832 [WP, slWP].
- **Danger to people:** none stated.
- **2126 stand-in needed:** yes. Part of its food is bat and bird guano (gone) and carcasses; the rest is plant and animal matter washed in from outside by seeping water.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leptodirus_hochenwartii · [slWP] https://sl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drobnovratnik (Slovenian Wikipedia, read in translation)
- **Not confirmed:** egg numbers; any duration; whether there is a pupa stage (WP gives one larval instar; slWP says the first moult turns it into the adult); its predators (no source names the ★ cave spider or anything else). Thin sources: only the two Wikipedias.

### Hercules beetle — Dynastes hercules
- **Eats (adult):** fresh and rotting fruit and tree sap; it carves bark with its mandibles to reach sap; in captivity peaches, pears, apples, grapes, bananas and mangoes [WP]; forages for fallen, rotting fruit in the undergrowth [ADW].
- **Eats (young):** rotting wood (the larvae are saproxylophagous) [WP, ADW].
- **Eaten by:** "No predation events on D. hercules have been observed"; ADW suggests birds, bats and small mammals such as rats may eat the larvae (a guess, not an observation) [ADW]. Nothing on the roster is named.
- **Prey on the roster:** none.
- **Breeds:** about 30 days from mating to egg-laying; a female may lay up to 100 eggs on the ground or on dead wood [WP]; probably laid into the decaying wood of a fallen tree [ADW]. No care beyond egg-laying [ADW].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg about 28 days to a month → larva up to two years (in the lab: 1st instar about 50 days, 2nd about 56, 3rd about 450) → pupa about 32 days [WP] (ADW: larva 12–18 months, pupa 2–3 months) → adult lives three to six months in captivity [WP] (ADW: 8–12 months).
- **Active:** night: adults forage for fruit at night and hide or burrow in the leaf litter by day [WP].
- **Lives:** the understory of montane and lowland tropical rainforest, southern Mexico to Bolivia, also the Lesser Antilles and Trinidad and Tobago [WP, ADW].
- **Social:** not stated; males fight each other for the right to mate with a female [ADW].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** a male grabs a rival between his head horn and thorax horn, lifts him as high as he can and throws or slams him down [WP, ADW]; the horn muscle's force was measured at 140 newtons [ADW]; it warns with a "huffing" sound made by rubbing its abdomen against its wing cases [WP, ADW]; its wing cases change colour with humidity, olive-green or yellow when dry and black when humid [WP]. Males reach 173 mm with the horn, the world's longest beetle [WP].
- **Danger to people:** none known; not a pest or disease carrier; kept as pets [WP, ADW].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none (fruit, sap, rotting wood).
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynastes_hercules · [ADW] https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Dynastes_hercules/
- **Not confirmed:** any observed predator or parasite; adult lifespan in the wild (sources give 3–6 months in captivity and 8–12 months).

### Stag beetle — Lucanus cervus
- **Eats (adult):** WP: "Adults suck up the liquids of nectar, fallen fruit, and tree sap." WT: it is "still unclear whether either sex actually feeds"; if they do, probably tree sap.
- **Eats (young):** rotting wood: stumps, old trees and shrubs, rotting fence posts, compost heaps and leaf mould [WP]; depends on old trees and rotting wood [WT].
- **Eaten by:** cats, foxes, badgers, carrion crows, magpies, woodpeckers, kestrels, and the false widow spider (*Steatoda*, not the roster's black widow) [WP]. Phoretic mites ride on adults [WP]. None of these is on the roster.
- **Prey on the roster:** none.
- **Breeds:** a female lays about 30 eggs in a piece of decaying wood deep in the soil [WP, WT]; no parental care described.
- **Life stages and timing:** egg → larva in rotting wood 3–7 years (WT: up to six) → pupa in the soil about 3 months → adult lives only a few weeks, emerging only to mate [WP, WT]. Context: adults are out from late May to early August [WP].
- **Active:** evening: adults are most active in the evenings, and males mostly fly at sunset in search of mates [WP, WT].
- **Lives:** across Europe (not Ireland): oak woodland, also gardens, hedgerows and parks [WP, WT].
- **Social:** not stated; males fight each other in competition for females [WP].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** males wrestle with their antler-like jaws, trying to throw the rival off the log or branch [WP, WT]; larvae "talk" to each other by stridulating with combs on their legs [WP]; females, with small jaws, can give a painful bite [WP]. Males reach 7.5 cm, females 3–5 cm [WP].
- **Danger to people:** harmless; the male's jaws are too weak to hurt, though a female's bite is painful [WP, WT].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none for food or breeding (rotting wood). Nearly all its named predators are mammals and birds, now gone.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucanus_cervus · [WT] https://www.wildlifetrusts.org/wildlife-explorer/invertebrates/beetles/stag-beetle
- **Not confirmed:** whether adults feed (the sources disagree); clutch timing; any invertebrate predator other than the false widow spider.

### Colorado beetle — Leptinotarsa decemlineata
- **Eats (adult):** leaves of the nightshade family (Solanaceae), above all *Solanum*: potato, eggplant, tomato and wild nightshades [WP]; also tomatillo [UMN]. An adult eats about 10 cm² of leaf a day [WP].
- **Eats (young):** the same host leaves; a larva eats about 40 cm² of potato leaf in its life [WP]. The last (4th) larval stage does up to 75% of the damage [UMN].
- **Eaten by:** the ground beetle *Lebia grandis*; lady beetles (*Coleomegilla maculata*, *Hippodamia convergens*); stink bugs (*Perillus bioculatus*, *Podisus maculiventris*); lacewings (*Chrysopa*); tachinid flies (*Myiopharus doryphorae*, *M. aberrans*); the egg-parasitoid wasp *Edovum puttleri*; the fungus *Beauveria bassiana* [WP, UMN]. Stink bugs and lady beetles eat its eggs [UMN]. None of these is on the roster.
- **Prey on the roster:** none.
- **Breeds:** bright yellow-orange eggs in clusters of about 10–30 on the undersides of host leaves [WP, UMN]; a female lays up to 350 eggs in her life [UMN], or over 500 in 4–5 weeks [WP]. No parental care described.
- **Life stages and timing:** egg 4–15 days → larva, four instars (2–3 days each for the first three, 4–7 days for the fourth; 10 days to over a month in all, depending on warmth) → a few days as a non-feeding prepupa → pupa in the soil 5–10 days → adult; egg to adult in as little as 21 days [WP, UMN]. Context: two to three or more generations a year; adults overwinter 5–10 inches underground [WP, UMN].
- **Active:** not stated (only seasonal timing is given).
- **Lives:** native between Colorado and northern Mexico; now across most of North America, Europe and Asia; potato fields, field margins, windbreaks and gardens [WP, UMN].
- **Social:** not stated.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** it breeds fast: egg to adult in as little as 21 days, several generations a year [WP, UMN]; it walks and flies to spread, with short, long and pre-winter flights, best taking off at about 27 °C [WP]; the species has evolved resistance to 56 chemical insecticides [WP]; the larvae drop from the plant and burrow into soil to pupate [UMN].
- **Danger to people:** no health hazard stated; a crop pest only.
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none (nightshade-family crops and weeds).
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_potato_beetle · [UMN] https://extension.umn.edu/yard-and-garden-insects/colorado-potato-beetles
- **Not confirmed:** time of day it is active; any bird predator; chemical defences or warning colours; whether the ★ bombardier beetle (a ground beetle) or ★ Chinese mantis eat it (the named ground beetle is *Lebia grandis*).

### Bombardier beetle — Brachinus crepitans
- **Eats (adult):** not stated for this species. For bombardier beetles in general: "Most species of bombardier beetles are carnivorous, including the larva", and the beetle "typically hunts at night for other insects" [WPf].
- **Eats (young):** the larvae are thought to be external parasites that feed on the pupae of other beetles, including the ground beetle *Amara convexiuscula* and the rove beetle *Tasgius ater* [WP].
- **Eaten by:** no predator is named. The spray "can be fatal to attacking insects" [WPf].
- **Prey on the roster:** none named (its larval hosts are not on the roster).
- **Breeds:** "The lifecycle is not known" [WP]; the larva grows on another beetle's pupa [WP].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg → larva (parasite on beetle pupae) → pupa → adult; no durations known [WP].
- **Active:** bombardier beetles typically hunt at night [WPf].
- **Lives:** Europe, central and west Asia and northern Africa; dry, sunny places, usually under stones; also chalk and limestone grassland, arable land and chalk quarries [WP]. 7–10 mm long [WP].
- **Social:** bombardier beetles "will often congregate with others of [their] species when not actively looking for food" [WPf].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** it mixes hydroquinone and hydrogen peroxide in a firing chamber and shoots the boiling, foul-smelling liquid, near 100 °C, from its rear with a loud pop [WP, WPf]; each burst is about 70 pulses at about 500 pulses a second [WPf]; its glands hold enough for roughly 20 sprays [WPf]; some African species can swivel the nozzle through 270° and aim accurately [WPf].
- **Danger to people:** the spray's main ingredient, 1,4-benzoquinone, irritates the eyes and airways of vertebrates [WPf].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none known (insect prey, beetle pupae).
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brachinus_crepitans · [WPf] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombardier_beetle
- **Not confirmed:** this species' adult diet, egg numbers and timing; whether it aggregates or aims (those facts are for the group or other species); its predators. Thin sources: only Wikipedia (the NatureSpot and UK Beetles pages failed to load).

### Cave spider — Meta menardi
- **Eats (adult):** "smaller invertebrates, most frequently myriapods and slugs" [WP].
- **Eats (young):** after hatching the spiderlings stay in the cocoon until their first moult, living on yolk [WP]; after that, not stated.
- **Eaten by:** no predator is named.
- **Prey on the roster:** myriapods in general, which on the roster are the ★ millipedes (garden, giant African, shocking pink dragon) and ★ centipedes (stone, tiger, giant); the source names no species [WP].
- **Breeds:** at the end of summer the female hangs a white, tear-shaped egg sac (WP gives its size as 2–3 cm in radius) from the cave roof near the entrance, where air moves at 0.3–0.6 m/s; it holds 200–300 eggs on average [WP]. No guarding is described.
- **Life stages and timing:** egg → spiderlings hatch and stay in the sac until the first moult → they leave in spring, head for the cave entrance and drift away outside on silk (ballooning) → adults, which shun light, live in dark places [WP]. Lifespan not stated.
- **Active:** adults shun light and live in darkness, but emerge around dusk to hunt and are sometimes seen outside caves and mines [WP].
- **Lives:** caves, tunnels and mines; often where bats roost [WP]. Scandinavia to North Africa and Europe to Korea, with introduced populations in Japan and Madagascar [WP]. About 15 mm long, 5 cm across the legs [WP].
- **Social:** not stated.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** drops onto prey on a single silk line, a "lasso", swinging down on it [WP]; adults flee light while young spiders after a few moults are strongly drawn to it, which carries them out of the cave [WP]; the hanging white egg sacs mark where it lives [WP].
- **Danger to people:** not dangerous; unlikely to bite if handled carefully [WP].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** partly: slugs are one of its two main foods (gone with the snails); myriapods remain.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meta_menardi
- **Not confirmed:** whether it builds an orb web (not stated on the page fetched); lifespan; predators; whether it hunts the ★ cave fly (the lineup's claim; no source fetched says so). Thin sources: Wikipedia only (the British Arachnological Society and Wildlife Trusts pages did not load or had no entry).

### Daddy longlegs — Leiobunum vittatum (eastern harvestman)
- **Eats (adult):** not stated for this species. Harvestmen in general: many are omnivores eating mainly small insects, all kinds of plant material and fungi; some scavenge dead organisms, bird dung and other faeces; unlike spiders they swallow chunks of solid food [WPo]. The genus *Leiobunum*: omnivores that hunt and scavenge, with earthworms named as primary prey and blackberries as the main plant food [WPl].
- **Eats (young):** not stated.
- **Eaten by:** harvestmen in general: some mammals, amphibians, spiders and scorpions; parasitic mites and internal gregarine parasites [WPo]. *Leiobunum*: four named spiders, among them the orb-weavers *Araneus diadematus* and *Zygiella x-notata* and a *Marpissa* jumping spider [WPl]. Spiders and scorpions are on the roster as groups; no roster species is named.
- **Prey on the roster:** none named (small insects in general).
- **Breeds:** harvestmen lay eggs through an ovipositor and some build nests; in some species the male alone guards eggs from several females [WPo]. Not stated for this species.
- **Life stages and timing:** egg → nymphs → adult; "Most species live for a year" [WPo]. No species-level timing.
- **Active:** most harvestmen are nocturnal [WPo].
- **Lives:** eastern and central North America [WP]. *Leiobunum* clusters also turn up on buildings: window ledges, under gutters, on oven walls [WPl]. Body 5–8.5 mm [WP].
- **Social:** *Leiobunum* has "a pronounced tendency to form into aggregated groups" [WPl]; harvestman aggregations reach about 200 in some groups and more than 70,000 in certain Eupnoi (one of the harvestman suborders) [WPo].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** drops a leg when grabbed, and the lost leg keeps twitching for 60 seconds to an hour, distracting the hunter [WPo]; this species has paired scent glands, and harvestmen release strong-smelling defensive secretions [WP, WPo]; long-legged harvestmen bounce their bodies ("bobbing") to blur where they are, and play dead as a second defence [WPo]; no silk, so no webs [WPo].
- **Danger to people:** harmless; the "most venomous animal" story is a myth: harvestmen have no venom glands and cannot bite a person [WPo]. Not a true spider [WPo].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** the genus's named primary prey is earthworms (gone), and harvestmen also scavenge bird dung; insects, plants and fungi remain. Its mammal and amphibian predators are gone.

- **Not confirmed:** almost everything at species level (diet, eggs, timing, clustering in this species); thin sources. The Missouri Department of Conservation and Penn State Extension pages did not load.

### Wolf spider — Hogna carolinensis (Carolina wolf spider)
- **Eats (adult):** insects and other small invertebrates, typically grasshoppers and crickets, among other arthropods [WP].
- **Eats (young):** not stated.
- **Eaten by:** "a variety of lizards, amphibians, wasps, and spider-eating birds" [WP]. Wasps are not named to species.
- **Prey on the roster:** grasshoppers and crickets as groups, which on the roster are the ★ house cricket, ★ field cricket and ★ desert locust; no species is named [WP].
- **Breeds:** the male courts with his forelegs extended [WP]. The female carries her egg sac attached to her abdomen (wolf spiders: on the spinnerets) for about two weeks of incubation; about 200 spiderlings hatch per sac [WP, WPf]. The spiderlings stay with their mother for their first six days [WP]; wolf-spider mothers in general carry the young on their backs, for several weeks in some species [WPf].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg sac about 2 weeks → spiderlings with the mother about 6 days → disperse → adult. Males die the summer they mature; females can live several breeding seasons [WP]. Context: active March to October, inactive November to February in its range [WP].
- **Active:** night [WP].
- **Lives:** much of North America; in a burrow it digs itself or takes over [WP]. Females 22–35 mm, males 18–20 mm [WP].
- **Social:** solitary; contact only for mating and early rearing of young [WP].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** no web: it waits at the edge of its burrow or another ambush spot and attacks prey that comes near [WP]; wolf spiders in general also pounce or chase over short distances [WPf]; its eyes shine back in torchlight at night [WP, WPf]; a mother hauling her egg sac and then her spiderlings [WP, WPf].
- **Danger to people:** venomous, but the venom is for prey, not defence [WP]; wolf-spider bites cause swelling and mild pain, and they usually bite only when threatened or handled [WPf].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none for food or breeding (insect prey). Most of its named predators (lizards, amphibians, birds) are gone; wasps remain.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hogna_carolinensis · [WPf] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_spider
- **Not confirmed:** what the spiderlings eat; burrow depth; which wasps prey on it (spider-hunting wasps are not named; the ★ tarantula hawk is not mentioned). The Animal Diversity Web page held no account text.

### Jumping spider — Phidippus audax (bold jumping spider)
- **Eats (adult):** insects and other land arthropods "such as caterpillars, dragonflies, grasshoppers, and other spiders", including crop pests (bollworms, boll weevils, leafhoppers) [WP]. Many jumping spiders also take nectar [WPj] (not stated for this species).
- **Eats (young):** not stated separately.
- **Eaten by:** spider wasps and thread-waisted wasps (which feed bold jumpers to their young), lizards, dragonflies, birds and other spiders [WP]. Dragonflies and spiders are on the roster as groups; the ★ tarantula hawk is a spider wasp, but no wasp species is named.
- **Prey on the roster:** groups only: caterpillars (the young of the roster's ★ butterflies and moths), ★ dragonflies, grasshoppers (★ desert locust) and other ★ spiders; no species named [WP].
- **Breeds:** the male courts by raising and flicking his front legs and shaking his palps while zigzagging sideways; mating takes about 1 h 18 min on average [WP]. The female spins a disc-shaped egg sac under rocks, bark or leaves with 30–170 eggs, covers it with a cave-like silk tent and guards it without eating until the second-instar young emerge about a month later [WP].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg sac about a month → spiderlings → mature about 9 months after laying (in the north the young overwinter) → adult; lab lifespan a little over a year, females living a couple of months longer than males [WP].
- **Active:** day: it hunts by sight, so it is diurnal, and shelters at night in a sac-like silk retreat [WP].
- **Lives:** grasslands, chaparral, open woodland and farm fields across the United States, southern Canada and northern Mexico; introduced to Hawaii and elsewhere [WP]. Females 8–15 mm, males 6–13 mm [WP].
- **Social:** solitary; hunts alone by day [WP].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** the best eyesight of any spider, with near 360° coverage and colour vision [WP, WPj]; stalks slowly to within range, crouches, ties on a silk safety line and jumps; if it misses, it climbs back up the line [WP, WPj]; its legs are extended by changing the pressure of its body fluid [WPj]; iridescent green fangs [WP]; flees from animals too big to eat [WP].
- **Danger to people:** bites are rare, only when threatened or handled; mildly painful, not dangerous, with redness, itching and swelling for a day or two or no symptoms [WP].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none (arthropod prey). Its bird and lizard predators are gone.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phidippus_audax · [WPj] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jumping_spider
- **Not confirmed:** this species' jump distance (jumping spiders range from 2–3 body lengths to 38 in one species [WPj]); whether it takes nectar. The Missouri Department of Conservation pages did not load.

### Brazilian wandering spider — Phoneutria nigriventer
- **Eats (adult):** "crickets, katydids, mantids, as well as larger animals, including tree frogs, lizards and bats" [WP].
- **Eats (young):** not stated.
- **Eaten by:** no predator or parasite is named in the sources.
- **Prey on the roster:** groups only: crickets (★ house cricket, ★ field cricket) and mantids (★ Chinese mantis, ★ orchid mantis); no species named [WP].
- **Breeds:** the male dances to win the female's attention and males fight each other; the female, larger than the male, may attack him after mating [WP]. She may lay about 1,000 eggs, kept in a silk sac [WP]. Females carrying egg sacs may be slightly more venomous [WP].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg sac → spiderlings → adult; no durations or lifespan given.
- **Active:** night: it wanders the forest floor hunting and kills by ambush, with no web or fixed lair; by day it hides under logs, rocks and in crevices, termite mounds, banana plants and bromeliads [WP, WPg].
- **Lives:** the Southern Cone of South America (Brazil, Uruguay, Paraguay, Argentina): forest floor, banana trees, foliage and towns, often inside houses, in dark, damp places in or near homes [WP, WPg]. Body up to about 5 cm, legs spanning up to 15 cm [WP].
- **Social:** not stated; males fight each other during courtship [WP].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** its threat display: it lifts its body upright, raises the first two pairs of legs high to show black and pale bands underneath, and sways from side to side with the hind legs cocked [WP, WPg]; it chooses whether to inject venom, about one bite in three carrying venom and a small dose in a third of those [WP, WPg]; it hides by day in banana plants, hence "banana spider" [WPg].
- **Danger to people:** truly dangerous: its bite can raise pulse, blood pressure and breathing rate, cause very severe pain and priapism, and in several documented cases death, usually from respiratory arrest [WP, WPg]. From 1925 to 1945, 415 bites were recorded, 400 needing antivenom [WP].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** partly: tree frogs, lizards and bats are among its prey (all gone); crickets, katydids and mantids remain.

### Black widow — Latrodectus mactans (southern black widow)
- **Eats (adult):** a variety of insects, and also woodlice, millipedes (diplopods), centipedes (chilopods) and other arachnids [WP, ADW].
- **Eats (young):** not stated separately; many spiderlings die from cannibalism among themselves, lack of food or shelter [WP].
- **Eaten by:** the blue mud dauber wasp *Chalybion californicum* and the spider wasp *Tastiotenia festiva* take adults; the brown widow spider preys on it; egg parasites include the flightless wasp *Baeus latrodecti* and chloropid flies; "Brown creepers, mantises or centipedes also will occasionally and opportunistically take widows as prey" [WP, ADW]. On the roster as groups: ★ mantises and ★ centipedes (no species named).
- **Prey on the roster:** groups only: ★ millipedes, ★ centipedes and other ★ arachnids (spiders, harvestmen, scorpions); no species named [WP, ADW].
- **Breeds:** the female may eat the male after mating, though the pair can also part peacefully [WP, ADW]. She makes four to nine pear-shaped egg sacs a summer, each with about 100–400 eggs [WP, ADW]. Spiderlings leave the web after hatching [ADW].
- **Life stages and timing:** eggs incubate 20–30 days; rarely more than 100 survive, about 30 on average past the first moult → able to breed at 2–4 months (ADW: 70–90 days), fully grown at 6–9 months → females live up to three years, males about three to four months [WP, ADW].
- **Active:** night; by day it stays in the silk tunnel of its web [ADW].
- **Lives:** dark, sheltered spots: under stones, in crevices and woodpiles, around outdoor privies [ADW]. Females 8–13 mm, males 3–6 mm [WP].
- **Social:** solitary and cannibalistic; it meets others only to mate [ADW].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** a three-dimensional tangled cobweb of very strong silk [WP] (ADW: irregular and funnel-shaped, with a silk tunnel); at the slightest disturbance it drops out of the web and plays dead [ADW]; a female may eat her mate [WP, ADW].
- **Danger to people:** venomous: bites cause pain and painful rigid stomach muscles [WP, ADW] but are rarely fatal: no deaths among 23,409 documented widow (*Latrodectus*) bites from 2000 to 2008 [WP].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none: all its prey are arthropods (woodlice are arthropods, though not on the roster). One named predator (the brown creeper) is a bird.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latrodectus_mactans · [ADW] https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Latrodectus_mactans/
- **Not confirmed:** how often females eat males; whether it builds the web low to the ground (ADW says sheltered spots under stones and in crevices, not height); whether spiderlings balloon.

### Goliath tarantula — Theraphosa blondi (Goliath birdeater)
- **Eats (adult):** "primarily ... other large arthropods, worms, and amphibians"; also seen eating rodents, frogs, toads, lizards and even snakes; "only rarely actually preys on birds" [WP]. NG: mostly insects, plus frogs and rodents.
- **Eats (young):** not stated.
- **Eaten by:** NG names the coati (a mammal) as a predator its hairs drive off; no other predator or parasite is named [NG].
- **Prey on the roster:** "other large arthropods" in general; no roster species named [WP].
- **Breeds:** the female lays 100–200 eggs (NG: an egg sac of 50–150) and covers the sac with her irritating hairs; the eggs hatch in 6–8 weeks [WP, NG].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg sac 6–8 weeks → spiderlings → females mature in 3–6 years and live 15–25 years (NG: about 20); males die soon after maturing, at three to six years old [WP, NG].
- **Active:** night [WP].
- **Lives:** deep burrows in the rainforest floor, commonly in marshy or swampy areas, in northern South America (Suriname, Guyana, French Guiana, northern Brazil, eastern Colombia, southern Venezuela) [WP, NG]. Leg span up to 30 cm, body up to 13 cm, weight up to 175 g [WP].
- **Social:** not stated.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** it drags prey back to its burrow rather than eating in the open, liquefies the insides and sucks them out [WP]; it makes a hissing noise by rubbing bristles on its palps and legs (stridulation) [WP]; it rubs its abdomen with its hind legs and throws off barbed hairs that irritate eyes and skin and send attackers running [WP, NG]; fangs 2–4 cm long, enough to break human skin [WP].
- **Danger to people:** venom relatively harmless, a bite comparable to a wasp sting [WP, NG]; the urticating hairs "can be harmful to humans" [WP]. It is eaten in north-eastern South America, roasted after the hairs are singed off; the taste is described as shrimp-like [WP]. The "birdeater" name comes from an 18th-century engraving by Maria Sibylla Merian of a tarantula eating a hummingbird [WP].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** yes, partly: worms and amphibians are among its main foods; rodents, lizards, snakes and the odd bird are also eaten (all gone); large arthropods remain. Its one named predator, the coati, is gone.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goliath_birdeater · [NG] https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/invertebrates/facts/goliath-birdeater
- **Not confirmed:** whether ★ tarantula hawks (the roster's *Pepsis grossa*) prey on this species; spiderling care; the young's diet. The Animal Diversity Web page held no account text.

### House mosquito — Culex pipiens
- **Eats (adult):** males only sugar (nectar and other sugary fluids); females both sugar and blood [WP, WPm]. Blood hosts: the above-ground form *pipiens* mainly bites birds (doves and pigeons preferred) but also people; the form *molestus* (the "London Underground mosquito") bites mainly people and other mammals [WP, ECDC]. Before overwintering they live on nectar [WP].
- **Eats (young):** larvae need organic matter in the water [WP]; they breed in clear or organically polluted water and tolerate a little salt [ECDC].
- **Eaten by:** (mosquitoes in general) larvae: dragonfly and damselfly nymphs, whirligig beetles, ★ water striders, fish, amphibians, turtles and ducks; flying adults: dragonflies and damselflies, swifts, swallows and bats; newly emerged adults: predatory flies and spiders; parasites: water mites, ciliates, microsporidians and fungi [WPm]. On the roster: ★ water strider, ★ dragonflies (blue dasher, emperor, dragonhunter) and ★ spiders, as groups.
- **Prey on the roster:** none (it drinks blood and nectar).
- **Breeds:** mating starts 2–3 days after emerging; swarms form at the light changes of sunset and sunrise [WP]. Females lay egg rafts of about 200 eggs on the water surface [ECDC]. Form *pipiens* needs a blood meal before its first batch; form *molestus* lays a first batch of about 40 eggs with no blood meal [ECDC]. Breeding waters: marshes, pools, ponds with plants, rice fields, still river edges, puddles and ruts, tree holes, manure piles, barrels and sewage ditches [WP, ECDC].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg raft hatches in 1 day at 30 °C, 3 days at 20 °C, 10 days at 10 °C → larva 6–7 days at 30 °C, 21–24 days at 15 °C (WP: 7–10 days) → pupa 1–3 days → adult [ECDC, WP]. Adult mosquitoes in general live from about a week to a month [WPm]. Context: only mated females of form *pipiens* overwinter, in cellars, caves, bunkers or burrows; form *molestus* breeds all winter [ECDC].
- **Active:** night: females bite at night, indoors or out, feeding most in the early hours after sunset [WP, ECDC].
- **Lives:** native to Africa, Asia and Europe, now in temperate regions on every continent but Antarctica, in towns and suburbs; adults usually stay within 500 m of where they bred [WP, ECDC].
- **Social:** mating swarms; otherwise not stated.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** females find a host by smelling breathed-out carbon dioxide and octenol, and by sight [WPm]; floating egg rafts on still water [ECDC]; the *molestus* form can live underground, as in the London Underground [WP]; adults rarely go more than 500 m from their breeding water [ECDC].
- **Danger to people:** bites; a major carrier of West Nile and Usutu viruses, also St Louis encephalitis and other viruses, filarial worms and bird malaria [WP, ECDC].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** yes: form *pipiens* takes blood mainly from birds (gone); form *molestus* from people and other mammals (only people remain). Form *molestus* can lay its first batch without blood; form *pipiens* cannot. Manure piles are one listed breeding site.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culex_pipiens · [ECDC] https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/en/infectious-disease-topics/related-public-health-topics/disease-vectors/facts/mosquito-factsheets/culex-pipiens · [WPm] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosquito
- **Not confirmed:** species-level predators (the predator list is for mosquitoes in general); this species' adult lifespan.

### Malaria mosquito — Anopheles quadrimaculatus (common malaria mosquito)
- **Eats (adult):** both sexes feed on sugars and nectar of many plants; females "must feed on blood" to make eggs, from people, cows, horses, pigs, sheep, dogs, cats and birds [ADW]. Males live about a week on nectar and sugar (genus) [WPa].
- **Eats (young):** organic plant and animal matter suspended at the water surface [ADW]; *Anopheles* larvae feed on algae, bacteria and other microbes in the surface layer [WPa].
- **Eaten by:** adults: birds and bats; larvae and pupae: carnivorous insects, fish and water birds; stranded larvae and pupae: ants and shore bugs [ADW]. Mosquitoes in general: dragonfly nymphs, ★ water striders, ★ dragonflies, ★ spiders [WPm]; the jumping spider *Evarcha culicivora* hunts blood-fed *Anopheles* females [WPa]. On the roster only as groups (★ dragonflies, ★ water strider, ★ spiders; ants unspecified).
- **Prey on the roster:** none (blood and nectar).
- **Breeds:** mating in flight, 10–15 seconds [ADW]. Females lay 2–3 days after a blood meal: 200–265 eggs per batch, 9–12 batches in a life [ADW]. Eggs are laid singly onto still fresh water and float on side floats [WP, WPa, ADW]. Breeding waters: ponds, swamps, bayous, slow canals and streams with plants and sunlight [ADW].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg 1–3 days (up to 3 weeks when cool) → larva, lying flat just under the surface (no breathing tube), 5–15 days → pupa 2–6 days → adult; the whole cycle 14–27 days at about 23 °C [ADW, WP]. Females live about 21 days on average (up to 62), males about 7 (up to 22) [ADW]; WP says adults typically live about 2 weeks.
- **Active:** dawn and dusk, when it feeds [ADW]; *Anopheles* in general are active at dusk, dawn or night [WPa]. By day it rests in hollow trees, culverts, homes and other shade near its hosts [ADW]. Context: females overwinter in homes, barns, caves or storm drains [ADW].
- **Lives:** the eastern United States, densest in the south-east and along the Gulf of Mexico coast; also Mexico and southern Canada [WP, ADW].
- **Social:** not stated.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** rests with its tail end pointed up, unlike *Culex* [WPa]; finds hosts by smell: carbon dioxide, ammonia and other odours [ADW]; flies about 1.6 km, up to about 4 km when hosts are scarce, but most stay near the breeding water [ADW]; communicates with faint sounds [ADW].
- **Danger to people:** the main malaria carrier in the United States (*Plasmodium falciparum*, *P. vivax*, *P. malariae*); also West Nile, Cache Valley and St Louis encephalitis viruses, and heartworm [WP, ADW].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** yes: females need a blood meal to lay eggs, and of their named hosts (people, cows, horses, pigs, sheep, dogs, cats, birds) only people remain.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anopheles_quadrimaculatus · [ADW] https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Anopheles_quadrimaculatus/ · [WPa] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anopheles · [WPm] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosquito · [PLOS] https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0108894
- **Not confirmed:** caterpillar feeding by *this* species. The 2014 study the lineup relies on (George, Blanford, Thomas and Baker, *PLOS ONE*) used a different malaria mosquito, *Anopheles stephensi*, on two moth caterpillars; feeding on them made the mosquitoes live longer, the paper did not measure egg-laying, and it never mentions *A. quadrimaculatus* [PLOS].

### Water strider — Gerris lacustris (common pond skater)
- **Eats (adult):** live and dead insects, such as the mosquito *Culiseta annulata* and non-biting midges, and other water invertebrates [WP]. Water striders in general eat invertebrates, mainly spiders and insects, that fall onto the water [WPg]; mosquito larvae are among their prey [WPm].
- **Eats (young):** not stated separately (the nymphs live on the water like the adults).
- **Eaten by:** water striders in general: "largely by birds and some fish"; some by frogs; scent-gland secretions repel fish [WPg]. Their own kind: cannibalism is frequent, females especially eating first-instar nymphs [WPg]. No roster species is named.
- **Prey on the roster:** mosquitoes as a group (★ house mosquito, ★ malaria mosquito; the named mosquito *Culiseta annulata* is not on the roster) and spiders that fall on the water (★ group) [WP, WPg, WPm].
- **Breeds:** in some water striders the sexes tell each other apart by ripple frequency: 3 Hz courts, 10 Hz threatens, 25 Hz repels; a receptive female lowers her abdomen, an unwilling one raises it and sends the repel signal [WPg]. In this species males stay mounted longer when other males are near, guarding the female, and the more food there is the more eggs a female lays [WP]. Water striders glue their eggs to submerged rocks or plants [WPg].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg 12–14 days → five nymph stages, 24–30 days in all → adult [WP]. (Water striders in general: 7–10 days per stage, 60–70 days to adulthood depending on water temperature [WPg].)
- **Active:** not stated. Context: adults emerge in April and May [WP].
- **Lives:** ponds and other still water across Europe [WP]. 8–10 mm long [WP].
- **Social:** territorial: males and females each hold a territory on the water and announce it by vibrations; males defend their territory and the female in it [WPg].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** it feels prey through its front legs from the ripples of a struggling insect [WP, WPg]; it stabs prey with its beak, injects digestive saliva and sucks out the fluid [WPg]; water-repellent hairs and surface tension let it stand on water, and rowing strokes carry it at 1 m/s or faster [WPg]; it "talks" in ripples [WPg]; young must leave as soon as their wings are grown or be eaten [WPg].
- **Danger to people:** not stated.
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none (insect and spider prey). Its bird and frog predators are gone; fish remain.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerris_lacustris · [WPg] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerridae · [WPm] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosquito
- **Not confirmed:** time of day it is active; this species' egg site and egg numbers; whether it has winged and wingless forms; species-level predators.

### Red swamp crayfish — Procambarus clarkii
- **Eats (adult):** an opportunistic omnivore: plants, animals, detritus and sediment; animal food is mostly insects (especially non-biting midge larvae), other crayfish, snails and fish; fish matter most in the adults' winter diet, and males eat more fish than females [USGS]. It also eats water fleas [USGS], thins and selectively grazes water plants [USGS], and eats the eggs and young of newts, toads and fish [USGS].
- **Eats (young):** not stated separately.
- **Eaten by:** "Native birds enjoy it as a food source" [WP]; no other predator is named. People farm and eat it widely [WP, USGS].
- **Prey on the roster:** other crayfish, which includes its own kind (★ red swamp crayfish); insects in general (no roster species named) [USGS].
- **Breeds:** females carry their eggs and then their young; females with eggs or young can be found all year [USGS]. A 10 cm female can produce up to 500 eggs, a small one about 100 [USGS]. Breeding typically in autumn in its range (context) [USGS].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg (carried by the female) → young carried by the female → mature in as little as two months; a generation takes about four and a half months; over 50 g in 3–5 months; wild ones rarely live beyond two to five years [USGS].
- **Active:** dusk and night: when settled it hides in its burrow by day and comes out at dusk to forage; it also has a wandering phase with short bursts of fast movement [USGS].
- **Lives:** warm fresh water: slow rivers, lakes, ponds, canals, seasonally flooded swamps and marshes, ditches with mud or sand bottoms, reservoirs, irrigation systems and rice paddies; native to the Gulf coast from Florida to northern Mexico and up the Mississippi to Illinois, invasive worldwide [WP, USGS]. 5.5–12 cm, over 50 g [WP, USGS].
- **Social:** it mainly builds simple burrows with a single opening that hold two crayfish [USGS]; it outcompetes native crayfish and other animals [USGS].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** digs burrows 40–90 cm down to the water table in drought and survives dry spells of up to four months [USGS, WP]; it can cross miles of fairly dry ground, especially in wet seasons [WP]; its burrows collapse banks and damage rice crops [WP, USGS]; males have bigger claws than females [WP]. Farmed in Louisiana rice fields since the 18th century [WP]; Louisiana's crawfish industry is worth over $150 million a year [USGS].
- **Danger to people:** none stated. It carries the crayfish plague fungus (*Aphanomyces astaci*) and other diseases and parasites [WP].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** partly: snails and amphibian eggs and young are part of its diet (gone); insects, other crayfish, fish, water plants and detritus remain. Its one named predator group (birds) is gone.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procambarus_clarkii · [USGS] https://nas.er.usgs.gov/queries/FactSheet.aspx?SpeciesID=217
- **Not confirmed:** predators other than birds (fish are not named as predators in these sources); how long a female carries eggs; whether it eats mosquito larvae; danger to people from its claws.

### River crab — Potamon fluviatile
- **Eats (adult):** plant debris, algae scraped from surfaces, and prey: frogs, tadpoles and invertebrates such as insect larvae, snails and worms [WP]. itWP: insects and their larvae, earthworms, small fish and fry, algae and mosses.
- **Eats (young):** not stated separately.
- **Eaten by:** rats, foxes, weasels, wild pigs, otters, birds of prey and jays; "The most significant predator may be mankind" [WP, itWP]. None is on the roster.
- **Prey on the roster:** none named (insects and insect larvae in general).
- **Breeds:** mating and egg-laying in June–September in its range (context) [itWP]; about 200 eggs per clutch, carried on the female's swimmerets (pleopods) [WP, itWP]; no free larval stage: the young hatch as tiny crabs after about 40 days [WP, itWP]; the mother carries and tends the young for their first two weeks, through two moults, after which they shelter under stones or in plants [itWP].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg about 40 days, carried → juvenile crab carried two weeks → adults reach about 50 mm across the shell; lifespan 10–12 years [WP, itWP].
- **Active:** after sunset (in summer, activity is concentrated in the hours after sunset) [itWP].
- **Lives:** in or near wooded streams, rivers and lakes of southern Europe: mainland Italy, Croatia, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Albania, Greece; also ditches, slow canals and rice paddies [WP, itWP]. Adults dig burrows in the banks; small ones shelter under stones [WP, itWP]. It tolerates fairly dry air and can go tens of metres onto land from the water [itWP]. Near Threatened [WP].
- **Social:** aggressive; adult males fight ritual battles for the right to mate [WP, itWP].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** about 90% are right-handed and attack mostly with the larger right claw [WP]; adult males have one claw, usually the right, much bigger than the other [itWP]; it can leave the water and go tens of metres onto land [itWP].
- **Danger to people:** not stated. Eaten by people since classical antiquity [WP].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** partly: frogs, tadpoles, snails and worms are part of its diet (gone); insects, small fish, algae and plant debris remain. All its named predators except people are gone.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potamon_fluviatile · [itWP] https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potamon_fluviatile (Italian Wikipedia, read in translation)
- **Not confirmed:** whether a pinch can hurt people; activity outside summer. Thin sources: the two Wikipedias only.

### Chinese mantis — Tenodera sinensis
- **Eats (adult):** mainly other large insects; adult females sometimes catch small vertebrates [WP]. Observed eating hornets, spiders, grasshoppers, katydids, small reptiles, amphibians and even hummingbirds, and monarch caterpillars, discarding the guts [WP, MDC]; documented attacks include pollinators and other beneficial insects, and small birds, mammals, frogs and snakes [WP].
- **Eats (young):** not stated separately.
- **Eaten by:** other mantises, birds, and in its native range the Asian giant hornet (★ giant hornet, *Vespa mandarinia*) [WP]; jumping spiders pounce on young mantises small enough to subdue (★ jumping spider, as a group; no species named) [MDC].
- **Prey on the roster:** ★ monarch (caterpillars, named) [WP]; also as groups: hornets (★ European hornet, ★ giant hornet; species not named), ★ spiders, grasshoppers (★ desert locust), and pollinators such as the ★ bees [WP, MDC].
- **Breeds:** the female often kills and eats the male after mating, in up to 50% of matings [WP]. She makes several rounded egg cases (oothecae) about 2 cm across, each with up to 300 eggs, usually stuck to twigs of bushes and small trees; they look like toasted marshmallows [WP, MDC]. Egg cases are sold by plant nurseries across the US and Canada [WP].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg case → nymphs hatch, eat, grow and moult → adults mate, lay and die; about a year in all [WP, MDC]. Context: eggs laid in late summer hatch the next spring [WP, MDC].
- **Active:** not stated (a lie-in-wait hunter on plants).
- **Lives:** native to China, Japan, the Korean Peninsula and Russia's southern Primorsky Krai; introduced near Philadelphia in 1896 and now in North America [WP]; around houses, grassland, pasture, farmland and other open areas [MDC]. Up to just over 11 cm, the largest mantis in North America [WP].
- **Social:** not stated; females may eat males [WP].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** sits motionless until prey walks or flies within reach, then snaps out its folded forelegs and holds it while eating [MDC]; it attacks "anything within reach that they can subdue" but "do[es] not actively hunt for insect pests" [WP]; the female may eat her mate [WP].
- **Danger to people:** harmless [MDC].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none needed: insects and spiders are its main food; the vertebrates it sometimes takes (small birds including hummingbirds, mammals, frogs, lizards, snakes) are gone.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenodera_sinensis · [MDC] https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/chinese-mantis
- **Not confirmed:** the lineup's use of mantis egg cases against locusts. Wikipedia says that "While commonly sold for pest control, Chinese mantids are said to lack effectiveness in pest management", and MDC recommends the native Carolina mantis for biological control instead. Neither source names locusts as prey (grasshoppers in general are). Also not confirmed: time of day it hunts; any threat display; egg-case parasitoids.

### Orchid mantis — Hymenopus coronatus
- **Eats (adult):** mainly other insects: crickets, flies, fruit flies, beetles, and smaller stinging insects such as bees and small wasps [WP]; "In the laboratory setting, it prefers lepidopteran prey" (butterflies and moths) [WP].
- **Eats (young):** not stated separately.
- **Eaten by:** no predator is named.
- **Prey on the roster:** groups only: bees and wasps (★ honeybee and the other ★ bees and wasps), crickets (★ house cricket, ★ field cricket), flies (★ house fly), and butterflies and moths in the lab [WP, AmNat]; no species named.
- **Breeds:** not stated. Males can be less than half the size of females, likely because females' prey choice drove the females' larger size [WP].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg → first-stage nymphs that look like assassin bugs (Reduviidae), which bite hard and taste foul → later nymphs and adults that look like orchid flowers [WP]. No durations given.
- **Active:** not stated.
- **Lives:** tropical forests of South-East Asia [WP]; the Malaysian orchid mantis [AmNat].
- **Social:** not stated.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** a field study found that a mantis sitting on its own attracts wild pollinators "at a rate even higher than flowers", and that to bees and wasps its colour cannot be told from the local flowers; it uses the flower look to turn pollinators into prey [AmNat, WP]; it climbs up and down twigs to a cluster of flowers and holds on there [WP]; it can shift colour between pink and brown to match the background [WP]; the nymphs glide on their petal-shaped leg lobes, with the shallowest glides measured in any land invertebrate [CB].
- **Danger to people:** not stated.
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none (insect prey).
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hymenopus_coronatus · [AmNat] O'Hanlon, Holwell and Herberstein (2014), "Pollinator deception in the orchid mantis", *The American Naturalist*, doi 10.1086/673858, abstract read via https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1086/673858%22&format=json&resultType=core · [CB] Zhao et al. (2024), "Petal-shaped femoral lobes facilitate gliding in orchid mantises", *Current Biology*, doi 10.1016/j.cub.2023.11.003, abstract read via https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22femoral%20lobes%22%20gliding%20orchid%20mantis&format=json&resultType=core
- **Not confirmed:** eggs and egg cases; lifespan and stage durations; time of activity; predators; size in cm; that it eats butterflies in the wild (the source says it prefers them in the lab).

### House cricket — Acheta domesticus
- **Eats (adult):** an omnivore of plant and animal matter; wild ones eat flowers, leaves, fruit, grasses and other insects, including dead crickets of their own kind [WP]. Crickets in general accept a wide range of organic food in captivity, and many scavenge organic remains, decaying plants, seedlings and fungi [WPc].
- **Eats (young):** not stated separately (the young look like small, wingless adults) [WP].
- **Eaten by:** not named for this species beyond disease: cricket paralysis virus, which spread rapidly in Europe in 2002 and the United States in 2010, is highly lethal to it [WP]. Crickets in general: the tachinid fly *Ormia ochracea* tracks singing males by their song and drops larvae on them; the horsehair worm *Paragordius varius* is an internal parasite that drives its host into water [WPc]. From other sections here, crickets are prey of the ★ wolf spider, ★ Brazilian wandering spider and ★ orchid mantis (cricket species not named).
- **Prey on the roster:** none named (it scavenges dead insects).
- **Breeds:** the female, with a needle-like ovipositor, lays eggs "in whatever moist substrate is available" [WP]; crickets in general lay in soil or in plant stems [WPc]. No parental care described.
- **Life stages and timing:** egg → nymphs (small, wingless; crickets in general go through about 10 moults) → adult; the whole life cycle takes two to three months at 26–32 °C [WP, WPc]. No overwintering stage; it survives cold in buildings and in dumps warmed by fermentation [WP].
- **Active:** most crickets are nocturnal, hiding by day in cracks, under bark or stones and in leaf litter [WPc]; not stated for this species.
- **Lives:** native to south-western Asia, now worldwide, in and around buildings and dumps [WP]. 16–21 mm long [WP].
- **Social:** not stated. Crickets in general: males fight for dominance, lashing with antennae and flaring jaws [WPc].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** males sing by rasping a scraper on one forewing over a file on the other; females are silent [WPc]; three songs: calling (draws females, warns off males), courting (when a female is near) and aggressive (when another male is touched) [WPc].
- **Danger to people:** not stated.
- **Farming:** farmed in South-East Asia, Europe and North America for people to eat, and for pet food and research; mostly freeze-dried and ground into cricket flour [WP]. Crickets convert food into body mass efficiently; Thailand has about 20,000 cricket farmers producing about 7,500 tons a year [WPc].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none (plants, fruit, insects, organic scraps).
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_cricket · [WPc] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket_(insect)
- **Not confirmed:** egg numbers; nymph stage durations; adult lifespan apart from the whole cycle; its own predators by name; time of activity for this species. The ADW page held no account text and the University of Florida Featured Creatures page has moved out of reach.

### Field cricket — Gryllus campestris
- **Eats (adult):** mostly plants: leaves and roots of various plants and herbs; also small soil animals and their carcasses [deWP].
- **Eats (young):** the same as the adults [deWP].
- **Eaten by:** no predator is named for this species. Crickets in general are hosts of the song-tracking tachinid fly *Ormia ochracea* and of horsehair worms [WPc]. From other sections here: crickets are prey of the ★ wolf spider, ★ Brazilian wandering spider and ★ orchid mantis (cricket species not named).
- **Prey on the roster:** none named ("small soil animals").
- **Breeds:** each male digs a burrow with a platform at the entrance and sings there to draw females, who wander [WP]. The female climbs on; the male attaches a 2.3 mm pear-shaped sperm packet in about a minute and keeps courting for one to two hours afterwards [deWP]. Egg-laying starts 3–4 days after mating; eggs are buried singly in bare ground near or inside the burrow, several hundred in a female's life [WP, deWP].
- **Life stages and timing:** eggs hatch in 2–3 weeks → the young stay together at first and moult several times → in autumn they separate and each digs in alone, overwintering at the tenth or eleventh stage → final moult to adult in April or early May; one generation a year [WP, deWP].
- **Active:** day and early night: males sing in the daytime and the first part of the night, only when it is well above 13 °C [WP]; mainly afternoons and evenings, late morning into the night [deWP].
- **Lives:** warm, dry, sunny places with short plants: dry grassland, heath, meadows, sunny slopes, gravel pits, open pine woods; North Africa through central and southern Europe to the Caucasus [WP, deWP]. Males 19–23 mm, females 17–22 mm [WP].
- **Social:** males are territorial and "defend their burrows fiercely"; fights can be severe, even fatal [WP, deWP].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** the male sings from the platform at his burrow mouth, audible 50–200 m away [WP, deWP]; rival males feel each other with their antennae, then trade blows [deWP]; the burrow is about 2 cm wide and 10–20 cm deep (deeper ones 30–40 cm) [deWP]; flightless, and it jumps relatively rarely [WP, deWP].
- **Danger to people:** not stated.
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none (plants, small soil animals, carrion of small animals).
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gryllus_campestris · [deWP] https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feldgrille (German Wikipedia, read in translation) · [WPc] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket_(insect)
- **Not confirmed:** adult lifespan; named predators; what "small soil animals" it eats (worms are not named). Thin English sources: the Buglife and Wildlife Trusts pages failed or had no entry.

### Firefly — Photinus pyralis (common eastern firefly)
- **Eats (adult):** "most Photinus fireflies do not eat" as adults [WP]. ADW instead says adults and larvae are both carnivorous, eating other insects (including other fireflies), earthworms and snails.
- **Eats (young):** the larva lives under the soil and eats worms, slugs and snails [WP]; ADW: insects (including other fireflies), earthworms and snails, which it immobilises and liquefies with injected poison.
- **Eaten by:** females of some *Photuris* fireflies, which lack the defensive steroids, prey on *P. pyralis* males by answering their flashes [WP, ADW]. Its bad-tasting steroids deter "birds, bats, and other insects" [WP]. Not on the roster.
- **Prey on the roster:** other fireflies, which can include its own kind (★ firefly) [ADW].
- **Breeds:** the male flies and flashes; a female answers 1–2 seconds later [WP, ADW]. She lays about 500 eggs on damp soil [ADW].
- **Life stages and timing:** eggs hatch after about four weeks [ADW] → larva in the soil one to two years [WP, ADW] → pupa one to two weeks [WP] → adult lives about 5–30 days (ADW) or three weeks to two months (WP).
- **Active:** dusk and night: most noticeable around twilight [WP]; nocturnal [ADW].
- **Lives:** meadows, fields, woodland edges, near streams, wetlands, desert canyons and dense forest, preferring damp soil and long grass; the eastern half of North America [WP, ADW]. 9–19 mm [WP].
- **Social:** not stated.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** the male's flight traces a J, lighting up on the upswing (the "big dipper firefly") [WP]; he flashes every 5–7 seconds (ADW: a 0.3-second flash every 5.5 seconds) and waits for the female's delayed answer [WP, ADW]; larvae glow as a warning to predators [WP]; when attacked it "bleeds" a bad-tasting fluid containing lucibufagins from the edges of its wing cases and shield [WP].
- **Danger to people:** not stated (ADW: "no known negative economic importance for humans").
- **2126 stand-in needed:** yes: the larva's named food is earthworms, slugs and snails (all gone); ADW adds other insects, including fireflies. Damp soil with that prey is its breeding ground.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photinus_pyralis · [ADW] https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Photinus_pyralis/
- **Not confirmed:** whether adults eat (the sources disagree); whether its eggs glow; flight height; whether the larvae can live on insects alone.

### Cave glowworm — Arachnocampa luminosa (New Zealand glowworm)
- **Eats (adult):** nothing: "The adults of both sexes cannot feed and live only a short time" [WPg].
- **Eats (young):** larvae catch prey in their snares: mainly small flies, especially midges [WP]; for the genus: midges, mayflies, caddisflies, mosquitoes, moths "and even small snails or millipedes" [WPg]; larvae on banks outside caves also trap spiders and other non-flying invertebrates [WP]. When prey is scarce, larvae eat other larvae, pupae or adult glowworm flies [WP, WPg].
- **Eaten by:** cave harvestmen are the most notable predators (*Hendea myersi cavernicola*, *Megalopsalis tumida*) [WP]; a white fungus (*Tolypocladium* sp.) can kill about 40% of pupae in caves, more where caves warm up [WP]. Harvestmen are on the roster as a group (★ daddy longlegs is a different harvestman).
- **Prey on the roster:** groups only: mosquitoes (★ house mosquito, ★ malaria mosquito), moths (★ group; no species named), ★ millipedes and ★ spiders [WP, WPg]; its own kind [WP, WPg]. The ★ cave fly (scuttle fly) is not named in these sources.
- **Breeds:** adult females glow, believed to attract a mate; pupae glow too, which may help males find females before they emerge [WPg]. A female lays about 100–130 eggs, in clumps of 40–50, usually straight onto the cave wall [WP, WPg].
- **Life stages and timing:** egg about 20 days → larva about 6–12 months, depending on food (up to a full year) → pupa about two weeks → adult fly lives up to about 76 hours (female) or 96 hours (male) [WP, WPg].
- **Active:** larvae glow to lure prey; in caves their glow follows a daily cycle that differs from the on–off cycle of glowworms exposed to daylight [WP].
- **Lives:** caves and grottos, and sheltered, humid banks in native bush, on New Zealand's North and South Islands; Waitomo's caves are famous for them [WP, WPg].
- **Social:** not stated.
- **Behaviour worth playing:** the larva spins a silk nest on the ceiling and hangs up to 30 threads (genus: up to 70, each up to 30–40 cm long) beaded with sticky mucus droplets, and its glow lures prey into them [WP, WPg]; it is sensitive to light and touch, and stops glowing and pulls back into its nest if it or its threads are touched [WPg]; in some species the droplets are poisonous [WPg].
- **Danger to people:** none stated.
- **2126 stand-in needed:** minor: "small snails" are among the genus's listed prey (gone); its main food, small flying insects, remains.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arachnocampa_luminosa · [WPg] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arachnocampa
- **Not confirmed:** whether hunger makes larvae glow brighter; species-level prey list beyond midges and small flies; danger to people. Te Ara and the Department of Conservation pages did not load or had no entry.

### Locust — Schistocerca gregaria (desert locust)
- **Eats (adult):** green plants, about its own weight (2 g) a day [WP, FAO]; nearly every crop and wild plant: millet, maize, sorghum, barley, rice, pasture grasses, sugarcane, cotton, fruit trees, date palms, bananas, vegetables and weeds [WP].
- **Eats (young):** green plants (the hoppers; not stated separately).
- **Eaten by:** predatory wasps and flies, parasitoid wasps, predatory beetle larvae, birds and reptiles; insect-killing fungi [WP]. FAO: "Control by natural predators and parasites is limited since locusts can quickly migrate away from most natural enemies." People in several countries net, fry, roast or boil and eat them [FAO]. From other sections here: grasshoppers are prey of the ★ Chinese mantis, ★ wolf spider and ★ jumping spider (locusts not named).
- **Prey on the roster:** none (it eats plants).
- **Breeds:** the female probes the soil with her abdomen, digs a hole and lays an egg pod 3–4 cm long with its bottom about 10 cm down, sealed with hardening foam [WP]; about 95–158 eggs a pod for a solitary female, usually fewer than 80 for a gregarious one; at least three pods in a life, 6–11 days apart [FAO].
- **Life stages and timing:** eggs hatch in about two weeks (10–65 days) → hoppers (nymphs) moult through five or six stages over about 30–40 days → adults mature in three weeks to nine months, most often two to four months → total life about three to five months, very variable (WP: 3–6) [FAO, WP].
- **Active:** solitary adults usually fly at night; swarms fly by day [FAO].
- **Lives:** deserts and dry lands of northern and eastern Africa, Arabia and south-west Asia; in quiet years confined to a belt of about 16 million km² [WP]. Solitary females 6–9 cm, males 4.5–6 cm; gregarious adults a little smaller [WP].
- **Social:** two forms of the same insect, solitary and gregarious. The change normally follows a drought, when rain falls and plants flush green; as the insects crowd, their hind legs bump against each other, and that touch triggers the change; crowded solitary locusts can act gregariously within a few hours [WP, FAO].
- **Behaviour worth playing:** the change shows in colour: solitary hoppers are greenish or pale beige, gregarious hoppers yellow with black head and stripes; gregarious young adults are pink and turn bright yellow when mature [WP]; gregarious hoppers form bands that feed, bask and move as one [WP]; swarms hold 40–80 million adults per km² (FAO; WP: a typical swarm about 150 million), fly downwind 5–130 km or more a day (WP: up to 150 km) and up to about 2,000 m above sea level [WP, FAO]; a 1 km² swarm eats as much in a day as about 35,000 people [WP].
- **Danger to people:** no bite or sting stated; it destroys crops and pasture on a vast scale [WP].
- **2126 stand-in needed:** none for food or breeding (plants, bare soil). Some named enemies (birds, reptiles) are gone.
- **Sources:** [WP] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert_locust · [FAO] https://www.fao.org/locust-watch/resources/frequently-asked-questions-(faqs)-about-locusts/en
- **Not confirmed:** that harvesting or mantises head off a swarm (FAO says natural enemies have limited effect because locusts migrate away from them); serotonin's role in the switch (not on the pages fetched); the hoppers' diet as distinct from adults'.

## Summary

**Food or breeding that depends on something not in the 2126 world**
- Depends on it for breeding or main food:
  - **Burying beetle:** breeds only on carcasses of small birds and rodents.
  - **House mosquito:** blood mostly from birds (form *pipiens*, which needs blood before its first eggs) or from people and other mammals (form *molestus*, which can lay its first batch without blood).
  - **Malaria mosquito:** must have blood to make eggs; of its named hosts only people remain.
  - **Firefly:** the larvae eat earthworms, slugs and snails.
  - **Cave beetle:** part of its food is bat and bird guano.
  - **Goliath tarantula:** worms and amphibians are main foods, plus rodents, lizards and snakes.
- Partly: the cave spider (slugs are one of its two main foods), Brazilian wandering spider (tree frogs, lizards, bats), red swamp crayfish (snails, amphibian eggs and young), river crab (frogs, tadpoles, snails, worms), daddy longlegs (its genus's named main prey is earthworms; harvestmen also eat bird dung), glowworm (small snails, minor).
- Not affected: the other 17. Many lose their main named predators (birds, bats, lizards), which leaves the predator side of the web to bugs and fish.

**Lineup claims the sources do not support**

- **Chinese mantis:** Wikipedia says Chinese mantids "lack effectiveness in pest management", and FAO says natural enemies do little against desert locusts, which migrate away.
- **Death's-head hawkmoth:** tomato and eggplant are not named as hosts; the sources say potato and other nightshades.
- **Giant huntsman:** "runs sideways" is not stated.
- **Cave spider and glowworm:** that they hunt the cave fly is not stated.
- **Orchid mantis:** it prefers butterflies and moths only in the lab.
- **Black widow:** a web "low down" is not stated.

**Thin sources:** giant huntsman (nearly all facts are for huntsmen in general), daddy longlegs (mostly harvestman and genus facts), bombardier beetle (its life cycle "is not known"), cave beetle, cave spider, Brazilian wandering spider, river crab, burying beetle (no numbers), orchid mantis (no breeding facts) and glowworm.
